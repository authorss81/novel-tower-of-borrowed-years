# Premise

## One-sentence premise

When a flood forces the people of Morrowfen to borrow years from their own future, a meticulous settlement clerk discovers that the city's celebrated tower was built by refugees from an erased future—and that every convenient loan is quietly making the next century pay for the last one.

## Series contract

- **Title:** *The Tower of Borrowed Years*
- **Genre:** time fantasy, civic mystery, moral progression
- **Target length:** approximately 900 chapters, arranged as 18 volumes of roughly 50 chapters
- **Lead:** Marek Vale, a twenty-eight-year-old settlement clerk in the Yearspire's public audit office
- **Relationship policy:** one slow-burn relationship, between Marek and Neris Kade; no harem structure and no instant romantic resolution
- **Core premise:** the tower can pull a plausible future into a present need, but it cannot make the impossible. The borrower receives a real effect now and names a real cost later. The more people depend on that bargain, the less any one future can remain possible.

A year in this world is a unit of lived possibility, not a calendar token. A loan can buy endurance, practiced skill, a favorable sequence of events, or a legally usable piece of the future. It cannot resurrect the dead, invent a history that never happened, or erase an existing person without replacing something else. It can, however, make a city choose one consequence over another before the city knows what it is choosing.

## The central dramatic question

When every emergency has a price, who has the right to name the price—and what happens when the person paying is a future that never chose to be born?

Marek begins by believing that careful accounting can keep power from becoming cruelty. By the end, he must accept that a just account is not the same as a just world. The tower can be changed only by replacing unilateral borrowing with relationships in which the people who bear a cost can see it, refuse it, and decide together.

## Protagonist engine

**External want:** clear the debt attached to his brother Tomas and keep the family from being turned into collateral; later, prevent the Yearspire from triggering a citywide settlement during his own lifetime.

**Internal need:** give up the belief that being the fairest person in the room means he must carry every consequence alone. He must become a witness who shares authority rather than a clerk who secretly controls the outcome.

**Central flaw:** Marek turns uncertainty into procedure. He hoards information, delays decisions until he can prove they are safe, and treats relationships as contracts because he cannot tolerate being responsible for an outcome he cannot guarantee.

**Fear:** becoming the official who decides which child, district, or future life is acceptable to spend.

**False belief:** if he can find the missing line in a ledger, he can find a solution that costs no one their agency.

The series tests that belief through practical work: floodgates, seed contracts, hospital ledgers, apprenticeships, military victories, family histories, and finally the tower's own machinery. Marek's growth is measured in changed decisions and new responsibilities, not in larger effects.

## Relationship engine

Neris Kade examines the living consequences of loans: a crop that grew because a village surrendered a future harvest, a child whose year is missing from the school roll, a bridge strong enough for one flood and brittle by the next. She begins as a hostile witness and becomes Marek's equal partner, critic, and romantic choice only after repeated evidence that he will share power and tell the truth when doing so is costly.

Their relationship is deliberately secondary to the civic mystery but never decorative. The central mystery asks who owns the future; their relationship tests whether two people can choose a future together without turning either one into a guarantee for the other.

## Tone and narrative boundaries

The story is grounded, bureaucratic, and humane. The tower is never an unexplained game dungeon and the ledger is never a substitute for people. Action comes from floods, construction, courtrooms, wards, hospitals, roads, negotiations, and the physical danger of opening old machinery. System-like messages are rare, brief, and part of the Yearspire's accounting craft.

The ending is planned rather than improvised: the tower is transformed, not simply destroyed. Existing borrowers keep the lives and relationships they actually built; extraordinary borrowed advantages are allowed to end; the people erased from history receive truth and public recognition where restoration is impossible. The final victory is a change in who may authorize a future cost, not a magical erasure of all consequences.

## Opening promise

The first volume must establish, in concrete work rather than exposition:

1. Marek's ordinary routine auditing loan receipts in Morrowfen's Bell Stair.
2. A flood-season shortage that forces the city to call a loan.
3. His brother's debt and the personal cost of treating paperwork as neutral.
4. The first proof that a ledger can hide a living consequence.
5. A small, earned use of Ledger Sight and a first settlement delayed through a risky decision.
6. A relationship with Neris that begins in professional opposition.
7. A local victory that stops an immediate harm without making the underlying system safe.
8. A blank page in the oldest ledger, the protected **A-0** signature, and the question of who owes the missing year.

The reader should finish the opening arc with a concrete answer about how the tower works and a larger question about why its oldest account is blank.
