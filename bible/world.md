# World Bible: The Morrowfen Basin

## 1. Scale and premise of the setting

The known world is divided by trade and old calendars rather than by a single empire. The story's main stage is **Morrowfen**, a dense city built on a black peat sink in a salt basin. Its people live ordinary lives—cooking, farming, repairing bridges, burying the dead, training apprentices, and arguing over wages—while the Yearspire turns their urgent needs into claims against futures they have not lived yet.

The Yearspire is not a distant magical skyscraper. It is the city's bank, courthouse, emergency office, archive, and power plant. Every neighborhood depends on it, and every neighborhood has a story about a loan that saved a life or a loan that arrived too late.

## 2. Geography

### Morrowfen Basin

The basin is low, rainy, and prone to flooding. Black peat gives way to pale salt flats at the edges. The city is connected to the outside by raised roads, barges, and two major trade routes. The basin is prosperous because the Yearwell beneath it can make a narrow present effect possible; it is poor because that effect is always paid for somewhere else.

### Stillstep Island and the Yearspire

The tower stands on **Stillstep Island**, a raised stone platform in the central flood channel. The island was built by the first generations of clerks and never allowed a private dwelling. A public causeway connects it to the city, so every petitioner must cross water to reach the people who decide what futures can be spent.

The visible tower has six major public sections:

1. **The Threshold:** intake windows, bond registration, and public complaints.
2. **The Tally Halls:** clerks, ledgers, hearings, and record-copying.
3. **The Season Vaults:** sealed stores of powers and materials whose future origin can be audited.
4. **The Witness Rooms:** small rooms where a borrower, surety, and independent witness state terms aloud.
5. **The Hollow Galleries:** records of people and places that have been removed from official histories.
6. **The Deep Engine:** the hidden machinery and the First Account, sealed from public access.

The lower passages are not a dungeon. They contain pumps, lockworks, heat exchangers, archive vaults, and maintenance lifts. The architecture is practical and often patched with materials from several centuries.

### The city wards

- **Tallow Ward:** kitchens, tanneries, fuel yards, repair workshops, and most of the city's manual labor. It is the first district to experience a public shortage because the poor are asked to pledge labor and future work more often than years of life; flood emergencies also force expensive vigor and health pledges.
- **Bell Stair:** the administrative hill of clerks, copyists, witnesses, lawyers, and public appeals offices. The Chamber of Accounts works here.
- **Rainmarket:** seed, grain, river trade, weather gauges, and the warehouses that keep the basin fed. It is the center of the harvest-loan economy.
- **Crown Terrace:** the wealthy offices of the Yearspire Trust, private lenders, insurers, and senior administrators. Its gardens are irrigated with water secured through contracts that never appear in the public price lists.
- **Hallow Ground:** the locally known district called the **Unwritten Quarter**, where people whose names have been removed from civic records live, work, and hide from collection parties. Locals know where it is; the Yearspire's official map and rolls omit it. It is not a slum by accident; the legal system made it one.
- **The Underbank:** a network of kitchens, bathhouses, and repair tunnels beneath the city where borrowed powers can be purchased without a public hearing. It is the entrance to the Hollow Court's safer cells, not a single criminal organization.

### The wider regions

- **The Red Uplands:** terraced orchards, quarries, and hill towns. Craft loans are common there because a skilled worker can pledge a future apprenticeship or a season of work while the present harvest is secured.
- **Saltglass Coast:** ports, shipyards, and foreign lenders. Saltglass uses the **Tide Reckoning**, a thirteen-month calendar based on tides, and refuses to recognize Yearspire contracts unless they are translated through a harbor notary.
- **Crown March:** the administrative heartland. Chancellors, grain reserves, courts, and the regional military meet here. It controls the laws that determine whether an emergency loan can be sealed quickly.
- **Hollow North:** villages and roads that repeatedly disappear from official maps. Some places are empty; others are inhabited by people who have been erased from a single record and have never been allowed to prove that they exist.
- **The Quiet North:** hidden routes used by people claiming descent from a future that no longer exists. The term is a public insult, not an accepted ethnic name.

## 3. The Yearwell

A **Yearwell** is a natural place where unrealized choices become dense enough to affect one another. It does not contain a literal stockpile of spare lifetimes. It is more like a pressure in the world: a grain field can carry the pressure of a harvest that has not happened, a road can carry the pressure of a journey never taken, and a person can carry the pressure of a life they might have lived.

The Yearwell under Morrowfen is unusually strong. It can be used to pull one plausible consequence forward, provided someone pays with a future consequence that is at least as real. The process is called **seasoning** because the present receives a fragment of what would normally be accumulated through time.

A Yearwell cannot:

- bring a dead person back;
- create an effect that no possible future could support;
- show the one exact future that will occur;
- safely move a living person backward (the founding crossing was not a Yearwell use; see §5, *The one-way year seam*);
- erase a settled past without changing the living people who depend on that past.

The tower does not break these rules. It exploits their ambiguity.

## 4. Public history of the Yearspire

The official account begins in **Morrowfen Reckoning Year 1**, when the city founded the tower to “make tomorrow pay for today.” The first public loans were limited to flood gates, bridge repair, and emergency medical care. A civic charter promised independent witnesses, public terms, and an appeal before settlement. Those promises became the moral language of the city even as the charter was amended.

The official history says the tower was designed by nine civic engineers after a natural well opened beneath the basin. It does not mention the **Last Convocation**, the crossing, or the missing opening year. Records of the first construction are stored in the Hollow Galleries under a seal that only the First Auditor may read. The seal binds the public copy, not every surviving fragment.

The present is **Morrowfen Reckoning Year 412**. The city is not newly built; it is old enough that many families have inherited loans, names, and scars across six or seven generations. The Tower's public face is bureaucratic stability, while its private machinery increasingly serves whoever can carry the largest legitimate claim.

## 5. The hidden history

This history is author canon and should be revealed in stages, not explained in the opening volume.

During the late era called **the Last Meridian**, a civilization farther ahead in time used a much older and more destructive version of the Yearwell. Its loans consumed possible futures until entire regions could no longer sustain a child, a harvest, or a remembered promise. The people known as the **Last Convocation**—nine survivors, including an accounting officer who would become the first custodian—escaped backward through a one-way year seam with records, tools, and a partial map of their own extinction.

They arrived in Morrowfen before the tower existed. To keep their arrival from collapsing the current timeline, they converted the unspent future of the lost world into a reserve of possibilities and built the Yearspire around it. The reserve became the **First Account**, the oldest and largest entry in the tower.

The crossing was supposed to be a one-time emergency. Instead, the engine treated every possible future as fungible currency. The Last Convocation's accounting officer, **Edren Sable**, was copied into the tower's continuity and later renewed through successive bodies. He became the **First Auditor**. The emergency agreement behind the crossing is called the **Founder's Clause**: it authorized the founders to pledge their own lost future, but it never obtained consent from anyone living in the present. Over centuries Edren watched the reserve shrink and concluded that the only stable world was one in which every future except a single approved continuation was closed.

The same missing year appears at the beginning of every ledger because the crossing itself was the first loan. The year is not a secret individual waiting to be rescued. It is the cost of the founders' arrival. Restoring it exactly would require replacing the current lives that grew around the altered history. The oldest surviving copies bear the signature **A-0**; even clerks who do not know the name are taught never to erase it.

### The one-way year seam

A Yearwell cannot carry a living person backward. The founding crossing was not a Yearwell use. It was a deliberate destruction of one.

The Last Convocation drove their well into itself instead of letting its pressure accumulate, and the collapse opened a pass that stayed open long enough for nine bodies and no longer. Four rules hold, and the tower has kept all four since; the one limit the Convocation broke on the way through was the Yearwell's, not the seam's.

1. **It cannot be reopened.** The well that made the seam is spent, and it stands in a country that no longer exists as a country. What sits over the crossing's landing point is the Deep Engine, and nothing in the tower can make a second pass.
2. **It cannot be aimed.** A seam is not a door between places. It opens at the point of collapse and delivers whoever is standing there into the middle of a history that is already running. The survivors did not choose their arrival year; they arrived into a plausible one and had to make staying survivable.
3. **It cannot carry a passenger twice.** Nothing living has crossed it since. Every later “return” ritual in Morrowfen is a fraud performed with borrowed records, which is why the Hollow Court's counterfeit returns end in coercion and why the Quiet Choir's version ends in blood.
4. **Its price is already paid.** The crossing was the first loan. The founders pledged their own lost future, witnessed by no one and consented to by no one on this side of it, and the remainder became the reserve that stopped the arrival from collapsing the history around it. The missing year at the head of every ledger is that payment. It can be disclosed, contested, and voided. It cannot be recovered.

Nobody is carried backward. A hole is made where a future used to be, and the present closes around the hole. This is why the tower works at all, and why no living person can be sent home.

## 6. Factions and institutions

### The Chamber of Accounts

The Chamber is the public audit office. Clerks copy terms, check witnesses, calculate due dates, route appeals, and publish settlement notices. Its stated purpose is to keep the tower honest. In practice it has little independent power because it depends on the Yearspire Trust for guards, paper, ink, and access to the lower vaults.

The Chamber is not a heroic institution. Its clerks are tired, hierarchical, and often more concerned with a clean file than a clean conscience. It is also the only body with a public duty to record dissent.

### The Yearspire Trust

The Trust operates the tower, owns the physical vaults, and has the legal authority to declare an emergency settlement. It contains engineers, administrators, doctors, and wealthy guarantors whose interests do not always align. Some Trust officers believe the tower is the only thing preventing a worse collapse; others use emergency powers to build a permanent political class.

### The Sable Compact

The Compact is a network of private lenders, grain merchants, insurers, and harbor notaries. It buys cheap future claims, repackages them as ordinary investments, and sells access to credit in regions the Trust cannot reach efficiently. Its leaders do not need to hate anyone. They only need everyone else to need credit.

### The Saltglass Harbor Council

The Saltglass Coast's council of port notaries, shipowners, and calendar translators. It governs which foreign year-marks can enter Morrowfen and profits from keeping the Tide Reckoning opaque. Its officials are not secretly allied with the Sable Compact; they compete with it and will cooperate when inspection weakens both. The Volume 6 crisis exposes how easily a translation dispute can become a weapon.

### The Lantern Wardens

The Wardens maintain pumps, heat exchangers, locks, bridges, and emergency lamps. They are essential workers who are accustomed to being told that a district must accept a loss so another district can survive. Their strike is a major political event, and their practical expertise makes them valuable allies and dangerous witnesses.

### The Hollow Court

The Hollow Court is a loose mutual-aid network of people with year scars, erased relatives, confiscated marks, and criminal copies of return rituals. It provides shelter, counterfeit papers, and legal help. Some cells steal from the tower; others have preserved evidence of atrocities the Trust calls accounting errors. It is not automatically virtuous, but it is the first community to treat the people written off by the tower as political actors.

### The Quiet Choir

The Quiet Choir began among people who believe the current world is an unstable copy and that the lost Last Meridian should be restored. Its members call themselves **returnists** and believe that every alternate future is a source of instability. They force loans, close public appeals, and remove people whose histories complicate the approved continuation.

The Choir is sincere, organized, and willing to sacrifice strangers. Its leader, **Lyra Dain**, was a former archive clerk who found evidence of the Last Meridian and mistook the founders' desperation for a mandate.

### The Crown of Continuation

The Crown is a later coalition of Trust hardliners, military administrators, Sable Compact lenders, and Quiet Choir cells. It does not have one leader until the final volumes. It wants a single authorized future and presents that future as the only way to prevent a continent-wide year drought.

## 7. Everyday consequences

Loans enter ordinary life through small, believable decisions:

- A family pledges a year of labor to repair a roof before winter.
- A student borrows a future year's practice to pass an examination, then discovers that the person who would have taught her has no future in the records.
- A city borrows vigor to hold a floodgate for six hours. Six hours are saved; the settlement later takes a decade of one neighborhood's health.
- A hospital borrows future recovery time from its staff, leaving workers with bodies that age in uneven jumps.
- A military commander borrows a future victory, making the present battle unwinnable for anyone who refuses the same promise.
- A person can be marked, stigmatized, or legally invisible without losing the ability to feel pain or remember the people they love.

A completed loan is not automatically a tragedy. Many borrowers knowingly accept a hard price. The moral question is whether they were told the truth, whether they could refuse, and whether the institution treated their choice as a permanent obligation after the emergency passed.

## 8. Story-use guardrails

- The first volume shows only ordinary loans, Ledger Sight, one narrow and failed use of Boundary Hold, and local administrative consequences. No wider borrowed power, no faction beyond the ward, and no answer about the missing year.
- The Last Meridian and First Account are fragments, not a complete lecture.
- The tower is never the only source of power in a region. Trade, craft, public works, and relationships remain useful without magic.
- A future can be changed, but the story must show what is lost when a change is made.
- No new cosmic threat is introduced in the final volume. The final enemy is Edren Sable and the First Account system established early in the series.
- The final transformation cannot restore every erased person, undo every injury, or make the protagonist all-powerful.
