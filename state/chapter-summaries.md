# Chapter Summaries

Two to five sentences per chapter, written for the next batch writer. Compact on purpose; details live in `state/continuity.md`.

## Volume 01 — The Unpaid Ward

### Chapter 1 — The Ninth Day
Marek audits eleven receipt bundles in the Tally Halls copy room on the ninth day of the rains and clears the posting board, and the chapter establishes the machinery of a settlement clerk by work: a receipt read against sealed terms, the five parts of a loan, the difference between a ward levy and a single loan, and the false-witnessed rule. Forty-one wet Tallow Ward water petitions arrive in a sack from the Threshold and Marek enters every one of them by the hour, against a note from the intake desk saying nobody upstairs wants them. Hesper asks for the unassigned Tallow file by name; Marek hands it over at the sixth bell, and through a closed door Hesper signs the ward settlement entry as supervisor and a junior counter-clerk countersigns the totals with a pencil. Marek goes down to the ward at dusk because he does not trust a file he has not walked, and at the Gate yard he watches a tank go dry while the trunk main is still running, and stops writing because no column exists for that.

### Chapter 2 — Nothing Wrong
Marek takes the Vale file down from the up-tray and reads his brother's vigor loan end to end, hoping to find an error that will get him recused from the fourteenth-day draw. There is none: two independent witnesses, complete terms, a scope naming only the north-road span, and a settlement the family agreed to with open eyes, witnessed by Tamsin in the plain words *I am signing anyway*. Tomas's hands are already failing and he hides it by working; Lio notices the sleeve over the hand; Tamsin refuses to be questioned about the family decision. Marek does the draw schedule in his own arithmetic on his own paper and hides it in his coat against the little book of promises he made his mother, then reads the ward's four hundred signatures again and understands that the ward is where the money comes from.

### Chapter 3 — The Eleven Names
At the Threshold on Stillstep Island, Aldis Kern brings a ward roll that the ward has been given by the levy office with pages one, two, four and five only, and line seven is *Vales of Sill Row*, Marek's own household, seventh of eleven. Neris Kade arrives as the Rainmarket-appointed independent examiner, forces Marek to read the roll aloud in a public aisle, and states that line seven is not the interesting line because it is the line that will be used to say the ward consented. The Trust hearing officer offers a hearing on the twelfth, calls a levy a standing obligation, and makes the ward's complaint a scheduling problem. Marek asks to inspect the witness chain as a party, reads the entry's counter-desk countersignature, and recognises it as a junior of the third week whose name he has been reading for a day without seeing.

### Chapter 4 — Two Names
Bohdan Rezek comes to the counter-desk for an instalment that asks for sixteen days of vigor against sealed terms of fourteen; Marek grants the wet-weather rider at the counter and signs himself into the margin, and Rezek's hands seize and he goes down on the copy-room floor in front of forty people. Ledger Sight appears on Marek's first contact with a live record under stress, read-only, and the record disagrees with Rezek's body. The Sight prints a single line, costs him a headache, tunnel vision and his memory of his own midday meal, and then he finds by hand what it pointed at: the labour-pledge schedule attached to the receipt, with eleven sureties named and all eleven witness cells blank, including a ward schoolmistress who was never asked. Marek copies the schedule into his own book, tells Hesper nothing, and is trapped on a stair by four words about page three.

### Chapter 5 — Four Names, Eleven Households
Hesper fills in the settlement date on the entry and initials it, and tells Marek that a schedule is housekeeping and not a term. Marek reads the entry with Ledger Sight, the file is internally clean, and he signs the separate one-page clerk's certificate of posting — which makes the settlement due at the peak rather than provisional — after writing *Yes* on the posting desk's query that names him as a party, and then files the labour schedule into the Tallow file as a matter of routine, which puts the contradicting document inside the folder he has just certified. On the north road, Tomas's hands fail in front of his crew and he hides it using his sleeve, his knee and his chin, while Lio notices the mannerism and cannot name what it means. At the rooms, Lio asks why the mark on Marek's wrist has four notches when Tomas's file has four months, and Tamsin sends her to bed without answering.

### Chapter 6 — The Standing Reserve
Marek goes down to verify the invented standing reserve and finds three walls. Marda Senn's chalk board shows the junction meter over-reading: the account certifies Tallow Ward at four times its standing schedule and her yard counters show two and three-quarter times, so the paper shortage is larger than the pipes' shortage; Ilya Senn has been holding the Third Sluice open against a sealed Crown Terrace order of the seventh to keep the low kitchens supplied, and asks to be put in the record; and the reserve recital resolves through one footnote to the oldest reference volume in the Season Vaults, whose first line is blank, signed A-0, and whose margin carries a founding standing hold held by a clerk of record, an office retired three centuries ago. Ledger Sight on that page prints that the page agrees with itself and tells him nothing, and Marek copies the footnote and the margin into his own book instead of filing an objection. On the causeway, Hesper tells him a certification goes live at first light with Vole's office wanting his name, Marek loses a quarter hour of the night, and he understands that the lie did not manufacture the shortage: it bought the ward its own floodgate inside a thirty-day notice period.

### Chapter 7 — Two More Days
The unsigned certification leaves the ward's lawful peak requirement as a quantity that exists in no document, and Marek buys the ward a day instead: the account shows the junction's strainer and bypass are maintained on a Crown Terrace irrigation account that serves gardens which never draw from the Tallow line, and he puts a Warden's crew and four of Kern's men into a cutting to clear the screen and turn the bypass back the right way round. The trunk reads honestly, Marda Senn rules the difference column out of use, and the yard gets two days. Renata Vole offers a clean recusal with a note in Marek's record saying the file was never his, refuses to argue when he declines it, cancels the twelfth hearing, and sets the hearing for the fifteenth at the eighth bell with herself presiding. Ondra Kelb is named at Marek's own bench, twenty years old and three weeks in the post, and Marek does not warn him.

### Chapter 8 — Six Bells
Marek rehearses the standing hold on a spoiled requisition and it costs him nothing, because nothing on the test page is due, and he reads the fact correctly and misreads the consequence. Hesper stands at the posting board and tells him, in a voice that sounds like housekeeping, what the remedy is and what it will do to the man who takes it on. Marek writes the terms in the plain order the Tower's forms keep them, names the ward line at the Tallow Gate as the boundary, and holds Hesper's entry — the paper with the pencil countersignature — from the last of the light to the sixth bell of the night watch, with no authority, no witness and nobody asked. The cost arrives at once: nosebleed, a hard-edged hall, a lost sense of his left hand, and then the disappearance of the hour between the fifth bell and the sixth. When the posting desk needs the whole levy copied by hand because a held settlement has no copy, the only clerk free is Ondra Kelb, and Marek does not say the boy's name.

### Chapter 9 — The Peak
The peak arrives on the fourteenth night and the delay buys no water at all: the yards run dry while the mains run, ninety-one people come out of the low kitchens into the Sill Road school house, which is holding three hundred and ninety-one of the ward's four hundred, and a boy of thirteen goes into the Underbank for a neighbour's goods at the fourth bell and comes out with a broken leg when the water comes up through the floor, which is the order Kern insists on and which the volume will insist on under oath in Chapters 23 and 47. Ilya Senn faces a hundred people at the sluice, owns the five days since the sealed order came and not the twelve since the valve opened, offers to shut it at the sixth bell if the ward says so in public, and Marda Senn puts the true thing on the road where nobody will say it, so the ward goes into the hearing holding both a rescue and a broken order. Tamsin walks up the Sill Road with soup for Tomas that he cannot hold and will not be helped with, and says nothing to Marek about any of it. In the Tally Halls the levy must be copied longhand on the Chair step, and Marek writes page three out from memory, in the levy office's hand, because a private page cannot be a copy.

### Chapter 10 — The Last Living Name
The settlement comes due at the sixth bell, the ward's levy fund is empty, no surety is named, the entry is false-witnessed, and Marek walks the chain himself in four steps to the last living name, which is the pencil at the foot of the fair fast hand — Ledger Sight only confirms the countersignature and the fact that the countersigner is alive. He finds Ondra Kelb on the Chair step of the Tally Stair with eleven pages of the ward's levy on his knees and his right hand open and still in his lap, lucid, answering his own name, and Kelb asks him to say it in the order entry, Kelb, Marek, because two clerks in one folder will otherwise leave only one of them in the room. Marek carries him to the clinic down the lane rather than through the door, tells him the whole of what he did, and Sera Holt tells him plainly that the body pays what the file could not, that the years are already gone, that a scar of his own is coming, and that the only question left in that boy's life is whose the years were. Neris Kade keeps a frightened crowd out of the surgery corridor for an hour and does not thank him and warns him about the hearing, and says that ninety-one names came out of the low kitchens tonight onto a list in her own hand. At first light, in the copy room, the founding volume goes back into its case with the blank first line still blank, and Nim Rook's ward roll names a district — four, Kallow — that the tower's own book of districts does not contain, and Marek keeps it out of the file and in his coat. Hesper asks in the corridor whether the junior clerk is awake yet, and says nothing else.

### Chapter 11 — What the Years Cost
The fifteenth, first light to the sixth bell. Sera Holt answers Marek's question in three words: **there is no form.** Nobody can put a boy's years back into a hand, there is no trust and no reserve and no office that owes it, and the only record is a vault book of yearline marks kept by a man called Osk. Milena Kelb has been in the clinic waiting room since the small hours with four facts written down in the order she wants them said; Marek gives them in that order, the entry, her son's pencil, and his own name third, and she tells him what the Fund will pay for (the clinic, the cot, a hand-book) and what it will not (the years), and asks him to stand in a schoolroom on the nineteenth. Outside the lane the ward is already gathering in two pieces — a third of it carrying pots and parcels to thank somebody, the rest there because the clerks have been saying since the small hours that a junior clerk was careless with a column. Marek finds the mark from the hold on his own forearm: a ruled line with four notches at the same four intervals as the mark Lio asked about, in the same place. Aldis Kern arrives with the ward's decision already taken — four hundred people going up the causeway, and a captain who intends to say where the water came from.

### Chapter 12 — What a Ward Is
The fifteenth, the sixth bell to the eighth. Nadja Ostrow, one of the eleven named households, waits at the clinic door to ask whether she owes it, and makes him say it back in words so that she can hear it. In the Gate yard Aldis Kern reads out two lists, the second longer than the first: the hold bought six bells and no water, the water came off a valve a Warden has held open against a sealed order since the second of the rains, and nothing has gone against the ward's account — except that the honest meters now show the ward drawing two and three-quarter times its schedule where the paper used to say four. Marek tells four hundred people that holding up a settlement does not make water, that the ward owes nobody for the hold and owes a debt to a man it has not yet agreed to name, and that a person who is angry should be angry at the office that wrote the column and not at a boy of twenty. Neris Kade crosses the yard, reads his forearm in one sentence — *that is a yearline mark* — and does not stop. He keeps his promise to Kelb's sister at the school house until the eighth bell, and then four hundred people walk up the causeway behind a lantern.

### Chapter 13 — The Totals Are the Totals
The fifteenth, the eighth bell into the afternoon, the Trust's third floor, Vole presiding, seventy in the chamber and the rest in the corridor, and an hour and a half of it because the Deputy Steward's office has a Crown Terrace irrigation account in front of it. Vole rules that page three was never served on the ward, so no party can rely on it — eleven unsigned sureties set aside, which is good for the ward today and bad for the ward later. Kelb is at the clinic and the ward asks where he is. The file's cause is not the reserve recital: it is an operator's failure by the district's irrigation contractor on Crown Terrace account 4-Crown, a maintenance failure of the junction screen and the bypass, corrected on the thirteenth at the water office's expense — every word of it true, and load-bearing for the lie. The hearing officer then says, once, in procedure, that a counter-signature certifies only that the document was before the desk and that the clerk looked at it, and that the chain follows the instrument and not the folder, so the certificate of posting is not on the paper the collection will walk to. Marek asks only that both copies be entered as the same document with the difference noted and not adjudicated, and is handed the settlement certified as lawful: the ward's lawful requirement for the peak night is 160 hundreds of pints and it drank 440.

### Chapter 14 — A Number of the Boy's
The sixteenth, second bell to dusk. The first two-thirds is Kelb's: a laundry, a basket he can still lift, a name written in nine-year-old script, and the ward school's suspension notice with four names on the front and four on the back. Then the Fund's one room over a chandler's shop, where **Zorka Vendl**, sixty-one, nineteen years, turns people into case numbers and is the instrument rather than the villain: the form has a box for *injury sustained in the course of duty* and no box for what happened, the Fund pays the clinic and not the years, and the discharge book holds forty-one marks in nineteen years, four of them meaning a clerk who never came back. Eleven counter-desk juniors fill the room and none of them asks about his hand; they ask what a counter-signature certifies, and **Emil Skar** asks the first question anybody has asked in six years about when the duty in a night watch begins. Marek arrives at dusk, gets the case entered under the right instrument so that the entry carries three clerks' names in one sentence instead of two, and cannot answer the one question a junior actually asks him: *will it happen to us.* Kelb closes the chapter by putting the ward's own figure on the Fund's board.

### Chapter 15 — The Second Document
The sixteenth, last of the light to the fourth bell of the night watch. Marek writes page three out longhand for the third time, in the levy office's hand, and finishes it with the four words at the foot — *copied from the original* — and has it worth nothing. A suspended settlement is not a state that was ever made, so the Tower will not copy it; and the footnote on his sheet carries, verbatim, the words of the page the Trust's founding order forbids any clerk to copy, so anything carrying those words carries the bar with them. A hand-copy is a certification, and a certification is only good against a document the Tower may certify; a man who makes one of those has not made a copy, he has made a second document, and nobody in any room is permitted to produce it. He cannot file it, cannot produce it, cannot withdraw it and cannot destroy it. Two hours and ten minutes of copying costs him the use of his hand and a wet pain in the inside of the elbow, and nobody's fund pays for it, because no office is responsible for the copy of a settlement that does not exist. **Nim Rook** takes it for nothing, states her price — the moment he finds out anything at all about a name of hers — and he says **Ilen Rook** out loud in the copy room, twice.

### Chapter 16 — Nineteen Years
The seventeenth, second bell into the afternoon, the supervisor's room. Hesper says without evasion that somebody has been copying pages out of a held case, believes the questioner is **Neris Kade**, and is wrong; Marek does not correct him, and notices with a kind of nausea how easily the lie comes out. He declines to put his hand on the Tallow file and reads the column by hand instead, and finds it entirely correct. Hesper makes his offer: **the Vale settlement schedule** — the supervised road work, named, dated for the twenty-fourth, independently witnessed — and explicitly not the years, which are gone, and not the draw, which posted on the fourteenth. He says, apparently without meaning to, that the ward's water was never the problem and the ward's bill is, and that he found the gate and four dead people in nine minutes on the ninth. Marek neither accepts nor refuses; he asks for it in writing, one line, a date and a form number, because a verbal offer by a supervisor in a room with nobody else in it is worth nothing, and Hesper writes it. Then he stands on the Chair step and works out two things. The first is that he is frightened of how much he wanted a form with a date on it. The second is the hinge of the batch: **not one of the two men ever said the boy's name.** Hesper had gone past the counter-desk junior twice in the same conversation and Marek had said *I carried him down a lane* and *I know you know about the hand* and had meant both, and two careful people had done the whole of the damage to a third man who was not in the room. It did not take a villain; it took a supervisor with a schedule in a drawer and a clerk with a reason to want it, and about eleven minutes, and nobody in it being unkind.

### Chapter 17 — The Vigor Bill
The eighteenth, first light to dusk, the north road above Tallow. Marek comes up to stop his brother and instead watches Tomas refuse the settlement schedule in front of the crew, in the voice of a specification: a named schedule with a third-party witness is a sentence a room can read aloud, and Tomas has been a line in a book his whole life — a grandmother dead eight years is still line seven of the roll and nobody has struck her out. Then **Bohum Ostrek**, thirty-three, a north-road hand who signed the same vigor clause at the counter-desk on the sixteenth, two days earlier, locks both hands in the middle of a lift, and nobody has done anything wrong; the pay chest takes the first four days of a draw up front, so he has had the strength since Wednesday and the bill since this morning, and his own figure for it is two weeks of the hand off the top. Ostrek's answer is that the form reads like a hire contract and he has never in his life not found terms reasonable. Marek takes the name, the date and the terms and files them in the **public petition book** in his own hand under the north road's name, the first honest public act he has committed. At the rooms Lio notices the new mark and asks one question more than she should; Tamsin says nothing about the mark, refuses to be drawn, and warns him that a public good obtained in a room he is not fully honest in is a debt.

### Chapter 18 — Four Names and a School Year
The nineteenth, second bell to dusk, the ward school on Sill Row and the Threshold's public complaint board. **Pella Rusk** has been counting what a levy does to a school year for nine years, which is why the Tower's clerks know her name and distrust her. The mechanism is arithmetic and nobody in the room has to be wicked: the school is paid from the district account, the levy draws days at the ordinary wage out of the same account, the account is overdrawn by 240 per cent, and the office balances it by cutting lines in a written order in which a school year is the cheapest thing on the page. Four children are off the roll — **Wren Ostrow, Tibb Vray, Salle Prent, Iven Marsh** — and no child does anything at all, which is worse, and Rusk says so. Marek's only instrument is the Chapter 13 record: a standing difference in a Trust proceeding holds a closure for a week. Rusk nails two sheets to the complaint board in four true sentences with no accusation in them. At the end she asks him directly whether page three has her name on it, and he gives her a technically accurate answer about what page three is, which is not a yes and not a no, and the chapter ends on him.

### Chapter 19 — Reading a Hand
The twentieth, second bell to the light, four yards, the low kitchens, the school house and a trestle table at the Gate yard. They do it badly for three hours: Marek writes names into columns, Neris reads hands, and neither can use what the other produces. The turn is not a technique — it is that **a scar has to be matched to an instrument and an instrument to a clerk**; a scar with no instrument behind it is somebody's private business, and a scar with a schedule behind it is a line in a case. The ward's scars come out in three piles: the Silt's years, the lawful vigor clauses with their form numbers, and the hold's years, which belong to nobody's book and everybody's body. They publish no number and no name; they write a method on the back of a complaint sheet — examine the body before the instrument, a scar is dated not counted, a person may stop, an independent witness, never two sheets with one keeper — and leave it in the ward. **What Marek loses is the belief that the record is the territory**: sixty-one names and four columns produce a document about a ward, not an account of anybody, and he understands in the wet that a record is only the one place the territory is allowed to stand still long enough to be looked at. They argue about what the third pile is called and neither of them wins, because she uses his own found rule against him and he refuses to lie; forty-one people give forty-one answers, the heading at the top of the column is left blank, and the Silt's pile is deliberately left unnumbered. Marek enters his own scar in the third pile, in his own hand, dated the fourteenth, and nobody remarks on it. Neris ends the day by telling him that Hesper Rann wants a recount and a third-grade clerk in the room.

### Chapter 20 — A Recount of the Totals
The twenty-first, second bell to the last of the light, the supervisor's room, the copy room and the stair. A recount is lawful: a supervisor's re-addition of a certified total with a third-grade clerk present, entered in the desk's book with the hour, the clerk's name and the reason. The cause line goes on the form *before* the arithmetic is checked, and it says: **the totals of this entry were added at the counter-desk from a defective schedule** — which is true, and which puts Ondra Kelb's instrument at the cause of the ward's shortage. Hesper is kind in the middle of it, asks after the arm, tells him the ward has its water, means both, and does not know that the page exists; he believes the questioner is Neris Kade, and he has already decided the ward is going to be paid and is now deciding who. Emil Skar adds up the column with a pencil and it agrees with itself. Marek does not sign, does not object, and does not leave. He asks one question out loud, in front of the witness — what were the totals added from — and Hesper answers truthfully, because a man who has told the truth about a document has never been punished for it. Then Marek copies the cause line into his own book, in the levy office's hand, against a standing order he is already breaking, knowing exactly what Chapter 15 taught him, and his hand fails again. Hesper offers the schedule a second time, in writing, and separately tells him to make sure the Fund asks for the right hand-book maker. At the end of the day the ward's arithmetic is in four hands — 640, 440, 279 hundreds and 40 pints of excess, and 441 — and none of them agree, and the 160 that only the Trust may use is not a hand's at all; and Marek writes his own scar into the back of his book in pencil, with the date and no name, and leaves the book open on the desk.

## Volume 01, Movement III — What the Valve Held (Chapters 21–30)

### Chapter 21 — A Ward Is Not a Person
The twenty-second, first bell to the light. The Trust's investigation opens on a sealed notice whose four terms cover the account, the maintenance record, the yard counters and such other books as the Deputy Steward directs — arithmetic and nothing about conduct, and the hold notice on the board is not in it. Renata Vole offers Marek the settlement desk's fourth clause: a twenty-nine-word statement that the Tallow file was never assigned to him, in exchange for striking his certificate of posting, and the offer is worth exactly four households. Marek does the arithmetic, asks for the terms in writing, and does not sign; by the fourth bell of the twenty-fourth he has put both papers on the *answered* side of Vole's tray. At the Threshold, three hundred and eleven signatures are read twice and then refused on standing orders: a ward cannot petition, appeal, swear or be cross-examined, and only two people can speak — the clerk of the day, who is a party, or Kern in his own name, alone. Neris Kade has entered ninety-one examined bodies in a Trust file as *a document received and not acted upon* and tells Marek to decide on Thursday because he can live in the city with the decision.

### Chapter 22 — The Difference Column
The twenty-third. Marda Senn's two chalk columns at the second junction are ruled through — *out of use* on the seventh, *reset* on the thirteenth — and she is gone. Marek traces her to the removed records, is refused the room and stands on the landing for two hours and eleven minutes while a Gallery clerk tells him that a schedule is not a record and that this is a filing system, not a mystery. She comes out with what she cannot put in a room: an unmetered take-off above the ward meter, eleven years old, carrying about two hundred hundreds of pints a night up the hill to the Crown Terrace north gardens, and a step of that size in the left column on the second of the rains that she can date to a watch and cannot explain because she was carrying pails. That evening **Warden Dettmer strikes her name off the warden's roll** at the change of watch for a shift not ended — one column, one hour, no cause and no form for a cause — and says the account will still read the left column, because the left column is the meter. On the Third Sluice platform, Ilya Senn, who has been holding it open for twenty-one days, tells Marek the intake has been drawing short since the second and cannot say by how much because he is not a clerk.

### Chapter 23 — Named Before the Sluice
The twenty-fourth, second bell. Kern reads a forty-item oiled sheet of the fourteenth of the rains to two hundred and eleven people in the Gate yard, and the Underbank comes first: a condemned storehouse that came down with Milan Vray's stock in it, and then Vasil Ferun, thirteen, with a shin broken in two places in a cellar. The boy says he does not want it named; Kern names it anyway, and also names the slate on the condemned building that no water office holds a record of, and takes the boy's own fault onto the sheet because an account that leaves it out is a handbill. Item three is the sluice, the only item anybody had any affection for. When Marek offers to certify the sheet and get it into the investigation, Kern refuses — lawfully, correctly, and in front of four hundred people — because a certified incomplete account sets the settlement aside, and the ward would pay the whole bill instead of the excess. The sheet stays on a board in forty hands. The yard's thanks, when they come, will have to be carried by one person, because a ward cannot walk anywhere and cannot thank anybody.

### Chapter 24 — Two Books
The twenty-fifth, second bell to the fifth. The chapter opens on the decision itself: at the fourth bell he takes the statement out of his coat, looks at the twenty-ninth word, and puts it back — **not for conscience, which would have cost the four households, but because the sentence is twenty-nine words long and every one of them is true, and a man who signs a true sentence because a superior has told him it costs nobody anything is told by the next man who reads it that he did.** He carries both papers down himself and writes *not signed* on the corner of the top one, on the *answered* side of Vole's tray, which takes four seconds and requires no form. Then Marek takes Vole's fourth-term production order to the Crown Terrace water office and asks for two books under two different offices. Sander Vey gives him the distinction the volume is built on: **a reading is not a document, it is a column, and a maintenance is a thing somebody did** — so a reading failure and a maintenance failure are different failures in different books and one does not cause the other. The reading book is a wall book with no copy, certified weekly: 640 on the ninth, about 410 the month before, and two hundred that has been climbing into a walled garden since the second of the rains. In the junction chamber, Neris Kade finds a blank plate where a meter should be, four screw holes painted over, and a pipe the size of the Crown Terrace garden main. Vole corrects the account in the Threshold vestibule, in public, to 440 against a lawful 160 — an excess of 280, where the ward's own roll says 279 hundreds and 40 pints, **sixty pints in nine days** — and adds a fifth term, and the take-off's account is 4-Crown, with nine gardeners on it, wages against it, and the district account 240 per cent over. No accusation is made. The ward's bill gets smaller and somebody else's gets larger.

### Chapter 25 — Four Dead People in Nine Minutes
The twenty-sixth, a Saturday. Marek puts the reading book on Hesper's desk with the wedge in the second of the rains, and Hesper makes him push back his sleeve first: the ruled line with four notches is not the cut, and a man who hides a mark has decided what it is. Hesper answers the arithmetic — the cause line is not true, it is *available* — and then names the four people he found in nine minutes on the ninth of the rains, a carter's boy of fifteen, a woman of eighty-one who had been in her room two days, a ferryman of thirty-eight, and a tanner's hand of nineteen, all dead before he wrote a word, and does not apologise. He will not ask Marek to sign the statement either, and says why: a word of objection from him goes to the intake floor in a week and costs Marek his post. He has known about the plate since the ninth. Marek goes and reads it. The gate is real, the notice is real, the date on the keystone is the twelfth — and Hesper was right about all of it, and did not care in the slightest.

### Chapter 26 — Two Stories of One Night
The twenty-seventh. The river comes up with no rain at all. Kern holds the yard meeting, reads the first three items again with the Underbank still on top, and then puts two sentences on a sheet: **the ward's water came down a channel off the Crown Terrace intake by a valve a Warden held open against a sealed order, and the ward was not asked** — and **the ward's water was not lawfully delivered to the ward, and the ward will pay for every hundred pints of it at the ordinary wage.** Nadja Ostrow wants the first sentence; Milan Vray wants the second. Kern explains that a beneficiary of somebody else's breach pays the whole of what the beneficiary took, and that naming the man is the surest way to get him named. He takes the second sentence, at two hundred, and says he does not enjoy it. The high water comes in about the fifth of the night, nobody is hurt, and Ilya Senn spends two hours holding a board in the mouth of the Underbank passage with the water above his knee. He tells Marek what he will be asked tomorrow and says he would rather be the one in it than the twenty-year-old at the counter-desk who took the whole of it off a column he never saw.

### Chapter 27 — The Maintenance Account
The twenty-eighth, a Monday, the eighth bell. Ilya Senn's hearing is a maintenance account, not a character: a Warden may be a party to one and may not be a party to a settlement, because a sluice is not a loan. He puts the order of the seventh and four letters on the table and names two breaches, the worse of which began five days before the other, because a man who holds a valve open against an order is disobeying and a man who holds it with no order at all is deciding. The officer's opening paragraph is rescue and damage and a broken order in one, because the account is a machine for moving water and does not know what it is carrying. Vole offers terms: the make-good becomes the district's charge, the irregularity goes on the plant's record, no proceeding about the fourteenth, his sister unnamed. Ilya puts a price and an offer on the table, refuses the terms for the day, and says the third part of the sentence is false — signing it would make the tidiest instrument in Morrowfen the end of the list. **Neris Kade puts four hundred and forty-one into the record beside the corrected four hundred and forty**, and the room holds two figures for the same night.

### Chapter 28 — The Sentence He Would Not Say
The twenty-ninth, eighth bell, and the yard at the last of the light. Vole reads the terms herself, as Ilya asked, and tells him what refusing costs: an open account is a year-end reading and a year-end reading can be made against a person, and she has four of those this year. Ilya refuses in the room and in front of the party, and refuses for the reason that matters — he will not be the villain of somebody else's account, because Kern has spent two days getting one sentence ready and a ward cannot say it for itself. He leaves his wages on the table and asks that it be recorded as an offer made and not accepted. Marek cannot read Kern's own name aloud in the room. At the yard, the ward's roll is four lines: the ward will not name the man, the ward will pay, the offer stands whether the account closes or not. The ward then argues for two hours about whether it is allowed to be grateful, and settles on paying and not thanking, and keeps the roll in the yard and refuses to let Kade or Marek hold it. Ilya closed the valve at the third bell and does not want to be thanked, and says why: in six weeks somebody will notice a rising bill, a rising river and a closed valve, and work out that the sensible thing was to shut it, and the ward's own roll will be the paper that says it.

### Chapter 29 — The Cheapest Line
The thirtieth. Marek uses Vole's fifth term to get the composition of the district account, and district accountant **Grale** — the same Grale who took Marda Senn's counts down on a tray for eleven years and went into the removed records on the twenty-third — reads the wall off the end of his own life: a **reserve carry of forty thousand nine hundred, every year, since the flood years, entered, unapplied, held against a standing reserve that has never been found.** Any assessment reciting that reserve offsets in full against the line, and no form anywhere asks whether the fund exists. Income is twelve thousand and something and the account presents forty thousand eight hundred, which is 240 per cent over; the true position is eighty-one thousand seven hundred and fourteen, which is 580 per cent over, and the carry has been making the account look half as bad as it is. Grale turns the cut order to the wall: it gets cheaper as it goes down, the north gardens' crew is ninth, the school is last, and the ward's levy days are not yet drawn. Pella Rusk asks for the page her school comes off and is told it is a nail in a wall, and says the number is not hers to say. The carry is entered into the finding, no name is spoken, and the frame comes off the cut order.

### Chapter 30 — The Ordinary Course
The thirtieth, fifth bell, at the Gate tank, in the open. The finding is served on the ward because a ward is served where it is: the settlement lawful, the reserve honoured as certified **against the water office's own account and not against the households**, the delivery corrected to 440 and the excess to 280 against the roll's 279 hundreds and 40 pints, the delay of the fourteenth not within the terms with no account of it arising — and the collection to proceed in the ordinary course, **and it may not be entered before the cause is entered in the box at the head of the form.** Bohdan Rezek, whose hands seized on the ninth, asks for her name to be read before his and then for his yard, which has no name. Marek understands in nine seconds that there is no forum anywhere in the city for six bells held without authority against an instrument that does not exist, and has known it since the sixteenth. Levy clerk **Ostrowsky** tells him the rider is new, was written by the Deputy herself at the fourth bell, and that the sitting is on the twelfth of next month and Marek is the clerk of the day for it — and then says the thing that frightens him, which is that on thirty-one recounts in nineteen years he always knew before the sitting **whose paper** he was filling the cause box against, and on this one he does not. He looks at the four things in his own book and puts the form back in the pad. Hesper is kind to him on the stair, tells him to bring his book shut, and says nothing at all about the twelfth. Nothing is filed. The box is still empty and there are twelve days.

## Volume 01, Movement IV — A Board of Strangers (Chapters 31–40)

### Chapter 31 — A Room Is Not a Decision
The first of next month, a Thursday. The recount notice goes up with the parties named and a last line reading *served at the Gate tank*, because a ward is served where it is. At the Gate yard **Bel Harrow**, a Crown March representative nobody in Morrowfen has met, arrives in a good coat and tells four hundred people the exact size of what he brought: he can make a room sit and nothing else, and *we are not clever, we are the calendar*. He needs a petition, the ward has one refused three times, and the discovery of the afternoon is that a petition asks for a decision and the ward does not want a decision — it wants a room — so what they write instead is **a six-line terms sheet** that is not a petition and that he must take to Crown March with the word *nothing* in a column meant for deciding things. Renata Vole refuses the whole thing in procedure with a schedule of reasons, all four of them correct, and the fourth is that the Crown Terrace account is a district irrigation account and the word Crown is a district and a terrace. She schedules the sitting anyway and says so out loud. The notice goes up on the second: first sitting, the ninth, at the eighth bell. And Harrow tells the yard the true price of a room — the bill comes due on the twelfth, the collection is unblocked by a cause written in a levy office he cannot enter, and a room is four days *after* the bill.

### Chapter 32 — The Six Bells Are Not In It
The second, a Friday. Tamsin hears it first, at the fourth bell, in a kitchen, from the woman who does the Fund's accounts — and is told the kind version, which is that a suspended clerk always has a paper he wrote on and never the thing that actually happened to anybody. Lio, nine, decides not to say his name in the yard. At the eighth bell the posting desk's woman reads the slip out twice: **suspended from countersignature, from the certification of copies, and from the assignment of files, until a hearing on the seventeenth; grounds, the certificate of posting of the eleventh, on which the clerk of the day answered the party query in his own hand; and at the foot, *No other matter is suspended.*** Eight words, and they are right, and the six bells are not in them because there is no matter to suspend. The cost is the inside of the building: Sedge will not countersign anything with him on it, Ostrowsky strikes *V.* off the assignment column and tells him he is not clerk of the day for the twelfth without saying whose he is, Vendl refuses to put a suspension rule into a mutual made by juniors, and Skar writes his name off the sheet. **Neris Kade stops asking him questions in a corridor** — not a sulk, a judgement, and she is right. Marek declines a Sight on a stair for the first time for a new reason: a panel in front of somebody is not a headache, it is a reason to be asked questions in a room. Hesper is kind and warns him about the true statement somebody will offer him at a cost that is not money. At home, Tamsin says the thing she has not said for eleven years: he has been asking about a mark on his arm, and I have been refusing the question, not the answer.

### Chapter 33 — A Schedule of What Comes Back
The third, a Saturday, in the back room of the Fund's office over the chandler's shop. **Ondra Kelb has made a table and three rules**: a thing is not done again until it has been done clean three days running; a stopping is written down as a stopping; and a day that cost nothing and was worse than the day before is a day. Vendl's hand-book, asked for by name off Ropewalk, works perfectly — on the wrong hand. The finding is that **the apparatus is working and the patient is not.** The controlled experiment is a column of twenty lines from the issue ledger in his ordinary hand, once, with the cost recorded; it takes four seconds and the figures come out the shape of a child of nine, and it costs him four days of the right hand, his mother's basket, and a rule of his own broken to allow for a stranger's good figures. He is his own witness except that he cannot be: the only person in the city not paid out of the district account and not living in the yard is a **suspended clerk**, and Marek signs the sheets and is told he will not be a witness again. The chapter's question is the one Marek cannot answer — *what do I call the column*, the column headed `what I am for now` — and the reason he cannot is that his own book has had a blank heading at the top of a column since the twentieth of the rains because forty-one people gave him forty-one answers. Kelb tells him he will not want the name if it arrives too easily, and does not ask him for it. **Marek does not go and ask anybody.**

### Chapter 34 — The Number Is Not an Accident
The fourth, a Sunday. The ward re-listens the finding at the Gate and nobody asks Marek to come. Tamsin has decided to do it her side: she puts a **house book** on the table with five domestic columns, one of them headed `carried`, and in the fourth column a ruled line and **four notches** against a name in the second column, and the whole line struck through in a third ink, by a hand that is not the one that wrote it, with no account of the striking anywhere in the book. She gives him the entry and refuses him the name, and she gives the refusal a reason that is not protection: *you have been asking me about a mark on your arm since you were eight. You have never once asked me a question. I was not hiding anything. I was refusing the question.* She will not say whose it is, because a name would be what the mark is for the rest of his life, and because she can see that the line was struck out by somebody with no authority and no reason, and she says out loud that he is not going to be allowed to say so. He does the arithmetic on his own stair and gets the shape of the answer and not the answer. He looks for a place in his book for it and does not put it there, and makes himself be clear that this is a decision and not an oversight. Lio, at the table, asks for one fact as the price of six days of not talking about him in a schoolroom, and he says he has not got one yet.

### Chapter 35 — One House at a Time
The fifth to the eighth. A ward that cannot petition, appeal, be sworn or be cross-examined discovers it can knock on doors. The rules are taken off the back of a complaint sheet from the trestle table at the Gate on the twentieth of the rains and the yard is not told where they came from. **Nineteen pairs go out, each with a keeper's sheet and a reader's sheet, and on the fourth day the pairs read their own answers out in the open at the tank while Kern writes them onto the two long rule sheets and a woman from the low kitchens calls each line back; the nineteen working sheets go onto a nail where anybody can lift them off.** The one box nobody can fill is the independent witness, and it is empty for a reason and not for a shortage: any one of them would do, which is why none of them can, there is no form on which a yard asks the city for a person, and no office will send a witness to a tank because a yard asked. **Marek is not asked and does not offer; Kade refuses a sheet and stands where the asking is.** **Sera Holt comes once — she is the answer to the empty box, and she was already there rather than summoned — to say that the rule being borrowed is a rule about a body and a levy is not a pail, and to name the one thing a yard has learned that a hall has not: **a count is a number and a number can be carried into a room by somebody who was not in the yard.** Forty-one doors, twenty-nine answers, and three no's: a Tanner's household asking for the work in its own yard, a Ropewalk household that carried pails and will not pay for water in any form again, and — the finding of the week — **Nadja Ostrow**, who will not take days at the ordinary wage out of the district account because that account is the one her daughter's school seat comes off, and who asks the yard which of them came up her lane without knowing that. The refusal is entered and stands. Nineteen more people give a fifth answer to the column with nothing at the head of it and the heading is still left blank.

### Chapter 36 — Who Keeps the Minutes
The ninth, a Friday, four hours and eleven minutes in a room built for thirty, and **a table of five: the Chair plus four members.** **Harrow has no clerk** — no office in the city will serve the record of a body it is a party to — so he serves the minutes in his own hand, and has put his own name on the list of members not eligible to be chair. The chair is taken thin: he presides until the board is satisfied it can sit without him, reviewed at each sitting and written. The recorder's pay is the real fight: the Fund **cannot** pay a sitting, and proves it out of nineteen years of the discharge book; the Chamber cannot, because a rate on the hall is not a fund; a ward cannot be assessed a rate at all, so the Trust's share rises to cover it, and Kern objects and is outvoted **three to one** (the Chair does not vote in a money matter; **Vendl is the person who declined twice and says so in the first person**). The Trust's motion that the ward's attendance is not required fails against **a term Neris Kade puts from a bench she was not entitled to speak from** — a party may decline to answer, the declining is written down as a declining, no inference is drawn — which Harrow calls standard March practice and neither Kade nor Marek will ever say where it came from. The last term is the volume's institutional change: **a member who dissents may have the grounds written in their own words, in the finding, and the duty to write them is the board's and not the member's.** Renata Vole refuses it on the record twice, in the same words, and the refusal goes in the minutes. At the end Harrow tells the room that two of the four terms are probably wrong and that a procedure invented in a room is a habit with a date on it.

### Chapter 37 — A Thing With No Copy
The tenth, a Saturday, with the school shut. Rusk applies, in form, for a copy of the cut order, and is refused in public at the Threshold — and the reason is the finding of the day: **a copy of a cut order would be a second cut order, and the water office is permitted one.** So the instruction that governs the ward cannot be given to the ward, and Vray realises the ward could all along have asked whether anybody else had one. Kade's one sentence sends them to the thing that *is* a record: the district account, of which the ward's ratepayers had never once thought to ask for a copy. The line is a **sum**, and Rusk and the yard work out in the open that the office values a school as a fixed thing, so what a cut removes is not a school but **the number of children in it**: fourteen places against ninety-one. Rezek refuses to write it and instead says the thing that carries the chapter — nobody cut his hand, **lines are cut**, by a man with a frame in a room on the last working day of a month, with nobody present. Rusk says the number out loud once, at the fourth bell, in a yard, and refuses to say it again anywhere but a room. Grale gives the yard his suspicion — the order gets cheaper as it goes down, somebody worked it out sixty years ago, and the day he proves it he has to stop — and will not say it in a room. Kade tells Marek she has stopped asking him things, and says so explicitly, so that her not-asking is a record and not a kindness.

### Chapter 38 — Lio's Name
The eleventh, a Sunday, in Hesper Rann's room, with a Sunday desk lamp going on outside and neither man mentioning it. Hesper sends for Marek, which he has never done. **The W-4 is in the drawer, nineteen days, first dry day the twenty-fourth, neither produced nor destroyed.** He says four things and refuses to give his reasons. He takes the W-4 off the table and says Tomas arrived at refusing it himself, over a fortnight, from a clerk in a corridor, and that Marek has never once asked his brother whether he knows the paper exists. He tells him about **his own sister** (announced as *a woman on a floor I have watched for nineteen years*), forty-four, twenty-two years on a copying floor on the fifth floor of the fund building, who found a mark on the underside of a board under a wounded man's wrist eleven weeks ago and washed it off and has not mentioned it to her brother once — and who comes home at a quarter to six every day, and what it costs a person to not ask a question at home. Then the third thing: he has read the **school register** at the Tallow Gate, and *Vale, Lio* has been off since the twenty-fourth, back on the first for the week the Ch 13 record bought, off again on the sixth — and the school is the last line on an order with no copy, and the ward's levy days have not been drawn, and when they are drawn that is a line above the school. So the last line is his daughter's seat, and it is the cheapest single thing in the ward. The fourth thing is the offer, in the same words as the seventeenth of the rains: take the schedule and he will enter the Vale household's assessment at the close of the quarter on a figure he chooses, four times in nineteen years, off the record, and Grale would know and will not be asked. He never mentions tomorrow. He tells Marek to send for him and says he will not be unkind again this month unless he opens a file. **He is near the recount the whole time and never says what it is.**

### Chapter 39 — Whose Pencil
The twelfth, a Monday, the eighth bell, in a room that holds thirty with three hundred and forty people in the corridor and on the stair, and **fourteen let in, of whom seven are in the room in their own names**, because a ward cannot be a party to a supervisor's step and cannot be sworn or cross-examined — and every one of the seven is a person with a name in a box, which is the volume's rule working for the ward for the first time. Marek is let in at the door because a suspended clerk is nobody's clerk, and sits in the last row of the gallery, and understands every word. Hesper announces the recount and says the cause goes on the form before the arithmetic. **The form is named in the chapter: a one-page certification of cause**, the only one-page instrument in the city that puts a clerk's name at the top of a sentence about a man's own work, and **Hesper reads the cause line into the record, and not one of the seven understands a word of it.** The officer states the rule once more — the collection follows the instrument, not the folder, and a false-witnessed instrument's collection walks to the living clerk whose counter-signature is the institution's act — and the third-grade clerk reads the foot of the entry, and the room says the name before the boy does. Rezek gets there first, from the front bench, and insists that nobody has said *careless* and that he could not tell one counter-desk junior from another in twenty-two years. **Kelb says one true thing and sits down: two hundred and seventy-nine hundreds and forty pints, the ward's own figure, which he added up twice with a pencil on the ninth, correct throughout, and sixty pints he cannot account for.** Then his mother stands up in a laundress's coat and says his name twice and says nobody has looked at the boy. Hesper is kind to him twice and means it. The suspension of the ninth is not disturbed, the collection is unblocked, the four households are read out again, and Hesper asks for exactly what he wanted on the ninth of the rains: that a ward's account be taken from four named households and set against a schedule nobody was ever given. **Marek had the sentence on the twenty-first and twenty-two days and could not put it in a room, and this morning he sat on an end of a bench and watched it land.** The nine words are not printed again; the exposure is the chapter.

### Chapter 40 — The Yard Is Not an Address
The thirteenth and fourteenth. Four attempts at informality, each failing in a small procedural way: the Gate yard is not an address and a sitting needs a seal on a door; the ward cannot be served, so a notice of a hearing is not a service but a thing a man in a good coat does at a tank; the board cannot strike an entry out of a desk's book, and Vole offers the honest version of that, which is that it can **hear** the cause on Thursday; and the board cannot choose between the four hands, so **Neris Kade's motion stands — record all four and adjudicate none of them**, which Harrow minutes and calls the whole of the moral content of a board in its first sitting. **She is not a member and cannot vote and the chapter says so out loud; her sheet's one function is to hold the four figures in the order they are called out, which is the one thing at that table nobody else was doing.** The one thing that succeeds is Harrow's own ninth word: **no collection is entered while the board is open**, from service of notice to the finding — which he says out loud is worth four days and is a queue behind a door and not a stay, and nobody moves against it, and Hesper Rann's not moving against it is what tells Marek exactly what it is worth. The hearing is set for the sixteenth with four days' notice. The notice is carried down the hill in the rain **by the clerk of the day for the twelfth**, because a notice is served by the clerk who held the sitting and the four other third-grade clerks look at each other and say nothing; **Kelb cannot untie the office string with the right hand and Vray does it for him and neither man says a word.** The notice goes on the board at the Gate under three other boards and is read aloud because the ward's own term says the day is told and not left at an office. And the chapter closes on the thinnest possible inventory: a chair, a Crown March representative serving his own minutes, five members of whom one does not concede she was wrong, a duty to record dissent carried three to one with one refusal on the record, an examiner at the second seat who cannot vote and is holding four numbers, and four days in which nobody's name goes on an invoice.

### Chapter 41 — Nobody Is Sworn
The sixteenth, a Thursday, the eighth bell, the second-floor hearing room on Bell Stair, **built for thirty and holding thirty-one** — a table of five at the wall, seven ward people in the front four rows, and eighteen more behind them, which is the office's three officers, the supervisor of the settlement desk, and fourteen persons of the ward whose names were in no box anywhere, with **the thirty-first having no place at all** — and about three hundred and forty people in the corridor and on the stair. Harrow serves the minutes in his own hand and says at the top of the morning that no office in this city will serve the record of a body it is a party to. Renata Vole opens with three points, two conceded: a certified finding is not appealable and a returnpath needs a living holder of the obligation, and the holder is a schedule with eleven names and no witness; the matter was enlarged on the thirtieth without asking any party. Her third point is the day's first real fight — a ward cannot be sworn, and that is not an injustice but an arithmetic. **Harrow rules she is right on the law and wrong about the consequence, and the ruling is that nobody in the room is sworn: every person is heard as a person in their own name, may answer or may decline, the declining is written as a declining, and no answer binds anybody but the person who gave it.** The second war is about chairs and Vole wins it: the office's three officers stand at the back, because a member of a party is not a witness, and a man who is neither member nor witness is the office, and the office has a chair at that table. Hesper Rann goes to the back and does not look at the table on the way. The Trust produces **one item** and refuses the rest of its own file, on the grounds that the arithmetic is four hands and the board recorded all four on the ninth and adjudicated none, and that an instrument read before an account is a document read to a room that has not been told what it is about. The ward's account then runs to the second bell of the afternoon in the wrong order — Rezek's hand, Ostrow's kitchen, Vray's water on a road and a boy's leg, Rusk's one sentence about a fixed sum and a variable one, Milena Kelb saying her son's name and nothing else for half an hour while Hesper Rann stands nine feet behind her and does not move, Janko Dobre's cellar of condemned tar valued three ways by three people and not at all, and Ondra Kelb last, saying he came because his name is in a book and nobody has looked at him. At the fourth bell Vole asks her one scripted question, and Kern says the ward pays for the water it took and will not sign anything, and says that if the Deputy wants the three refusals' share on somebody else's account she will have to be the one who says so in her own hand in a room, and Vole says it will go in her own hand in a form in a room and will be correct and she will not enjoy it. At the end of the recess Harrow states that he has no clerk, names the clerk of the day for the Tallow file by asking the room, and **Renata Vole is the one who says the name** — because she is not going to be the woman who finds out. Marek comes four steps into the light and says he is the clerk of the day, that he certified the posting on the eleventh, and that the room cannot ask him for the paper because there is no form on which a suspended clerk is a party, and that the paper will be in the record on Saturday whether he asks for it or not.

### Chapter 42 — The Order Things Happened In
The afternoon of the sixteenth. Harrow explains why the ward's account cannot be used: the office keeps its papers in the order of what is owed and the ward keeps its in the order of what happened, and the second order cannot be filed. **Three boards on a wall in forty hands and nineteen answer sheets on a nail, and not one can be carried into a room.** Seven people, because a room holds thirty and a ward is four hundred. The Chair moves the chairs round so the table can see the ward and the ward can see the table, and Rezek has to be helped out of a low chair. **Pella Rusk says the number — fourteen places against ninety-one children — once, in a room, and refuses to say it in a yard ever again, and Harrow insists it go into the finding with the ninety-one in it, because a number said in a room and not in a record is a rumour in nine months.** The Deputy names the bottom of the cut order — the clinic's coal, the ward's night lamp, then the school — and says in the first person that the office has always known the school was the cheapest line and has never had to say it in a room with the ward in it. **Nadja Ostrow refuses the days a second time, in a room, now that the number is in it, and the cost is named in public: a man in a good coat has come up her lane twice and asked her to take money out of her own daughter's seat, and a number being in a room does not put it back.** Kern then objects that the consent is twenty-nine doors out of four hundred and six and that a levy on four hundred names comes apart in a fortnight. Harrow rules on the shape: not days, not four hundred names — **work, named, at the ordinary wage, on a board** — and says the instrument it will be paid out of is the reserve carry, forty thousand nine hundred, unapplied for three hundred years, and that he does not know what is on the line and neither does the Deputy nor the accountant, and that he is going to find out on Friday. Hesper Rann, from the back: "I read it... and it resolves to nothing." "Did you do anything about it." "I wrote the clause."

### Chapter 43 — What the Recital Leans On
The sixth bell. **Hesper Rann asks to read his own entry and gives his reason: nobody in the room knows what any of his sentences mean.** It is two hundred words and the room hears in two minutes that a thing which ruined their month was a page they could have understood in an afternoon. He reads the plate, the date the notice expired, and the third clause — the standing reserve — and slows down on it, and it is a beautiful sentence and it does not say what it means. Harrow works the mechanism out loud: a recited reserve is only a fund if there is something to fund with it, and what funds it is a make-good carried on an account nobody has ever applied, so **the ward does not appear to pay for the gate loan because a thing that does not exist has been set against a thing that has never been applied, and it took about four minutes.** The Deputy says in the first person that she has certified against it four times in nine years and did not know that was what she was doing. Hesper then states that the page is sealed, that the standing order forbids any clerk to copy it, that he has wanted to bring it up the hill for six weeks and has not, and that he understood the order on the ninth and not afterwards. Vole rules that a reference is not a page and lets the footnote be read: *The standing reserve is the first line of the oldest register and is not to be supplied otherwise.* Beneath the line there is a signature, and Hesper reads it out in a room, and it is **A, a stroke, and zero — A-0.** Nobody in the room can say what it is: the Deputy calls it the founding hand and says she cannot tell him what a signature is without the page; Sedge has looked in the Chamber's book; and Pella Rusk asks the only question anybody asks about the blank line, *then what is it for*, and there is no answer, and Harrow writes the admission of that down. The labour-pledge schedule is read as a shape — eleven lines, four columns, eleven sureties named, **the witness column empty on all eleven** — and Harrow counts them himself in the room. Rusk says that a surety on that page is a person who has not been asked to stand anything, and **Marek gives her a technically accurate silence for the second time in a volume, in front of more people than the first.**

### Chapter 44 — The Second Certification
The same evening. Hesper answers the Chair's question about the one-page certification of cause: the totals were added at the counter-desk from the schedule cited at the foot of the fourth clause, which is page three, which the ward's copy does not have, and the only action the cause was capable of is the collection, and the only place that collection can land is the counter-desk. **Kelb says "Oh" from the fourth chair and does not stand up.** Renata Vole dissents on the word *aimed* and her grounds go into the finding in her own words, written by the Chair, because a member proposed the term and she was the one vote against — **the first use of the board's own duty to record dissent, and Harrow notes that it is being used against the man who proposed it.** Then Marek, from the back, with no standing and no paper, says that he wrote nine words on the twenty-first of the rains in his own book in the cause box, that they are the nine words of that page, and **that he cannot produce them because the book also holds a sheet copied out of a page no clerk may copy, and producing one produces the other** — and states that he is not confessing, because he wrote a copy of a cause, not the cause, and that his name is not on the page and has never been. **He surrenders the certificate of posting**, the paper he signed on the eleventh with *Yes* on the party query, on the grounds that a man who keeps the only clean thing he has and then asks a room for anything is a man who kept it for that, and states that he is not able to give it because there is no form on which a suspended clerk can. Vole rules that a person may put a paper before a board. **Harrow rules that the supervisor of the settlement desk certifies nothing further in the proceeding** — not a suspension, a Chair declining to accept an instrument. Hesper takes the point and wins it: he could produce the page and the Chair could not have taken the power, and he has not produced it, and it is because he is not willing to be a man who brought it up the hill. And he says the other thing: **I have never said I was wrong about the gate.** Nobody asks him.

### Chapter 45 — Does the Ward Have Water
An hour and a half of the Chair asking questions, **and the examination of Rezek is played rather than reported: his hands out on his knees and shut again, the Chair asking what the wet-weather rider was for, and *there is no line anywhere for the twelfth* said twice by a man who has been saying it to himself for a month; Grale's name; and Rezek putting his hands into his coat pockets at the end, which takes him longer than it should have.** Hesper Rann stands nine feet from the window for the whole of it and is not looked at by **any of the seven**, which was a decision about forty people made at once. ### Chapter 46 — Fourteen Places
The seventeenth, in the Gate yard, on two trestle tables, with about four hundred and six people and the rain not doing anything. **Vole says in the first person that the office certified the settlement and is not striking it, that the collection was held four days by a term she could not out-reason, and that the ward drank four hundred and forty against a lawful hundred and sixty and is still going to pay.** The finding is made in the open, in sentences, one at a time, and nobody holds a page. ### Chapter 47 — Both, in One Paragraph

### Chapter 48 — Eleven Days a Season
The twenty-second, in the Gate yard. **The W-4 comes out of custody in a Trust officer's bundle, produced by Renata Vole unasked, because a form that has been in a drawer in a cell for three days is a form that has been taken out of a man's hands.** The form names a span, eleven days a season, three seasons, a start, and a witness. **Tomas Vale comes down and says no** — not to the schedule, which he signed in front of two witnesses, but to the framing: nobody is giving him anything, the draw posted on the fourteenth as scheduled, twenty-six months is twenty-six months, and a man who takes the help for the schedule and lets anybody call it a rescue has misunderstood what a schedule is. He also says the one thing nobody else will: that his mother has a mark on his brother's arm with four notches in it and she has never said where it came from, and that he is putting *nobody was helped* into the yard's mouth now so that in four years there is one sentence from somebody who was there. ### Chapter 49 — Four Rooms
Four rooms, four accounts, none of them resolved. **In the Fund's room over the chandler's shop**, Zorka Vendl pays a clinic, a cot, medicine and the Ropewalk maker by name (Hesper's kindness of the twentieth of the rains, entered because it was correct and cost him nothing), the Chamber pays a clerk's injury out of a levy on the hall after nine years, and the new sum comes out of the carry. She says four things about *enough* in the first person — it does not buy a hand, it does not buy the years, and the fourth thing is that she does not know what the fourth thing is, and there are four marks in her discharge book meaning a clerk who never came back and she is not opening that in a room with a mother in it. **Kelb's only question is whether the finding says the right name: not *careless*, not *the clerk at the counter-desk* — and it does, and the word *careless* is in the finding too because it is in the certification on the twelfth and cannot be taken out of the record, with the finding saying beside it that no witness said it.** **In a room in Crown March**, Hesper Rann offers four dates on which he made an entry in the account desk that was not a levy, a schedule, a recertification or a return, and there is no box for the fourth kind; Grale stands up at the fourth date and gives three of the four and keeps the fourth, because there is a line in that account standing since before the flood years and if he says the date in a room it is in a record in Crown March, and *the day I prove it, I have to stop.* **In her own office**, Renata Vole is reassigned to the coastal returns and does not accept the characterisation: the finding of the thirtieth stands, the ward will still pay, and she was argued out of her own opinion in a yard. And she says the other thing — her office has twice entered a member's grounds in a year-end reading without being asked, because of a term she refused for nine years, put there by a Crown March representative she refused on a point of form. **On the Tally Hall door**, a sealed Trust notice: the public copy of the first page is to be replaced, the founding hand is not to be reproduced in it, the original is not to be removed, and the work is to be carried out by the officer of the record and by no other hand. The duty clerk reads it out twice and nobody will admit to having read it.

### Chapter 50 — The Line He Leaves Open
The last evening of the rains, the copy room, the lamps going along the north wall, and Marek with the oldest reference volume out of its case and shut on the table, and nobody in the building who would come and close it. The standing order is in two parts and he has it by heart: no clerk may erase the signature, and no clerk may copy the page. **He copies the page.** The head-line first, and then the ruled line — and he puts the nib on it and understands, for the first time in five weeks, what Hesper was doing in the vault on the ninth: a man alone in a room deciding whether to put something in an empty line. **He starts a four, stops, caps the pen, and then does not blot it — because a blot lifts the grain and that paper is three hundred years old — and goes along the four once with the blade of the small knife that lives in the drawer of every desk in that room, and stops where the stroke stopped, so that the line is blank and clean, and the page says what it has said for three hundred years.** Then he writes the signature — which is a thing a clerk is taught to do properly: written out in full, letter by letter, with *written in full*, the date, and his own name and no office. **He reads the line back and does not change one word of it.** **The reasoning is the volume's: the Trust's notice cannot be carried out, because the clerk of record has not been held for three centuries and nobody can be appointed, so the public register will simply have an empty page where the oldest page should be — and an empty page with nothing under it is not a blank line with a signature on it, it is a page anybody at all may write in, and in nine months somebody will, for a good reason, with no idea.** Lio, nine, off the roll, term shut, comes up because her mother is at the seamstress's and the duty clerk lets children in and has never written that down. She asks why he left the line empty. **He gives her the only true thing he has: somebody has to write down what the tower owes, and the taking is always written down before anybody has looked at the giving, and a man who writes a number in an empty line so that the page balances has only moved the emptiness.** She asks who the clerk of record is; he says nobody, and that he is not going to go and ask why the notice has been under his feet for fourteen days, because the answer would be that nobody knows. Then she asks who is going to write the next page, and he says **the line is not going to be closed while I am the clerk of record** — and hears himself say it, and does not take it back, and there was nobody in that room to take it back in front of, and it was said to a nine-year-old on a bench, and not in a room in Bell Stair, and it is in nobody's record, because he had no office to file a record in. **The copy goes into the public register on the table by the window, where anybody who comes up the hill can read it, and about forty people did.** The Trust's sealed notice stays nailed to the Tally Hall door, unsigned by anybody who will admit to reading it. The four yards are running on the public levy, the lamps are lit along the Tallow Gate, the Ropewalk standpipe is short, and Vray's storehouse is still under nine feet of water with the pump on it.

## Volume 02 — The Sable Market

### Chapter 51 — Nine Months
The **second of the seed month, MR 413**, nine days after the reckoning turned. The levy office receives its first question about the reserve carry in three hundred years — not a demand, a query from the water office asking the levy office to exhibit the line and to **advise the district** — and Grale reads it twice and says the thing that matters: *it is not about the money, it is about the word "named."* On Bell Stair, Marek finds that the assignment column has no letter beside his name; **Sedge** explains without unkindness that there is no form for a clerk who is employed and not assigned, and that the eight words of the slip that came down on the eighteenth are the reason. ### Chapter 52 — The Man Who Is Useful
The third, in the Elmsworth seed house: forty-one shallow trays of river sand under oiled paper, turned twice a day by a woman who knows them by the marks on the ends. **Mirren Fisk** will not speak to a man from Bell Stair. **Neris Kade** is there first with a sheet she does not use, because she is Rainmarket's examiner this season, and she says the volume's argument from his side — *useful is not standing, and somebody is going to take the usefulness off you at the worst possible moment and it will not be malicious, it will be a Tuesday.* The Share's membership is read out and a decision is carried **on a count of hands**, forty of the forty-one hands in the room, and Kade knows without being told that a count of hands is why a cooperative can be sold: fifty-two people can be asked the same question separately over eight months and there is no meeting anywhere in the instrument. **Wenna Drel, sixteen, is on the roll because her mother is**, has drawn seed, has filled eleven cones, and has never voted. Marek asks whether anybody has read the whole instrument; Fisk says the fourth clause is the threshing share. **It is not.** He cannot tell her, and notices that the wall he is on is the wall from last year and he is on the other side of it.

### Chapter 53 — What a Counterweight Is For
The fourth, in the copy room, and the batch's first practice chapter. **Ostrowsky**, nineteen years in the levy office, explains what a counterweight is, and is embarrassed to be asked, and is wrong twice before he is right: a sequence loan moves the order of one narrow local event, whatever does not happen is booked against something, **and if the name is a person then that person is in the room with a witness, and if the name is a district then it is a box the office can write in.** The Share's fourth clause is correct, well drafted, and lethal, and it names the ground the seed is going into. He wants to write **twenty-two words** in the margin and there is nowhere to put a true sentence about a thing nobody has asked about. **Alone on the stair landing, the batch's one Ledger Sight panel, and it shows an absence:** > The counterweight is named. / Behind the name there is nobody. / Bring nothing to the low ground. Count. **The cost is a promise he made to his mother, at her own door, and cannot remember making.** Tamsin tells him at the door that he said he would come at the second bell and did not come, and does not ask, and says once that she is not going to ask him what it is, and gives the reason, and tells him to go to the low ground on the ninth because the seed carts come on the sixth and seventh. **He names the mark on his forearm once, in his own head, as one of the things the Sight has cost him, and nobody in the room can see it.**

### Chapter 54 — Ovid Merrow's Morrowfen House
The fifth, second bell to sixth, one room over a chandler's shop on the Rainmarket side. **Ovid Merrow**, 47, nine years running the Morrowfen house, has a licence framed on the wall and offers two hours and no management. He does not lie once. He explains that the product is not credit but **explanation**; that the Trust cannot explain its own paper to anybody who is not a clerk; that the counterweight clause is not the Compact's invention and is **eighteen months old** and well drafted; that the assignments are not the Compact's either. He says a district is not a person and never was, and that the office which cannot ask a district has been handing him a district for nine years. And then he says the thing the batch is built on: **he has tried for nine years to buy the oldest page in the city, in two refused offers, and cannot, because a claim is a thing somebody stands behind and a line with nothing in it has nobody behind it — and when a public register carries a page anybody may read, somebody will copy it, and a claim with nobody behind it will have a piece of paper, and he will buy that in a morning.** (**The four years he names twice in this chapter are the four years it took him to put the difficulty into one sentence and to see what he would do with it, not the years he has been buying.**) He does not know what the marks at the foot of it are. He discloses his own future crime: he would enter it as a claim with no counterparty, which is worse than selling it, and he has not been able to make himself not do it. He leaves a card with a figure on it, and Marek puts it in the pocket where he keeps the little book of promises he made his mother.

### Chapter 55 — The Kitchen With the Second-Hand Plough
The sixth, after the second bell, on the low ground. **Marek is not in the room** and the chapter is not his; he stands in a wet field thirty feet from an open door for an hour and forty minutes and hears it. **Ilse Ferrow** sits on a barrel, is likable, gives her name twice, and reads the figures: seed at eleven hundred, seven hundred and forty if you take only what you can stand, the paper at three hundred and not a loan, a second-hand plough at forty, a girl of nineteen who goes or stays. The only question in the room is *who pays the other year*, and Ferrow answers it exactly: the failure is booked, the tower has nobody to give it to, it has a place, and the place is a district. And when Iren Orleb asks what happens in a **good** year, she tells him the second thing: the good year is not the safe one, it is the year that pays for it, and buys him the time in which to be charged. He signs, and Marek hears the pen. **And then the floor drops out:** Orleb signed the assignment on the fourteenth of the second month, MR 412, eight months before the Compact had a house in Morrowfen. He is not being cheated tonight. Ferrow says it out loud in the lane for him, at the top of her voice, on purpose. **The question in the kitchen is asked by the daughter of nineteen, not by Orleb, and the woman who answers it is Ferrow, in the lane, about an hour after the bell — Kade is not in this chapter at all.**

### Chapter 56 — It Balances
The seventh, the counting floor. **Ondra Kelb** will not let Marek help, and is doing the seventeenth bundle of the day with his left hand, which is nine years old. Four hours of counting, the whole thing again in a different order from the back of the sheet, and then: **the bundle balances to the penny.** Every rate, date, witness, signature and figure in the stack is permitted by a rule that predates the Compact. **There is no false term, no invented reserve, no omitted page, no aimed chain, and nothing anybody can be shown to have done wrong.** Kelb says the sentence the chapter exists for — he is the only one in the building who could find this, because he is the only one who counts twice — and then says that he has been **relieved**, and that the relief is shameful, and that he is saying it out loud because there is one other person in Bell Stair who would understand what it costs to be comforted by an absence of proof. Marek asks for the number to be said twice more, out loud, with a witness, and the duty clerk from the posting desk reads the aggregate, **one thousand nine hundred and sixteen in fifty-two assigned claims out of sixty-one members**, twice, the second time slower. And then out of the same arithmetic, in a room, by a character: **the fourth kind is named.** There is no box for an assignment, because the form was cut before anybody was doing it, so for eight years an assignment has gone in the box for a return — lawfully, because a district may hand over a returnable sum. **The fifth box goes back further than eight years, and there is a hand in it Kelb cannot place, and he is not going to say more about it in that hall.**

### Chapter 57 — The Price of a Market
The eighth, from the second bell in the morning, on **Oren Pike's floor**. An hour on the floor teaches Marek two things: that Pike has been kind to the eleven men on his slate in a way the floor talks about, and what the call is — four numbers, nine seconds, four seconds of silence in which ninety people do not shout, and in those four seconds the day's price is set by men who cannot read a single instrument on their own desks. Pike does not want to stop the Compact. He wants to price it, and the argument is commercial and is made well: an assigned claim is a supply of collectable water, it puts a floor under the collection, the Share's hearing gets worse and every other price gets better, and the day he buys the paper the low ground's collection is worth less to the district and there is no way to put it back. He says the unfair true thing about Tomas — the span, three seasons of eleven days at the ordinary wage, and a Crown March countersignature still on a road between here and the coast — and he says **Ansgar Drel**, whom he paid a hundred and forty for a quarter of the oldest kept-back stock in the basin in the bad spring, at a good price, which was the price at which you stop. Then he lays his own house's read of the same papers on the table and gives it away: *I do not care who is owed. I care that I cannot price a thing I cannot read.*

### Chapter 58 — A Shower in the Wrong Place
The ninth, first light to dark, in a field. A shower went over the ridge at the fourth bell and did not touch the low ground, and four miles of country is the whole of the mechanism. At the bench, **nine of sixty-one trays have failed**, all of them off the oldest kept-back stock in the basin, out of a shed that was shut up wet in the flood year — and **Sera Holt** refuses to let the two bad facts of the same nine days be made into one: *a tray of seed does not fail because a shower went over a ridge four miles off*, and a man in a good chair in the ninth month will say which one it was with a figure in front of him and not with a tray. Then she gives the chapter's cost in a body's calendar: a sixth of nothing is nothing, four men for nine days were paid on the tenth, the second sowing is not a rescue of the first, and the difference goes on the district, and the district's answer is a rate. Kade returns at the sixth with the one household that did not take the paper — **Ansgar Drel, 78**, on the high ground, which got the curtain — and is pleased, and then is not, because the household that took it is **Iren Orleb's**, two hundred yards away on the flat, and being pleased about a good year is the same as being pleased about this. **The paper did not do it. The clause is eighteen months old, sixty-one names are on one seal, forty hands went up for it in a shed and twenty-one of the sixty-one were not in the room, and nobody hid anything from anybody.** And then the sentence the volume runs on: *it has been called a district for three hundred years; somebody has to be the first person who says the word out loud in a room.* Marek writes four words in pencil on the back of the list — *A district is not a person* — and knows there is no box for it.

### Chapter 59 — Fewer of Them Than in the Rains
The tenth, in rain. The Trust's Rainmarket credit window is barred and the painted board is dated the sixth: *seed advances suspended, district in arrears of two quarters, no advance may be made against a district whose rate is uncollected.* **Rustam Vey** comes out and says there is no argument available, and that the low ground has been in arrears since before the water came into the kitchens, and that he would lend at the ordinary figure this afternoon to a clean district, and that the window was shut on the sixth because of the flood years and would have been shut on the fourth. ### Chapter 60 — A Copy in the Open
The eleventh, the sixth bell to the last of the light, and the batch's climax. **Kelb** comes at the closing bell and Marek makes the copy in the open with the door open and a second person reading the figures aloud, and at the foot of the last sheet he writes the standing that a copyist is taught — the figures agree, the copy made in the copy room, with a second person present, **M. The Trust's notice is still on the door, the public register still has a blank line and a signature written out in full, and the seed goes into the ground on the twelfth.

### Chapter 61 — Forty-Three and Some Months
The **thirteenth**, the carriers' row between the Tallow Gate and the north road, four sheds and forty feet of wet stone, and a plate on a door with a district number under it. **Kelb does not want to be there and says so twice**, and the chain is counted out in chalk on the flat side of a handcart's shaft rather than at a table: five households, the low ground's uncollectable flood quarters in two parts on two dates, the Compact's house, a carrier, a district. **The eighth link's rule is the chapter's institutional discovery:** an uncollectable private sum of under fifty can go to a fund (there is none), an estate (a year, a clerk, a seal, and nobody does that for forty-three), or a ratepayer — and the only ratepayer that can take it without a person's name going anywhere is **a yard with a plate on the door.** **Bartel Reisz**, 58, thirty-one years on that row, is hoisting tallow churns and does not put the hammer down when he is told. He has paid the water office every quarter of his life and has never had a bad word off a clerk, and when he is asked whose forty-three it is he says **he will pay it, will pay it to whoever brings it, and wants it written down that he will pay it even if it is a bad thing** — and Kelb writes it in a book with no letter beside his name and cannot give it to anybody. Marek says *not yet* and means it: nobody has come to that door yet. **The batch states a refusal and not a total:** Kelb cannot say how many of the twenty-two files Marek finished, because a column of names is not a number and a total would be a claim; Sedge has the fourth and will do it badly on purpose. On the causeway Marek understands the real change of the day: a man willing to hold a thing can be **given** it.

### Chapter 62 — The First Thing in Three Hundred Years
The **fourteenth** in a Crown March room over a granary, and the **fifteenth** in a corridor at Bell Stair. **Ivo Sarn**, a Crown March officer with a minute book and no clerk, **reads the list before he discusses it** — four dates, in order, without adjectives — and Hesper Rann corrects the second one, and Sarn enters the correction and does not ask why, and when Rann offers to be asked he tells him that would open a second minute about a different question. Asked what the entry was, in words, **Rann names the kind: an assignment.** A ratepayer in arrears hands the collection right at a discount, one line, dated, signed by him, and it has gone into the box for a return for eight years and **the return is the correct box**, because a rate arrears is a returnable sum. He does not give the fourth date: it is not his, the man who gave the line under oath is going to have to stop when he proves it, and a date said in a room is in a Crown March record inside a week. He is asked whether he would do it again and says **yes**, and says that nobody ever puts that sentence on the same page as the other ones. He distinguishes the gate from the water entry and does not apologise. The minute's institutional object is the referral: **the fifth box, and the hands in its entries earlier than the eighth year, referred to the office at Bell Stair which keeps the form.** Sarn's point: a conviction ends in a sentence a man can serve; a practice ends when the people doing it stop, and only they can stop it. **Marek's letter of the twelfth is the reason any of this happens**, and in the corridor **Neris Kade is right about a market in a Crown March record and cannot do anything about it**: she states in Sarn's book, in her own words and read back twice, that no member of the Share has read the instrument end to end in nineteen months, and Sarn warns her she cannot take it out. That is the Tuesday arriving on her, and nobody thanks her.

### Chapter 63 — The Same Four Questions
The **sixteenth** at the Gate tank, about two hundred people, and the nineteen working sheets from the first round still on the nail with their strings on. The second sheets are copied by two children and **not permitted one word of difference.** The round's rule: ask the same four questions, write the answer in the words given, and where the answer is not the answer of the first round, write the first answer underneath it with the date. **No totals, because a total read in a room in six months is a number of people who agreed with something.** Then **Teodor Lask, nineteen, asks whether a round that repeats is a series**, and Vray says: a series of asks at a stated interval is a schedule, a schedule is the third box on the settlement desk's form, and the four kinds that go in the four boxes are the four kinds Hesper Rann has been in a cell for a fortnight working out. **The yard votes to go round once more and once only, and the board at the tank says two and nothing under it.** The pairs go out in rain at the third bell; three hundred and seventy-seven of four hundred and six have never been asked anything by anybody. **And the Tuesday arrives, in the yard, in front of two hundred people, and it is not malicious:** Sanne Droll, thirty-nine, a rope-maker, tells Marek to his face that he has read four unclosable figures out loud in her yard, that he knows what four hundred and six people owe before they have been asked, and that when a paper comes up this road with a district's name at the head of it there will be somebody in the city who says *ask the man in the yard, he had it first*. **She gives no date and neither does any state file.** She is right. Nobody thanked her for the first round either. The cost of a second round is named: a day and a half out of a wage, twelve days off a vat, two hundred and sixty households that can spare somebody and a hundred and forty that cannot — and a household that said yes five weeks ago says no, in the doorway, before either of them has asked anything, and nobody asks why, and the yard draws a second line and refuses to say what it means.

### Chapter 64 — In the Yard's Own Words
The **seventeenth**, a letter dated the fourteenth, and an offer of about **nine hundred and forty** in exchange for the ward taking on, as a levy, a rate arrears of forty-three and some months against a licensed carrier at the Tallow Gate and the low ground's uncollectable flood rate in two parts. **Ilse Ferrow comes herself, in a working coat, and opens no ledger**, and states in a yard with two hundred people in it — at the top of her voice, on purpose — that the offer is real, that nine hundred is the ward's, and that **the third thing is also the ward's**: this is the fourth clause coming due at the bottom of eight steps, Tallow Ward is the district, and a ward is the only account in this basin that can take money it is not owed without anybody's name going anywhere. The yard refuses in **nine minutes, in its own words, on a second-round sheet with two of the yard's hands on it, read out twice by the keeper and by the nineteen-year-old reader** — because the carrier offered to pay his own forty-three and the yard is not going to take it off him because a man in a good coat will pay it for him, because a levy is a thing a ward raises on itself and the ward has one and it is not drawn, and because a carrier in this ward paying a stranger's rate turns a plate on a shed door into a sign that says this yard takes money. **Marek says nothing for all nine minutes and would have been able to stop it**, and knows that if he had, the sheet would have had his hand on it. The refusal goes on the nail on the twentieth with the nineteen working sheets and is still on it, and no office has come to that tank. And then, four days later, a woman from the second pair says her household signed for the nine hundred in a lane two days after the yard said no, because her daughter needed shoes, and the yard asks her once whether the yard's no had anything to do with it and writes the answer down: **a yard's refusal binds the yard and has never in the history of this city bound a household, and there is no form on which it could.**

### Chapter 65 — Eleven of Sixty-One
The **eighteenth** at the seed house bench. Two more marks on the marks: **eleven of sixty-one, all off the same stock out of the same shed**, and Fisk's position is that a seed does not fail because of a schedule, and that she has said the same sentence three times in four days and the weather has not moved. **She offers Marek the room over the seed shop on the far side of the road — four a month, a table, a lock, a window on the seed house yard — and the reason is that he read the fourth clause out loud in her building on the third, and she says it is not because he is useful, because a person who is useful in a yard is a different person from a person who is useful to a house.** The season is the other half: a return in kind, eleven days of labour and a third of another member's kept-back, and where a member cannot bring in a third the shortfall goes on that member's ground as a rate, because **there is no box on the roll for a member in illness and no box for a member who cannot work.** **Ansgar Drel, 78, who did not take the paper and whose ground came up, is carrying a third of his sack for eleven households**, and asked whether the rule is the same if his own lot fails, and was told yes, and said *then it is all right*, and meant it. Marek does the shortfall out loud: a settlement out of a harvest that will not come, no surety for a member's own lot, and the chain walks past the Share to the office that certified it — **not the same rule as the carrier's, but drawable with one pencil, and better said in this shed than by a man in a good coat in the ninth month.** The bench book gets eleven, two sheds and a year, and no names. And Wenna Drel is at the far end filling cones, and one of the eleven trays is hers, and Marek works it out and says nothing, and Fisk tells him she is not to be told at the bench, and says she will tell her in three days in a yard with her mother not there.

### Chapter 66 — One Sentence
The **nineteenth**, two offices and a counter. **The Compact answers in a day**, with one page, and the page is one line — *received, one instrument* — because a claim in a register is a claim a buyer comes to find himself in, and a house cannot unbuild that in a year. Merrow's four sentences are the whole of his trade: **a schedule is a list of people, a register is a place people come to look for themselves, and a person who finds himself in a register has been in it.** He will read a rate in his own house, free, in a chair, in the words of the roll. **The district refuses the second request on one sentence:** a district's roll shows what is owed to the district, a sum handed over is no longer owed to the district, and there is therefore nothing on the roll for the district to show. **The margin of the whole failure is one word** — she asked for the *names*; the *entry* (total, two dates, receiving party) is exhibited on application in one day under seal, and the accountant has offered it eleven times in four years. Then **a rate can be read and a copy of a rate cannot be produced**: Marek reads the low ground's third quarter off the roll at Berta Lund's counter, finds a component in the same line that is not water and has no box of its own, and is told he may read as long as he likes, that a copy of a rate is a return, that a return is an office's document, and that there is no form for *I stood at a counter and asked*. And the reason the paper travels at all: **a Saltglass counterseal names a month, a tide phase, a harbor seal and a translator; no officer on Bell Stair may read one; an audit in this city goes as far as the seal and stops, and the seal is the whole of the paper's portability.** He takes nothing away.

### Chapter 67 — The Last Line
The **twentieth**. **Vey's rule applied correctly, and the lane empties**: the flood-years assessments are restated on the eighteenth, the window reopens at the first bell, and Vey stands in front of it and will not be a hero about it — the window was correctly shut, four households lost a fortnight, and he has been right and it cost a fortnight of somebody's seed. Tam Rell holds a list for an hour for a queue that has stopped existing. **The first cut order of MR 413 goes on the wall in the frame that has no copy**, read in the words of the office to every party it names before it is entered, carrying the day and not the month and the name of the officer who enters it. Six parties; five are offices and each has a person to write beside its own line; **the sixth is a business, so the officer goes**, and Grale walks up the causeway to the seed house, which is last on the order. **Fisk asks for the last line again.** *The Elmsworth seed house's lamp — cut after every line above it, supplied in the third month.* And she says she has no answer and was not asked one, and then says the one thing she came to say: *you have signed two of these in your life and this is the second, and it is the first that has been read to a party before it was entered, and that happened because of a rule and not because of you* — and decides, out loud, not to say the rest of it. **His hand goes bad on the last letter of his own name at the foot of the frame and nobody sees it.** And at the second bell that morning a Crown March referral arrived: the fifth box, and the hands in its entries earlier than the eighth year, referred to this office. **He reads it, and does not open the form, and leaves it in the tray** — because a referral asks a question, and a man who goes looking for his own condition has already decided the answer, and his condition is that the day he proves the line in the account desk he has to stop. Then Fisk walks up the hill at the seventh bell and takes the room back, in four flat sentences: the last line on the first cut order of the year is her lamp, the second is her coal, and in the ninth month a person with no office who lives over a seed shop is a person the office will send for — **not because of what he did, because of where his bed is** — and she will put the room in the back of the bench book with the date it changed, because a thing that changed and does not say when is a thing somebody can use two ways.

### Chapter 68 — The Second Line
The **twenty-first** and the **twenty-second**, a girl of sixteen and a second line. Fisk tells Wenna Drel in the seed house yard with the bench book open, in its own words and without improving them. **Her first question is *whose name is on the tray*, and the answer is her mother's, and the second is *whose ground is the rate on*, and the answer is: the family, because a member who is ill is still a member and there is no box on a rate on which a person is asked whether she is well.** She goes back to the trestle alone and asks for Ferrow's second-line paper, and Ferrow gives her the same answer in the same voice and refuses again to write the mother's illness on anything, **and refuses in front of Marek, who does not help.** Then the argument of the batch, and it is not won: **Kade says a fact that lives in one woman's coat in one house over a chandler's shop dies with the house, and a register with nothing about the girl in it will be right and wrong at once; Marek says the moment it goes onto a form it becomes a form about a girl of sixteen that she did not give**, in a month when a cooperative is putting a rate on her mother's ground. Both say the true thing; neither says the braver thing. Later, at the seed house door, Marek reads her the line and cannot write it out; **she works out that her name is on a tray and not on a paper, that her mother's name is on the roll, and that the only form in the city with her name on it is a bench book in a shed that nobody has to look at.** She tells him he has a sentence in his coat, and he tells her it has no box and will not be in any room he ever goes into, and she says: *then somebody should be given one* — and he tells her that is the whole of what he has been unable to do for five weeks, and that nobody in this city will invent one this year. She counts nine cones and a half of the second sowing onto the bench and goes up the road to turn sixty-one trays.

### Chapter 69 — The Best Thing in the Lane
The **twenty-second**, from the fourth bell, an eleven-by-nine shed forty feet down the lane, taken on the fifteenth from a man who sells pitch. **A REGISTER OF ASSIGNED CLAIMS. OPEN FOUR HOURS A DAY. NO CHARGE. ANY PERSON MAY ASK.** Fifty-two lines, each with a date, a rate period, a sum, a discount, the receiving party — and beside them, in a second hand, **what the household said when it was asked what it had signed**: eight days, thirty-eight answered, fourteen entered as *asked, and no answer given, and asked once*, which is the ward's rule and was not the house's. **The fourth clause is in it, in the low ground's own words**, and eleven of the fifty-two used the word *district* and understood it. Merrow did not teach them anything; he asked a question and wrote down what came out, and he says that is his trade and that this city has been explaining itself in kitchens for two hundred years and has never written any of it down. **The seventh column has nothing at the head of it**, because a board painter and a signwriter were going to head it and he sent them home: *a heading is the one thing on a sheet that cannot be put in afterwards by anybody who was not there on the day* — which is the yard's sentence, out of a lender's mouth, and a Tallow rope-maker hears it and does not tell him where he got it. **Halvard Sunn**, 44, puts a counterseal on the board and refuses to put one on the book, because a mark in the Tide Reckoning is a calendar mark and not a warranty about a figure. **Marek sees two marks on that board in the same hand**, one of which has been on the bundle for a fortnight, and understands that nobody in Morrowfen can check either, and writes nothing down and tells nobody. Two hundred people go through the shed; the Tallow Gate entry is on a bench on the right-hand wall and shows a licensed carrier, forty-three and some months, and *asked, and no answer* — **and no other link, which is not an accident and is the whole of what a lender is for.** And then Merrow, from the step of his own shed, says the best thing that has happened in this lane this month happened in Tallow Ward on a sheet with a yard behind it and no holder at all, and that a yard is not a person and there is no form on which a yard can be a party, and that for the first time in nine years there is a document in this basin that nobody can be shown to be the seller of — and that he has worked out in eight days what an empty column head is worth, and it is worth more than the shed.

### Chapter 70 — A Date
The **twenty-third**, the second-floor hearing room built for thirty with nine in it. **The Trust's seat is empty** and Harrow has asked twice and been refused twice, and the office's second answer contained the words *in the ordinary course*, and he has minuted them in quotation marks. **A Crown March representative can call a meeting and cannot pay anybody out of it** — that is the chapter's spine and Harrow says it about himself, in the first person, and the Fund has proved out of nineteen years of books that it cannot pay a sitting and a ward cannot be assessed a rate at all. **Kern answers the question himself: there is no date.** Then an hour and a half of business that is not the thing anybody came for: a date for the next sitting (the third bell on the fourth day of the growth month), the empty Trust seat minuted three times, Vendl's four-line dissent in her own words under term four, and **Neris Kade from the second seat, which is not a membership and cannot vote**, minuted as an examiner: *being right about a market has been worth exactly nothing for twenty days, and the best thing anybody has done about it belongs to a man with a licence, and she has not thanked him and is not going to, because he is not a friend and she does not think he wants to be.* At the end Harrow asks Marek one question, from the back of the room, with no standing: **is there a date on which anybody in this city can be made to say what the charge is, before the charge is made?** Marek says no, and then gives the table the only date he has — a frost in the last week of the month that is coming whether the second crop is sown or not, after which there is no second crop this year for eleven households — and asks that it be minuted that a man with no office has named a date he cannot reach and declined to say where he got it. Harrow reads his own five lines into the minute, **and the clock is named out loud: the carry due in the ninth month, the letter that will use the word *advise* and name a district which cannot be in that room, the chain of assignments arriving at the same district's account in the same month, the school last on an order with no copy, and this board with no fund and an empty seat and no date given.** It goes on a nail at the door and nine people read it and nobody admits to it. Kade says two things in the passage that have to do each other for eight months, and tells him she has noticed he has not been back to the carriers' row since the thirteenth. He stands at the foot of a stair with nothing in his hands, a card in his coat, a pencil line on the back of a list, no office, no form and no room, and two charges coming into the same month, and not one person in Morrowfen who can be made to say what either of them is.

### Chapter 71 — The Line in the Ditch
A frost comes in off the ridge in the night of the **twenty-fourth of the seed month**, twelve days after the seed went in, and finishes the second crop in nine hundred and forty acres. Fisk sends a boy up the causeway with three words on a card. In a field at the bottom of the low ground, a strip nine feet wide along the drainage ditch is **a fortnight up** on the rest of the field, and Kade says *nine days* once and will not say it again. The man who owns the top half is forty-one and has a high-rate side and a low-rate side because a sluice was cut into the high side twenty-two years back; he read his own line at Berta Lund's counter on the twentieth and was told, correctly, that the ground coming up early is not a line on this roll. **Fisk's four facts and no fifth**: a shower over the ridge on the ninth that missed by four miles; the stock out of a shed shut up wet in the flood year; a ditch with a rate in it on each side; the restatement of the flood quarters on the eighteenth, on a correct rule, by an officer who has said out loud that four households lost a fortnight while it was shut. *A rate is a line in a ditch.* Kade answers that this is a fact about a roll and not about ground. He asks whether his rate will change, and Marek says no, and the man agrees and says he had thought so. **A counterweight is not a switch.** It is a rule about what happens to a district, the district has no hand on anything, and it will do exactly what it was written to do in the ninth month with nobody able to object or even to hear about it. He takes out the list of twenty-two files, writes nothing on the back of it, and puts it away.

### Chapter 72 — Nothing Is Charged
The Share's eleven failed lots and one remaining lot of kept-back, forty-one sacks in **Hartlow, four terraces up in the Red Uplands**, and the old rule that **a season examiner may not carry a lot she has examined**. Kade is Rainmarket's examiner, Fisk is a party, the Trust is the certifier, and all eleven men on Pike's slate are paid to be elsewhere; the only person who is none of those and has no office and therefore cannot be sent for in the ninth month is Marek, and Kade says so and does not pretend it is anything else. He says yes before she has finished being uncomfortable about having said it. In the valley: **Janko Vrabec**, fifty-eight, last of them, with a boy of nineteen who is better at it than he was and a woman of fifty-one who does not want it. **She is the mother of the boy and she is the woman who writes the names on the wall by the fire, and she is fifty-one now; she was fourteen when the paper was done to her, found out eleven years afterwards, and was twenty-five when she came to Vrabec in the winter and asked to see it. Those two figures were in conflict in the prose and are now fixed, and no later chapter may give her a third.** A room off the seed room with a fire and a bench and a table and a notice nailed to the outside of the door — *ANYBODY MAY COME IN AND SIT AND LOOK. NOTHING IS CHARGED. NOTHING IS SIGNED.* — opened **fourteen months ago** because a girl of fourteen in that village found out eleven years afterwards that she had been on a roll her whole life. **Kade certified the village's instrument two years back**, before the room was open, and wrote one sentence on the back of the second leaf: the pledge is accepted as a class and not as an individual — and under it, *And the seed-keepers of this village after us bear the keeping.* She has looked for what she did wrong for a year and a half and there is nothing wrong with it. Two witnesses, neither paid, one dead. Six women on the valley's roll, four of them over fifty, the fifth a child, the sixth Kade, who is not a member of anything. On the wall by the fire, names in a fair hand with dates, the last one Vrabec at **nineteen**, in a different hand, and no office behind it. On the flat above the mill she puts the argument herself and neither of them wins it: **a class is not a person, and a person named in a paper is a person**, and fourteen months of a village doing a thing themselves is not a class being a person. Nobody has thanked her and nobody is going to, and she did not come up the road to be forgiven either, and on Tuesday he will still go and she will still be right about him. The forty-one sacks go down on the hired cart at the third bell two days later.

### Chapter 73 — A Body's Calendar
The Share works its **eleven days of labour on the sixth of the growth month**, in one field, in a line, in the frost in the shadow of the ditch, and nobody voted for it and nobody can be asked about it. Nineteen people: eleven households whose trays went to sand, and eight members who carry a share because the return in kind divides it across the members. **Ilma Tresch**, forty-one, drew one of the eleven trays in the fourth week of the seed month, filled cones, carried two sacks, did all eleven days, and has had a cough since the floods that is now in the small of her back and that she did not say on the field. At the low ground clinic at the second bell of the seventh, **Sera Holt, twenty-six years**: **a field's calendar is a year and it is the same year for everybody and the district can be named in it; a body's calendar is a month and it is a different month every season and it is not a district and cannot be one.** A rate is a field instrument, written for a parcel, and a woman who cannot lift in the growth month is not a parcel. **There is no box on a rate on which a person is asked whether she is well, and the absence is not an oversight; the absence is the form working exactly as it was cut.** Marek describes the rule in nine seconds — if she does the days the balance is a rate, and if she cannot do the days the balance is also a rate, and she has one of them whether she is well or not, and nobody ever has to find out which — and Holt says that is the whole instrument in a sentence and that nobody has said it in this basin before today. She certifies her out of the remaining days, out of the days and not out of the rule, and then names the price of it: the certificate goes to the clinic board as a cost, and a clinic's costs go on the same account as everything else, and **the district's answer to a cost is a rate.** She asks somebody to put the obvious question in an outsider's mouth and nobody does, and she says that is what she wanted. Marek asks Tamsin nothing and notices himself not asking. Fisk, from thirty feet: the eleven, every one of them drew seed from us, and in the ninth month every one is handed a number on a piece of paper and not one can tell you which clause it came from. **The boy of sixteen is on that list, and her mother's ground is on that list, and there is no box on it for her either.**

### Chapter 74 — Four Bells
The **eighth of the growth month**, second bell, a cart yard forty feet deep on the low-ground road with a forge-fire in a shed at the back that is not the drayman's. **Per Vahl**, thirty-eight, six years collecting pledged goods, has come for the cart and gear under the fifth box, which says any bell after the second. **Ondra Kelb is there to measure it**, with his own yard-wide rule, and measures with his left hand, which is nine years old in the sense that it does fine work at the age of nine and has none of the rest, and says: they will take the whole bed and not the wheels, because that is what the fifth box says, and he has not been asked to be fair. Marek is on the road with nothing in his hands and walks in because it is the one road in this city where everything happens in the open, and watches a drayman take the halter off the horse and stand with his hands at his sides, and **puts his hand flat on the sheet on the tail-board and holds the settlement for four bells.** He could not have held it longer, and that was not a decision either. **The first bell of the hold is the easiest and it is the moment the thing becomes real, because nothing at all happens:** the horse's water goes in, somebody else's fire goes on being somebody else's fire, a woman asks after a barrow and does not say thank you, a man from the forge says *o* and means it kindly, the ordinary rain of the growth month starts. There is a **vault book of marks on the second floor kept by a man called Osk, and there is no fee, because the tower never decided a person should be charged for being damaged**; the entry would be made by a man with a pen who does not know who it is for, in a shorthand that a clerk who has been hurt at work can read and a man who has not cannot, and **whatever it says, it does not say *four bells*.** Marek will not go and look at his own line, and the reason is not a rule. Duss uses the four bells and uses them well: four loads of quicklime up to the Ropewalk standpipe, a job four men had refused. At the sixth, Vahl takes the bed and the gear, gives him the gear, and says in the way of a man finishing a piece of business properly that four bells late is nothing and there is nothing in that paper about four bells. Kelb tells him four loads of lime went up the Ropewalk and that he does not know how he came to be stood in that yard and is not going to ask. Then: **between the second bell and the sixth the whole of the market eating Rainmarket went on eating it and not one single part of it noticed** — a boy of fourteen with a list for a queue that had not reformed, a woman asking to see a line in the shed with the seventh column bare at the fourth bell and bare at the sixth, the low ground's third quarter charged four miles off with nobody present. A hold delays perfectly. It is the best instrument in this city. And there was a moment in that yard when a delay was the only honest thing he had, he used it, it felt like nothing at all, and he did not once wish for it.

### Chapter 75 — The Under-Row
**The fourth line of the assignment schedule is not a household in Rainmarket.** It is forty-one acres on the middle terrace in the Red Uplands called **the Under-Row**, kept by **Josip Malnar**, forty-nine and fifty next spring, with a press, a lean-to of tarred board, two hundred old apple trees and eleven hundred bushels of windfalls going soft behind it. Kelb gave the address in a corridor with the book shut and said a man asked for a thing by a person with no office is a man who will be asked one day by a person with an office, and would do it anyway, and told Marek not to come back; Marek came back the same afternoon, because the road up is the same road up and it takes a whole day. Malnar asked about the price of lime because a question is cheaper than an explanation. He **bought a claim on the low ground's flood quarters off a house out of the Crown March road for four hundred and ten pounds** — his daughter's wedding money — and the man was honest with him: a piece of a thing, at a discount, and you may get a bit of it. **He has not read the schedule and says so in his own yard before he is asked, twice.** Marek does not go and get it, and tells him not to read it, and explains that a rate may be read at a counter and a copy of a rate may not be produced, that a claim is not a rate, and that there is no way for him to prove what he bought, and that the reason is a good one. He tells him the two things: the low ground's ratepayers have assigned their collection rights since and the paper has been sold out from under him lawfully, and he is a piece of a thing in a line of steps from five households in Rainmarket to somewhere Marek will not name. Malnar's settlement: **forty-one days of his own pruning to a factor out of the Crown March road, a crew of four in the last ten days of the growth month, the only ten days a year a graft goes in properly, due on the eleventh, and he read that page twice and the rest of it never.** Marek tells him in advance that he is going to try to hold a settlement for a few days, that Malnar will lose what he loses, that he will not know Marek was anywhere near it, and that he has decided Malnar is the person he will tell; and Malnar says: you will not get it back. **Then why are you telling me.** He is invited for the second of the month, a room over the press, a bench for a bed, boots, and told to be up before the light.

### Chapter 76 — Printed in Crown March
The **twelfth of the growth month**. **Marta Brel**, forty-six, has a rate of her own she can read at the counter, cannot read the paper, and signed where the man at the window put his finger. Her window shut on the sixth, she lost a fortnight, her husband's half of a seed order went into a neighbour's ground at a price that was not a price, and the advance came off the re-opened window on the twenty-first. It is one sheet: the **Trust's own seasonal advance form, four boxes and a fifth at the foot**, well made, every part filled in her husband's hand but the counter's, two weighbridge men as witnesses, and **in the fourth box, in type, in the middle of the form: the district named in the schedule bears the failure of the season favoured.** It is not a copy. A cooperative's clause is drafted by whoever drafts it; **this is stock, set by a printer, on a form used in every season office in the kingdom, in the fourth box, in the place clause four goes.** **Rustam Vey**, nineteen years at the window, reads it in eleven seconds and is not surprised: nineteen years on every one, about four hundred certified, a form number and a run number of eleven hundred and something, and no office in the kingdom knows what was done with eleven hundred copies of a form. He has certified the fourth clause, which is not the same as reading it, and has never in his life drafted an instrument, and has never once in nineteen years thought about the fourth clause — and the office has been printing it for nineteen years without asking the man who uses it. **Kelb has certified sixty of these forms and has put his mark beside sixty clauses he has never read**, and there is no gap in it, and *that is the finding*, and he has not been able to find out yet what he feels about it, which is a worse thing to say out loud than the other one. The letter goes at the sixth bell, first post, **addressed by name and not by office, one sheet, three questions, in the first person, signed with his name and *no office*:** is the fourth box the founding hand's clause or an addition, and whose and in what year; when you certify a seasonal advance, are you certifying that a counterweight named as a district is named correctly and to whom the district answers, and if not, what are you certifying; **is there any form in the Trust in which a person may enter a true sentence about a thing that is not a levy, a schedule, a posting or a return.** He is not asking her to answer it to him. Four hundred miles is four hundred miles and no version of this puts anything in a Morrowfen room inside a month.

### Chapter 77 — The Window in the Trees
The second to the eleventh of the growth month on the Under-Row, with borrowed boots and a bench over the press, and a frost that has left the terrace a soft brown mess by the eighth bell. **Halda Brnec**, thirty-nine, has worked the press since her mother stopped, is four days behind, and says so before she is asked: eleven days of her own hands pledged in the spring, due on the eleventh, **the season is twelve weeks and the press is the second half of it and the screw went in in the middle.** Malnar signed as surety in the spring and neither of them has read the schedule and both have been living on that since the seed month. The instrument is in a tin box in the front room: one sheet with **a schedule of fifty-two lines annexed, and it is the Share's assignment schedule, and the fourth line is the Under-Row, dated the fourteenth of the second month, MR 412, with a house in Crown March as the receiving party.** Nobody had lied to him; that is the part that stayed. What he bought was the security, it is unproducible, and it has been sold twice since he put his name to it, and **the correct sentence is that nobody had any duty and that was the whole of the design.** He could not tell him, and the reason he told him not to read the schedule ten days ago is that it is harmless to a man who is not a surety, and it is the same document. **On the seventh he goes down off the terrace to do Chapter 74's cart, because the road up is a day's road, and comes back up on the ninth. On the ninth he holds the settlement — eleven days, the pressing, the event on the morning of the eleventh — and holds it three days**, which is the most he has ever held and about a third of what he wanted, and the limit arrives without asking him anything. Nobody knew. Malnar grafted, and put his own graft in with his own hands on the sixth, and it took; **while Marek was down, two men came from the holding two miles down and then three, a cart came up with sacking and no fruit, and a boy's mother said the fruit would go to the animals, and Malnar told him all four of those things in ninety words on the evening of the ninth and editorialised on none of them**; and a Crown March factor's man came up on the morning of the eleventh and did not find anybody to sign for anything, and Marek watched him go down and could have stopped him and did not. On the eleventh the hold ended with no noise and no mark anywhere, and the days had to be found in a different year. **The chain walked in the ordinary way:** the borrower first, and could not pay, not because she would not but because the days were in a season that had gone; then the surety, off the ladder. **In front of a witness she says the obvious sentence herself:** the frost is a bad thing and is nobody's fault and **the frost did not do it** — the season is twelve weeks, the press is the second half, and she has been behind on the top since the ninth because the best fruit came down first and it is the fruit that pays the screw, and **nobody made her do that and there is no rule that made her do that.** What he bought was three days out of ten. What it cost was ten. **A hold delays perfectly and cannot touch one single legal consequence of a single thing that is any of this about:** the claim was gone on the twentieth of the seed month, the low ground's ratepayers assigned on the sixth, the schedule stayed annexed to the sheet, and **there is no form in the kingdom on which a man with no office enters that he has made a season late.** He got nothing, because there was nothing on sale. There would be two entries in the vault book, in a shorthand he cannot read, and he was not going to ask and was not going to write anything down, and there is no rule about any of that and it is all his own. Malnar comes down off the ladder and gives his own account of what happened, says he is aware of what he is saying, and asks Marek to go down the road and say nothing. He is welcome to stay the night.

### Chapter 78 — Forty of Forty-One
The **seventeenth of the growth month**, second bell, in the seed house, standing, because there are forty-one people in that building and eleven chairs and the chairs are for the old. Nobody called it; the question went up on a slate on the ninth and about four hundred people came to look at it over two days and none of them were members. The question is whether the Share shall write to each of the fifty-two who have signed, telling them in the words of the instrument what they signed, and asking them to do whatever they think is right about it — and it says neither *withdraw* nor *buy back* nor *the Compact* nor *repay*. **Bessok Tan**, twenty-nine, second-generation, secretary four years, keeps the roll of sixty-one lines, brings a string and a slate, and asks the only question in that shed that had not been asked in any other room in the basin: **whose hand is a hand** — are we counting households or people, does the instrument know the difference, and did it care. **Wenna Drel**, at the back by the door where she always stands: *have any of you read it.* Forty of them have read it in the last month, Fisk has read it four times and did not read it until the third of this month, and Fisk will not open the schedule in front of the girl and says so. The letter is **nineteen lines**, one paragraph, written by Bessok Tan, and nobody improved it. **Iren Orleb**, sixty-one, member since the founding, whose gate he cannot afford to mend, reads it back and votes against with a reason nobody in the shed could improve on: nothing on the face of the earth obliges one person in one household to answer one letter, and if a letter does not get an answer then in four years this shed is forty-one people with a piece of paper and no way at all of making anybody do anything. The count, taken standing: **one hand against, forty for, of forty-one in the room.** In charcoal on the side of a seed sack, so that anybody can pick it up and turn it round: forty, of forty-one in the room; twenty lines of the roll not in this shed; **twenty-one were not in this shed the last time this instrument was carried.** Both numbers, because one has gone down and one has gone up and Bessok Tan has no way of saying what that means. *Forty is not a majority. Forty is forty hands in a shed.* The fifty-two letters go out over two days, about four of them by **Ansgar Drel**, seventy-eight, in a hired basket and a boy with a cart, **into Merrow's shed on the nineteenth at the house's cost, to be entered in the register in the second hand** — a person who cannot find out whether a letter came for them is a person who cannot find out what they signed. **Ferrow says that is not approved**, and Merrow says he has known it was not approved since the twenty-second of last month and has decided it is worth a clerk's time. The shed agreed. Fifty-two letters nobody is obliged to answer, one number in charcoal on a sack, and a rival register doing a thing the Share could not have entered anywhere itself.

### Chapter 79 — Nobody Is Holding It
The **twentieth day of the growth month**, the last of it. **Wenna Drel**, sixteen, comes up the low-ground road at the second bell with the Share's letter in a flat board of her own making, with a crease through the word *cooperative*, and asks Marek to come and stand in a room and tells him not to have to say anything. She is asking for the room in the Red Uplands with the notice on the door, and she tells it in the wrong order, and the wrong order is that it is there because two of them were in it in the second week of this month. **The Threshold on Stillstep Island**: a hall with six intake windows and four rooms behind it, of which the third is the witness room, eight feet by fourteen, a table planed soft, a rail, a window with a wire in it, and a bell that is not rung for time. **Sabina Krela**, fifty-one, twenty-nine years, economical. Her terms, and she says them again slower to the girl: sealed at the front table and after the bell, witnesses on the rail and silent, anything on the record said after the bell and read back, **nothing in this room is sworn because there is no oath in this room**, and a person is not charged to come into it. The girl asks for it written down that she has been on a rate since she was born, that it is on her mother's ground, and that nobody has ever asked her whether she minds — **and the keeper writes nothing at all.** She can write that the girl said it, today, at the third bell, in her own words, in the day book, **which has no heading on it because it is not a heading; it is a list of what people came for.** She cannot write that the rate exists: **a room witnesses things that happen inside it**, and a rate entered in a shed four miles off in the seed month did not happen in this room, and if she wrote it down the girl would have a paper with a figure's worth of words on it and not one thing in it that anybody would have to answer to. Read back twice, the second slower, two corrections, both by *that is not what I said*, entered in the margin in a smaller hand and initialled. The bell is rung once, with a thumb. Then the girl asks **what it is called**, and Marek says it once, and says beforehand that it is not for writing down: a counterweight is a thing somebody is holding up; if the name is a person the person is in the room, and being named is the only way of being in the room, and being in the room is the only way anybody can refuse, and a refusal is what voids the instrument, and that is the design and it is a good one; **if the name is a district then the district is not in the room and cannot be asked or refuse or be told or traced to a door, so nobody is holding it up, and a thing nobody is holding does not come down** — it is not a weight, it is a thing up in the air with no hand on it, and it has been up since before this city had a hall. And the word the city has been using without noticing is **the charge**: the four words in the boxes are four words for a charge, a fifth has been going into a fifth box for eight years because there was nowhere else to put it, and **not one of the five is a word for a person.** He does not name the seventh column in the shed, and gives the reason out loud, and the reason is that there is no day on which anybody in this basin agreed to anything. *Then it can stay empty.* The letter is read out, nine minutes, in the words of the Share, by two men who came in to bond a debt to each other and stayed. She may have a copy of her own words out of the day book, free, on the second day of next month, **and it will not be worth anything in a room** — and the keeper cannot say whether that is a tragedy or the best that is available, and is looking forward to finding out. *It is for the person who said it.* On the causeway she asks which of the five is the right one and the answer is **both**: it goes in that box on the footing that it is a return and it does the work of a charge on a district, and no office has as its job to notice the two things in one box — and that is the truest thing he has said in six weeks, said to the one person in Morrowfen who cannot take it into a room. The seventh column on the low-ground road is still bare at the head of it, four people standing in front of it not knowing what they are looking at. On the third day of next month he is going to stand in a seed house yard and watch a rate go onto eleven pieces of paper with nothing at the head of them, and the ninth month is about a month off.

### Chapter 80 — Which Clause
The **third day of the harvest month**, cold and clear, four days of the best weather since the flood year and the worst possible weather for a second sowing; the kept-back went into nine hundred and forty acres on the nineteenth, the last day of the growth month having been the twentieth, off the forty-one sacks off the upland road, and it went in well and nobody was pleased. At the second bell in the seed house, standing, about thirty people, **eleven of the eleven present and the other nineteen not, on purpose, because the rule it settles is the Share's own rule.** **Mirren Fisk** does the thing she said in a yard on the twenty-first of the seed month that she would have to do. One sheet, cut off a seed sack, **nothing at the top of it**, eleven lines, each a household's ground and a figure, no rule at the head and no column names and no total at the foot, and all three absences are one reason: **there is no form in the kingdom in which a rate on a member's ground goes, and none for a member in illness, and none for a member who cannot work, and the absence is not an oversight, it is the rule working exactly as it was cut.** If she uses one of the four words — schedule, levy, return, posting — then in nine months somebody in a hall asks which one, and the day she says one of the four is the day a piece of sack has become a thing the Share is owed by somebody instead of a thing the Share did. She writes the date and nothing else for a minute and a half, then eleven lines, and **does not read them out.** Then: you did the days, and the days were work, and the days are not the whole of it, and the balance is a rate on your ground in your own name or your mother's; and **there is no box on a rate on which anybody is asked whether you are well** — if you are entirely well you are on it, and if you cannot get out of bed you are on it by the same figure, and nobody will ever know which you were. Marek stands with the flat board under his arm and there is nothing in the city he can do with it, and gives **four facts and no choice between them**: the shower that missed by four miles, the stock out of the shed, the correct restatement on the eighteenth and the fortnight four households lost, and the fourth clause naming the low ground. He cannot show anybody that the clause did it and has been failing at it since the twenty-fourth. And the rate on the sack is not the fourth clause; it is the Share's own rule, out of the Share's own book, and in the ninth month a man in a good coat will be right about half of it and it will be the half that is easier to say. **Fisk is glad he came and is not going to use his sentence, and gives the reason:** a word at the top would make it a thing of a kind, and a thing of a kind goes in a column in Bell Stair with a date against it, and the day somebody comes with a form for it is the day one of these eleven women is told something by an office about her own ground — the sentence does not need somewhere to go, it needs to stay in a keeper's day book where it cannot be used by anybody, including him. **Ilma Tresch** asks whether the figure will be on her own line at the water office counter, and it will not: the Share is not a district, this is not a district's rate, there is no roll in this city with her figure on it and no office that holds it, and nobody can produce a copy of a thing nobody has — and she says, to the middle of the room, that she is not asking him to do anything, and that her mother was one of the four that lost a fortnight and has never said a word to her about him, and that she is telling him what this is not. **Wenna Drel is not on the sheet**, and her mother says the ground is in her name and a rate is a charge on ground and a charge on ground does not know there is a girl of sixteen at the end of her family; Fisk refuses to put the girl's name on anything unless she is asked to by somebody who has to live in this valley after the ninth month. **Nobody asks her to do it.** And there are **eleven houses in the basin where the ground is in a name and there is nobody in the house**, which is not a fifth fact about the frost and is not the fourth clause and is not a rule in the Share's book. **Ovid Merrow** comes up the road and does not come past the step, because a member's meeting is a members' meeting and a man with a licence is not a member of anything. The register holds the fact of the fifty-two letters; about two in three of the households have been in to ask, and eight are not at the house the letter went to, and he will not say what that is a number of. **From that morning people have been asking him what a rate is**, which nobody has ever done in nine years, because nobody has ever asked him and there is no line on any roll in this basin that would tell a person where to ask, and the price of not knowing in his trade is that he buys a piece of paper instead of an answer. On the twenty-second he will open as usual and carry a line for each of the eleven households and, in the second hand, in the words the household used, what it has been told it owes. Nothing is charged. He is not being kind and says so; he is a man in a lane with a bench and a column with nothing at the head of it, and the only honest thing left for him is to be the second person in this basin who writes down what a person said when they were asked what they owed. In the lane, Marek looks at the low ground's line, which now carries a further line added on the sixth of the growth month in another hand, because a sum handed over to a receiving party is a returnable sum and a returnable sum is the fifth box, and it names a party in Crown March and a date and does not say where the thing went after that; and at the Tallow Gate line, a licensed carrier, forty-three and some months, *asked, and no answer given*, and nothing after it. **He knows all nine links and could not have said one word of it to a person, and the reason is not the ward and not loyalty and not a rule:** the next word after the low ground is a place, and the next word after that place is the end of it, and forty people would have both halves by the evening, and a ward is not a person and nobody can serve a notice on a yard, so a thing that goes into a roll that way goes in with nobody in the room and nobody able to refuse it. The sheet is not in the shed; it is in Fisk's own house, because a sheet with nothing at the head of it does not stay in a building. The Share's lamp has been cut after every line above it and is supplied in the third month, and **the cut has not come off the ground, because a cut is not a date, and in the ninth month the office will send somebody.** He ends lighter than he was in the seed month by exactly one thing: the belief that if he got the words right it would make a difference. He got the words right. It did not make a difference. No office is closed, no licence withdrawn and nobody is convicted, and the ninth month is just under four weeks off.
