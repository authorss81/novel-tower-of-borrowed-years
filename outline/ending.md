# Ending Outline

This is the author-level ending plan. It remains hidden from readers until the final volume, but the preceding volumes must plant every major image, rule, relationship, and cost required here.

## 1. Final external conflict

Edren Sable, the First Auditor, activates the hidden **First Account** beneath the Deep Engine. The Account contains the possible years created when the Last Convocation crossed backward from the Last Meridian. It is an unappealed founding claim under the Founder's Clause, not a lawful public loan. Edren intends to use it to close every divergent future and restore the lost world he remembers as the only survivable continuation.

The activation produces a citywide **year drought**. The effect is not a generic explosion:

- public records begin disagreeing about dates and identities;
- people who have arranged a future find that the arrangement is suddenly impossible;
- crops, injuries, pregnancies, repairs, and inherited skills develop visible year scars;
- the basin's pumps and flood locks lose reliable timing;
- people who borrow from the tower receive power but lose the future capacity that was supposed to repay it;
- no individual can tell whether a painful loss is a consequence of the drought or a choice made by a person caught inside it.

Edren argues that the collapse is proof that only one approved future can survive. He offers the current world a bargain: surrender the Open Charter, accept his authority, and he will restore the Last Meridian's stable history. The offer is real in one narrow sense—the First Account can stabilize a city if Edren is allowed to spend it—but it is false in its claim that the current people have consented. The account's power is invalid under the public witness rules because its sureties are absent and its founding consent cannot be renewed.

The final conflict is therefore both physical and civic. Marek must prevent Edren from forcing a single timeline, while the people of Morrowfen and the surrounding regions must decide whether to hold a shared present under conditions no prophecy can guarantee.

## 2. Final internal choice

Edren offers Marek the life the First Account has been keeping in reserve: a future in which Tomas never loses his vigor, Tamsin's old debt is harmless, Neris is never exposed to the political consequences of the tower, and Marek becomes the competent, universally trusted First Auditor. The offer also restores the missing crossing year exactly, which would make the tower's original sacrifice disappear from the record.

Marek refuses the offer. He releases the future self whose possible years sustain his Ledger Sight and uses the accumulated loss as part of the common settlement rather than a private reward. He then accepts the Open Charter's central condition: no living person's future may be used as collateral without that person's informed, revisable consent.

This is not a suicide plot and not a magical sacrifice of Marek's lifespan. It is an irreversible loss of control. After the climax he is alive, ordinary, and unable to read the future. He must trust other people with the system he helped build.

## 3. Relationship resolution: Marek and Neris

Their relationship reaches its final form through a choice made before the climax, not a last-minute declaration.

- They do not use their bond as a magical consent token. Each signs the Open Charter as an independent witness.
- They do not try to rescue one another from the First Account. They take separate responsibilities and remain able to disagree.
- Neris refuses any version of the Open Charter that would give Marek special authority. She also refuses a version that removes his ability to participate.
- Marek tells her the full cost of releasing the First Account before asking for her help. She chooses to help after hearing it, not before.
- Their shared life is practical and intimate: a workroom in Hallow Ground with a small seed counter, separate desks, shared meals, and an agreement to tell one another when fear is being mistaken for authority.

The romance remains a slow burn and does not resolve every disagreement. It ends with chosen companionship in a world that no longer promises a guaranteed future.

## 4. What happens to the major characters

- **Marek Vale:** loses Ledger Sight and all direct First Account permissions; survives as a clerk, teacher, and witness organizer.
- **Neris Kade:** keeps her practical sensitivity to year scars and becomes one of the first public field examiners under the Open Yearspire.
- **Tomas Vale:** loses the artificial vigor that kept him working beyond his natural body. He lives a shorter, less privileged life and takes up bridge inspection and teaching.
- **Lio Vale:** remains in Morrowfen and is free to choose her future without an inherited debt attached to her name.
- **Sera Holt:** works with the new medical reserve and publicly accounts for the clinic's old loan. She does not receive a perfect absolution.
- **Nim Rook:** becomes a public records courier and helps maintain the Unwritten Quarter's archive. Ilen is never resurrected; Nim receives a verified final message and a legal history that acknowledges the loss.
- **Ilya Senn:** remains a Lantern Warden, maintaining the engine's ordinary pumps and locks. He refuses a ceremonial post so that the work is not made into a myth about Marek.
- **Hesper Rann:** gives testimony, faces trial, and spends part of the aftermath helping identify false-witnessed contracts. His early lie remains part of the public record.
- **Lyra Dain:** lives as a protected political exile, allowed to argue for a single future only so long as she cannot compel a settlement. The Choir's surviving members receive a place in the public archive rather than an automatic pardon.
- **Edren Sable:** loses access to the First Account when Marek releases it. He survives in a public custodial ward, is tried for the harms caused by the tower and the Choir, and cannot be believed without independent witnesses. He is not killed because killing him would make the institution's final authority a martyr's legend.
- **The Nine founders:** their records, names, and culpability are restored. Their relief motive is understood, but their authority is not inherited by the present.
- **Tamsin Vale:** releases the old family loan record, testifies about the childhood mark, and resumes ordinary seamstress work without making Marek's choices into a family legend.
- **Pella Rusk:** keeps a public school that teaches yearline literacy and contract review without turning children into miniature clerks.
- **Marda Senn:** returns from the Hollow Galleries with the partial map and joins the public archive as a maintenance historian.
- **Councilor Bel Harrow:** serves on the rotating oversight board and remains willing to compromise only when the compromise is written and reviewable.
- **Sabin Rell:** accepts the censure recorded in Volume 3 and works under regional oversight, never returning to a role that can hide a granary pledge.

## 5. Institutional aftermath

The final settlement changes the entire chain that made the crisis possible:

- **Ovid Merrow** is prosecuted for fraudulent bundling and loses the Sable Compact's lending monopoly. The Compact is broken into licensed local mutuals with public risk ledgers.
- **Chancellor Ysara Venn** accepts a permanent censure and regional oversight. She may help coordinate a public emergency reserve, but cannot suspend appeals or appoint a sole settlement officer.
- **Seren Calder** surrenders her marshal command, testifies against the Crown's coercion, and faces a sentence that includes restitution and witness work.
- **The Yearspire Trust** is dissolved. Its physical assets become public infrastructure, while senior administrators remain individually accountable for contracts they certified.
- **The Crown of Continuation** loses legal authority. Its military and administrative members are judged for the harms they caused rather than handed a clean institutional rebirth.
- **The Chamber of Accounts** becomes the first appeals body of the Open Yearspire, with rotating citizen review and a public duty to publish dissent.
- **The Hollow Court** becomes a registered mutual-aid network. Cells that used counterfeit marks or spread debts to uninformed people face trials; cells that sheltered and documented victims are not criminalized for surviving.
- **The Saltglass Harbor Council** adopts a shared translation standard and loses its monopoly over Tide Reckoning marks.
- **Oren Pike**, physically alive after being left unwritten, chooses a new legal name and works as a grain witness. The ending does not pretend that his lost legal history has been restored.

## 6. The transformation of the Yearspire

The final charter is called the **Open Account**. It changes the tower's source of power in four concrete ways.

1. **No hidden extraction:** the Deep Engine can no longer draw possible years from an unborn, absent, or unchosen future. A living person may still make a narrow future pledge in person, with independent witness and no silent collateral.
2. **Voluntary present capacity:** people may offer skill, labor, care, or a portion of present recovery through independent witnesses. Contribution is revocable before sealing and never creates a lifetime claim.
3. **Distributed authority:** the tower is governed by a rotating public council and local witness rooms. No single First Auditor can approve a settlement alone.
4. **Permanent record:** all contracts, amendments, refusals, settlements, and historical injuries remain public. A person can be compensated or recognized later without a hidden future being quietly added to their bill.

The Yearspire still offers controlled vigor, craft, sequence, and record effects in emergencies, funded by present contributions or by a living person's narrow, witnessed future pledge. It draws no claim from an absent, unborn, or unchosen future and no claim from the absent founders. It is a public utility and an archive, not a bank of lives.

The four forms also change how they pay, and this is where the old system's ordinary cruelty is corrected rather than merely prohibited:

- **Vigor and craft** costs are the borrower's own yearline, pledged narrowly and in person. A clinic can no longer put a doctor's future recovery behind a ward's heating.
- A **sequence** loan's counterweight must be named to the people it will fall on and accepted by them as a group, withdrawable before sealing. If nobody accepts it, the tower records a shortfall and the public reserve pays. No stranger's harvest is the price of somebody else's storm again.
- A **record** loan's contractual cost falls on the borrower's own yearline and then the reserve. A witness can no longer be made to forget, and a family can no longer have a legal tie sold out from under them by someone else's filing. The human damage a changed record does to other people is still caused, still recorded, and still someone to answer for—it just can no longer be pledged away by a third party who was never asked.

A sequence loan, a record loan, and a narrow future pledge all remain available. What no longer exists is a category where a stranger's future is the collateral and the stranger is not in the room.

The transition takes several years. For the first transition period, people who already hold extraordinary borrowed effects may keep a limited version until they settle. The effects fade; the people do not. No one is forcibly aged to balance the old First Account. Restitution comes from seized Trust assets, public funds, labor, and pooled voluntary contributions.

## 7. Final-volume movement plan (Chapters 851–900)

### Movement A — The Unsealed Tower (851–862)

The Deep Engine's locks fail, and the first year-drought signs appear in Bell Stair and the flood gates. Edren broadcasts a version of the public charter that promises stability under his sole authority. Marek and the team must secure the tower without turning the emergency into a new one-person rule.

The first local victory is a network of pumps and witness rooms, not a battle. It costs the team the ability to keep one district's records intact; that district's people choose a public loss rather than a hidden transfer. Lyra Dain's returnists are divided between helping with maintenance and trying to close the engine.

**Movement turn:** the tower reveals that the missing crossing year is not a reserve waiting to be used once; it is the hinge on which several currently living communities depend. Restoring it would not simply restore the founders—it would displace the present. Volume 15 already established the mechanism and the price of restoration; what is new here is whose life the price falls on. Nothing in this turn revises the earlier finding, and Marek must say so out loud, because the temptation in this room is to treat a new fact as a new authority.

### Movement B — The Gathering of Witnesses (863–876)

Marek travels through the Red Uplands, Saltglass Coast, Crown March, Hollow North, and Quiet North with small teams rather than a conquering army. Each community must decide whether to join the Open Charter, negotiate a different local form, or refuse to participate.

The political work is practical:

- the upland seed-keepers establish a labor-and-skill exchange;
- the ports create a multi-calendar witness procedure;
- the Hallow Ground residents restore their names without claiming years they never received;
- the returnists publish their own records and accept that their future is not the sole legitimate one;
- the Lantern Wardens hold the physical infrastructure while the council debates authority.

Marek and Neris disagree over whether the returnists deserve equal vote. They resolve the question by making participation a right of the people who will bear the cost, not a reward for historical purity. The relationship is strengthened by the refusal to hide this conflict.

**Movement turn:** the independent witnesses agree that Edren's authority is not merely dangerous; it is structurally dependent on the First Account's claim that one future is the only valid settlement. His power is real but not absolute.

### Movement C — The Open Account (877–888)

Edren activates the Account. The city experiences the full year drought described in the final external conflict. The team executes the distributed response:

- Ilya and the Wardens keep physical systems moving.
- Sera and the clinics establish present-capacity medical reserves.
- Tomas and the bridge crews maintain evacuation routes without borrowed future victory.
- Nim and the Unwritten children record every person who is being misidentified.
- Hesper testifies to the false-witness chain.
- Lyra's returnists deliberately release their claim to the remembered Last Meridian, accepting that their lost world cannot be used as a shortcut.
- Neris coordinates independent witness teams and refuses to let Marek centralize the process.
- Marek uses Charter Weaver to link local compacts, accepting liability for the links rather than pretending that coordination is neutral.

Edren offers each group a private favorable outcome if it defects. Some accept temporary relief. The charter permits that help; it does not permit anyone to take an unwitnessed future claim.

**Movement turn:** the Account's power begins to fail, but the tower's physical locks are still tied to a single central control. Someone must enter the engine and separate the old settlement authority from the new public one.

### Movement D — The Release of the Absent Claim (889–896)

“Release” here has exactly one meaning: the founding claim is voided and its entry is returned to the public record, so that the missing year is disclosed as a debt owed to no one and payable to no one. It is not recovery. Nobody in this movement gets the lost world back, and the language of getting it back is the argument Edren expects to win.

Marek, Neris, Ilya, and a small witness group descend to the Deep Engine with the authenticated sealed founding copy. Edren explains that the founders' rescue was genuinely necessary and that the current world will collapse without a managed future. His argument is not a lie: the First Account has helped prevent several catastrophes.

Marek discovers the final mechanical and moral fact: the engine can run on present contributions, but only if neither an absent founder's future nor a living person's unchosen future is used as the anchor. The crossing year is the anchor because it is the one future the founders erased to make the current history possible. It cannot be recovered without a replacement history.

**Building the quorum (a real scene, not a line of bookkeeping).** The finding needs three independent witness rooms, and the rooms must be assembled inside the engine while the drought is still deepening. Give the movement three concrete beats:

- **The first attempt fails on paper.** The three rooms nearest the tower all draw their members' stipends from the Yearspire Trust, which Edren's side points out in the first hour. Independence is not a matter of opinion, and Neris is the one who spots the shared payroll in time to stop the finding from being recorded and destroyed by its own objectors.
- **The challenge window costs days.** The copy was authenticated in Volume 15, so nothing here turns on opening it again; what the nine days govern is who is allowed to sit. Three untied rooms are seated — one from a Rainmarket grain cooperative, one from a Saltglass harbor notarial guild, one from a Hallow Ground tenants' assembly — and the moment they are seated, Edren and the administrators who certified the founding records get nine days to object, extendable once on a filed objection. The delay is the cost of doing this properly: pumps must be hand-mended, a courier's satchel of hand-copies goes into the flood and has to be rewritten by lamplight, and Neris uses the days to make a room that can survive being cross-examined. One room is still dismissed mid-window for a surety tie its own member discloses, and the replacement it seats from a Rainmarket district with no other tie to the fund is what proves the procedure is real rather than decorative.
- **The finding is recorded by the rooms, not by Marek.** Each room states the finding in its own words in its own record before anyone sees the others'. Marek has no power to overrule it, and the shape of the scene is that he stands there while three people he did not appoint end the oldest claim in the building—the third of them seated only because an objection had cost the first choice its room.

Edren offers Marek the perfect life. Marek tells him and Neris the entire cost, then releases the future self behind his Ledger Sight. The three rooms record that the Founder's Clause has no living consent chain; the first settlement of the old Account is paid by voiding the clause and returning its claim to the public record rather than transferring it to a new chosen life.

Marek uses the Open Account to turn the engine's output into a public, expiring resource. The tower's old year-marks fracture, not because the building explodes, but because the lines no longer have private owners. Edren is stripped of the First Auditor's authority.

**Movement turn:** the physical engine is stable, but the current world must now live with the consequences. The final emotional work is not stopping the countdown; it is helping people understand what they are choosing.

### Movement E — The Year That Is Not Ours (897–900)

The last chapters are aftermath and resolution rather than a new threat. The last two of them open the dated coda in §8, which carries the transition years MR 435–438 and closes on the final image in §11.

- A public assembly reads the names of the dead, the missing, and the people who survived with permanent scars.
- The new charter records the First Account's history, including the founders' responsibility and the cost of the crossing.
- Existing extraordinary powers expire one by one under public terms; the people who used them are not shamed for needing them.
- Tomas signs a settlement that ends his artificial vigor and chooses a shorter future with his daughter.
- Marek and Neris sign the first household agreement as equal witnesses, not as a magical marriage contract.
- Edren is taken into public custody. The Last Auditor's title is abolished, and the records remain open.
- The Yearspire begins accepting voluntary present contributions and narrow living-person future pledges, with local appeals and a rotating council.

The final chapter ends on a practical new day, not a prophecy. The tower's doors open; a child can read the first entry without being asked to believe it; a worker can offer an hour of practiced skill and knows when the hour ends; and nobody receives a bill from a future they did not choose.

## 8. Epilogue plan: MR 435–438

The series spans eighteen volumes, and most of the character fates in §4 and the institutional fates in §5 are processes rather than announcements. Trials, dissolutions, restitutions, the expiry of borrowed effects, the first years of the rotating council, and the ordinary work of living with a public system are carried in the four years after the charter is sealed, and they must be written, not summarized in a speech. This section owns them.

**How it is carried.** Movement E is immediate aftermath in MR 435. The coda is Volume 18's last two chapters (899–900) written as dated fragments, one date per scene, moving forward from MR 435 to MR 438 and closing on the final image in §11. Two chapters is enough only if each fragment is a short scene of four hundred to seven hundred words under its own date line, and it fails if it becomes a summary with a date stamped on every paragraph. If the Volume 18 outline needs the coda to breathe, it may give Volume 18 more chapters than fifty; the 851–900 range is a plan, not a wall, and the epilogue takes the room before anything else does. The coda is not a victory lap. It is the part of the story that shows what the new institution does on an ordinary bad Tuesday.

**MR 435, first months.** The Trust's physical assets are inventoried and the public reserve is seeded, smaller than anyone promised. The first rotating council is seated, and its membership is an argument everybody can check. The first restitution payments go out slowly, and the Threshold keeps a queue of people who only want to know what the tower still owes them. Sera's clinic publishes its old loan and its reserve figures in full, including the part that does not balance. Tamsin releases the old family loan record and testifies about Marek's childhood mark in the same week, in the same flat tone, and goes back to seamstress work. Pella Rusk's school becomes the first to publish its yearline literacy materials, including the ones that say a district can be wrong.

**MR 435, third quarter.** Hesper's trial ends on the false-witnessed Tallow Ward entry. He is convicted, his early lie stays in the public record, and his sentence includes the restitution work he asks for. Edren's first custody review confirms that he cannot be believed without independent witnesses, and the trial for the harms done to the basin through the tower and the Choir is set rather than promised. Lyra's protective exile is formalized: she may argue for a single future in a public forum and may not compel a settlement. The surviving Quiet Choir cells are offered a place in the public archive on terms that are not a pardon, and some of them take it. Ilya declines the ceremonial post offered him and goes back to the pumps.

**MR 436.** The dissolutions happen as paperwork and as fights. The Trust is dissolved and its administrators are individually accounted for the contracts they certified. Ovid Merrow is prosecuted for fraudulent bundling, and the Sable Compact is broken into licensed local mutuals with public risk ledgers. The Crown of Continuation loses legal authority and its members are judged for harm. Ysara Venn accepts a permanent censure and regional oversight without ever being granted the emergency power back. Seren Calder surrenders her command, testifies against the Crown's coercion, and receives a sentence of restitution and witness work. The Saltglass Harbor Council loses its monopoly over Tide Reckoning marks and adopts a shared translation standard with the other ports, which means the first contested translation is now a matter of public record rather than private judgement. The Hollow Court registers as a mutual-aid network, and its cells that used counterfeit marks or spread debts to uninformed people are tried while the cells that sheltered and documented victims are not criminalized for surviving. The Chamber becomes the first appeals body of the Open Yearspire, with rotating citizen review, and publishes a real dissent in its first month, which the council is then obliged to answer in public. The reserve's first annual report is published in the same year and admits that it was insufficient in two districts.

**MR 436–437.** Tomas's artificial vigor finishes expiring. He is shorter-lived, unbothered by it, and takes bridge inspection and teaching; Lio, now an adult with no inherited debt attached to her name, chooses work that is not the family's. Their scene is ordinary on purpose. Sabin Rell works out the last of his censure under regional oversight, teaching grain inspection to people who will one day audit him, and never asks for the title back. Neris is appointed one of the first public field examiners under the Open Yearspire and keeps the practical sensitivity to year scars that she started with; her first published finding contradicts a district's own account of a scar, and she publishes the correction with her name on it.

**MR 437.** Nim receives Ilen's verified final message: not a voice and not a resurrection, but an authenticated statement with a date, which establishes Ilen's legal history and says plainly that she is dead. Nim becomes a public records courier and helps bring the Unwritten Quarter's archive under public keeping on the Quarter's own terms. Marda Senn returns from the Hollow Galleries with the partial map and joins that archive as a maintenance historian. The nine founders' names are restored in the public record as responsible parties, and their relief motive is understood without their authority being inherited by anyone.

**MR 437–438.** Oren Pike files his new legal name and works his first season as a grain witness. The Unwritten children's notebooks are copied into the public roll on terms the children themselves set. Bel Harrow serves on the rotating oversight board and compromises in writing, reviewably. Marek teaches a term of yearline literacy in Pella's school two mornings a week, keeps a desk as a witness organizer, and still copies public records by hand because he cannot stop doing it. He and Neris move into the Hallow Ground workroom with the seed counter, keep separate desks, and argue about a draft charter clause in the middle of moving furniture.

**MR 438.** The council completes its first full rotation of seats, which means the person who answered the Chamber's dissent in MR 436 has been replaced by somebody who disagreed with the answer. A sequence loan is sealed under the new rule in a village that has learned the hard way: the counterweight is named to the people who will bear it, accepted by them as a group, and still withdrawable, and one household withdraws at the last moment and the contract survives without them. The rule is demonstrated on a small real case instead of declared in a speech.

## 9. Fully answered mysteries

- The tower was built by the Last Convocation, survivors of the Last Meridian.
- The missing first entry is the crossing year, sacrificed to stabilize their arrival.
- The First Account is a reserve of possible years created from their lost future, not a literal collection of unborn souls.
- The First Account's authority comes from the unappealed Founder's Clause, whose absent founders cannot renew consent; this is why the original system is both powerful and unlawful.
- Edren Sable is the copied and renewed custodian of that reserve, not a new enemy from outside the established system.
- The tower's ability to alter later history is caused by real future consequences being pulled into present effects; it is not prophecy or random time travel.
- Restoring the crossing year exactly would overwrite current lives, so the lost future cannot be recovered without a present-day choice to replace it.
- The Tower can be transformed because distributed, voluntary present capacity is a real alternative to hidden future extraction.

## 10. Intentionally open questions

These questions may remain as atmosphere and future work, but they must not overturn the central resolution:

- How did the natural Yearwell first form, and are there comparable wells elsewhere?
- Do the Last Meridian's unreachable futures exist as physical places, or only as records and inherited possibilities?
- Can a few isolated year echoes be recovered without reopening the First Account? The answer is not yet known, and the story does not need it.
- What becomes of every individual erased name? The final world can preserve their history and relationships without claiming that history is the same as resurrection.

## 11. Final image

At dusk on the day the sealed foundation archive is unsealed — the last locked room in the tower, at the close of the transition years — the red year-marks disappear from the foundation and ordinary maintained fire glows behind the lower windows. A line of residents carries lamps up the public causeway, not to summon a collection but to witness the opening of the first shared archive. Marek is inside at a plain desk, correcting a public copy and listening to someone decide whether a freely offered hour of skill is enough. Above the desk, the oldest ledger lies open. The first entry is no longer hidden.

The world is not saved from time. It has only agreed to stop pretending that one person's future belongs to everyone else.
