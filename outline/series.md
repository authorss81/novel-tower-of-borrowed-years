# Series Outline: The Tower of Borrowed Years

## Premise

When a flood forces the people of Morrowfen to borrow years from their own future, a meticulous settlement clerk discovers that the city's celebrated tower was built by refugees from an erased future—and that every convenient loan is quietly making the next century pay for the last one.

Marek Vale wants to save his brother and keep the family account clear. He learns that a clean ledger is not the same as a fair world. Over eighteen volumes he moves from copying entries to auditing institutions, negotiating across regions, and finally helping transform the Yearspire into a public system that cannot spend an unborn future without informed consent.

## Protagonist

- **External want:** clear Tomas Vale's flood-repair loan and prevent the Yearspire from treating the family as collateral.
- **Internal need:** share authority and accept uncertainty instead of hiding behind procedure or trying to become the sole person who pays.
- **Central flaw:** Marek turns relationships into contracts, hoards information, and delays decisions until he can prove a safe answer.
- **Fear:** becoming the official who decides whose future is acceptable to spend.
- **Final change:** he gives up the perfect future offered by the First Account, releases the future self that sustains his ledger ability, and lives as an ordinary citizen inside a system that is accountable but not guaranteed.
- **Final emotional answer:** a life is worth protecting precisely because it is not guaranteed; responsibility is meaningful only when the person bearing the cost can refuse it.

## Relationship policy

Marek has one slow-burn romantic relationship with **Neris Kade**, an independent field examiner. There is no harem and no competing romantic partner. Their relationship develops through professional opposition, field work, disclosure, a breach of trust, repair, and a final decision to share risks without making either person a guarantee for the other.

## Central mystery

### Reader-facing question

Why does every Yearspire ledger begin with the same missing year, and why does the tower’s oldest reserve contain consequences from a future that no longer exists?

### Author answer

The Last Convocation escaped backward from **the Last Meridian**, a future civilization that had exhausted its plausible futures through an earlier and more destructive form of year-loaning. The nine survivors used their own lost future as collateral to cross into the present and stabilize their arrival. That reserve became the **First Account**.

The crossing year—the year they gave up in order to arrive—is the missing entry repeated at the beginning of every ledger. The tower's public promises are built on that hidden cost. The reserve is not a collection of unborn people waiting to be rescued; restoring the crossing year exactly would overwrite the present lives and relationships that grew because of the altered history.

Edren Sable, the Last Meridian's accounting officer, was copied into the tower's continuity and became the First Auditor. After centuries of renewals, he concluded that only one approved future can prevent another collapse. He is the final antagonist, seeded from the first volume through the signature **A-0** and revealed fully in the middle and final volumes.

### Mystery reveal ladder

- **Volume 1:** a blank first entry and a missing ward on an official map.
- **Volumes 2–4:** loans can be traded, forged, and made to attach to institutions; the missing entry is not a simple clerical error.
- **Volumes 5–7:** living “unwritten” people and the Hollow North suggest that the tower has removed more than names.
- **Volume 8:** survivors claim descent from a future that no longer exists; the Last Meridian is named.
- **Volumes 9–11:** inherited craft and the Quiet Choir show that the founders' rescue is being treated as a mandate. Volume 11 confirms the Last Convocation and Edren's copied authority, but leaves the crossing year unidentified.
- **Volumes 12–15:** the First Account and Founder's Clause are formally identified in Volume 13, which also names the crossing-year entry as the oldest debt on the books. Volume 15 owns the crossing year's *mechanism*: why that entry anchors the tower and the exact price of restoring it. It does not yet say who is standing on the anchor.
- **Volumes 16–18:** the answer is tested in public. Volume 18's Movement A owns the crossing year's *human cost* — the living communities whose continuity is keyed to the altered history — and the climax ends the claim by disclosure rather than recovery. The mystery is fully resolved in the final volume; the origin of the natural Yearwell and the fate of a few isolated erased echoes remain intentionally open.

## Ending in brief

Edren activates the First Account to close every future except one safe continuation. Marek refuses both Edren's perfect history and the usual response of destroying the tower. He gathers independent witnesses across the basin, uses the **Open Account** charter to release the absent founding claim and return the crossing year to the public record as a disclosed debt rather than a recovered world, and turns the Yearspire into a public institution that funds ordinary effects through present contributions or narrow, living-person pledges while refusing hidden extraction.

The cost is real: extraordinary borrowed powers fade, Tomas loses his artificial vigor and lives a shorter ordinary life, the First Auditor is stripped of his authority and publicly accountable, the dead and the historically erased are not resurrected, and Marek loses all Ledger Sight. The world is safer but not healed. The victory is that no individual or institution can quietly spend a future without making the cost visible and contestable.

## Antagonist ladder

The ladder is a sequence of pressures and compromised institutions, not a parade of unrelated monsters.

1. **Hesper Rann, senior clerk:** falsifies a Tallow Ward entry to save a floodgate. He represents the first dangerous idea—that one good reason can make a hidden cost acceptable.
2. **Ovid Merrow and the Sable Compact:** turn emergency credit into a tradable market and make risk disappear into complicated paperwork.
3. **Chancellor Ysara Venn:** centralizes emergency power after seeing how quickly appeals can fail. She saves regions while making dissent legally inconvenient.
4. **Seren Calder, settlement marshal:** enforces emergency settlements and treats refusal as a public danger. She is the face of “the river does not recognize consent.”
5. **The Hollow Court's hard cells:** use counterfeit marks, sabotage, and unauthorized returns. They are necessary allies and a warning that resistance can reproduce the same coercion.
6. **Lyra Dain and the Quiet Choir:** want to restore the Last Meridian by closing divergent futures. They turn grief into a mandate to control the present.
7. **The Crown of Continuation:** combines the Trust, lenders, military officials, and Choir cells into a political project for one approved timeline.
8. **Edren Sable, the First Auditor:** the final enemy. He is not a new threat discovered late; his A-0 signature, copied authority, and continuity are present from the first volume. His final conflict is with the people he claims to be saving.

## Power stages and costs

Marek's growth is permission and practice, not a set of numerical ranks.

1. **Ledger Sight:** read visible entries and immediate contradictions. Cost: headaches, tunnel vision, and temporary memory gaps.
2. **Boundary Hold:** delay one settlement or hold one boundary briefly. Cost: the delay is stored in his own yearline, and when the hold ends the contract's chain collects from the borrower, the surety, or the living clerk who certified a false-witnessed entry. A Hold adds nobody to that chain, so he carries the toll of the delay and never the debt. He cannot aim it.
3. **Season Hand:** direct an already-authorized effect to a named target within its scope. Cost: incompatible targets attach the debt to him; consent remains necessary.
4. **Returnpath:** split a claim among consenting holders and send part of it to a public pool. Cost: he carries other people's echoes and loses privacy.
5. **Branch Auditor:** compare several plausible continuations using evidence. Cost: contradictory memory and the danger of mistaking a plausible outcome for a fact.
6. **Charter Weaver:** build a multi-party compact that pools present surplus. Cost: he becomes liable for the compact and can no longer hide his own future claims.
7. **Open Account:** anchor a city-scale present effect through independent voluntary witnesses. Cost: he releases his future self, loses Ledger Sight, and accepts an unreadable life.

## Relationship milestones

- **Volume 1:** Marek and Neris clash over a public settlement; they respect each other's competence without liking each other.
- **Volume 4:** forced fieldwork gives them a shared practical method and the first honest disagreement about who should bear a risk.
- **Volume 8:** the Hollow North expedition produces mutual disclosure and a private intimacy that neither mistakes for a solution.
- **Volume 12:** Marek withholds a record that could clear him of an old audit error but expose Tomas's original claim and endanger Neris's district; the relationship breaks over secrecy, not lack of love.
- **Volume 14:** after a failed attempt to protect one another, they choose a public, equal partnership and stop treating love as permission to overrule the other.
- **Volume 16:** they sign as separate witnesses to the four districts' provisional year commons, making consent structural rather than romantic. They sign again, for the Open Charter itself, in Volume 18.
- **Volume 18:** they choose a shared life after the tower no longer promises a future. The relationship survives because neither person has to be saved first.

## Volume progression

Each volume is approximately fifty chapters and resolves a local promise before opening a larger question.

### Volume 1 — The Unpaid Ward (Chapters 1–50)

**Central pressure:** a flood-season water shortage forces Tallow Ward to settle a loan that will draw future recovery from several residents and leave Tomas's bridge debt unresolved.

**Starting relationships:** Marek distrusts Neris; Marek believes his brother will accept any reasonable accounting solution; Sera is a distant mentor; Ilya is the unknown Warden who has kept Tallow Ward's valves open against orders.

**Major pressure:** Hesper Rann falsifies an entry to save the district, and the hidden cost appears in a ward missing from the tower map. The oldest surviving copy bears the signature **A-0**, which the Trust forbids Marek from deleting.

**Power change:** Ledger Sight appears. Marek then attempts an unauthorized founding standing hold on a settlement he already knows is false, holding it six bells while he looks for a lawful alternative. It is not a practice: the entry is false-witnessed, the ward's fund cannot pay, and no surety stands behind it, so when the hold ends the chain walks to the last living name on the contract—the under-clerk who countersigned it in good faith. The under-clerk is injured. Marek learns the first narrow use of Boundary Hold in Chapter 8, in Chapter 10 learns in his body that he cannot aim it, and understands only by the end of the volume that the delay is his and the collection was never his to redirect.

**Resolution:** Marek exposes the falsified entry, forces a public emergency appeals board, converts the scheduled settlement of Tomas's loan into supervised road work, and brings Ilya's valve sabotage into the record. The ward is saved for this season; the old debt is not erased.

**Next question:** who owns the blank first entry in the oldest ledger, and what does A-0 mean?

### Volume 2 — The Sable Market (Chapters 51–100)

**Central pressure:** the Sable Compact buys distressed Rainmarket claims and turns a cooperative seed loan into a tradable debt package.

**Starting relationships:** Hesper is arrested but offers evidence; Neris distrusts Marek's institutional access.

**Major pressure:** Ovid Merrow makes a technically lawful offer the only affordable option for several families.

**Power change:** Marek learns to delay a settlement through Boundary Hold but cannot stop the market's legal consequences.

**Resolution:** Marek, Neris, and Oren Pike expose the repackaging scheme and create a public register of risk. Ovid loses his monopoly, not his wealth.

**Next question:** why do counterfeit year-marks work in a different calendar?

### Volume 3 — The Borrowed Harvest (Chapters 101–150)

**Central pressure:** Red Uplands grain and seed contracts fail across several districts after a sequence loan redirects a favorable harvest.

**Starting relationships:** Ysara Venn treats Chamber officials as emergency tools; Tomas refuses Marek's attempt to quiet the case.

**Major pressure:** Sabin Rell, a Crown March granary official, signs Ovid's underwriting clause; a future famine is being used as collateral while the present harvest appears abundant.

**Power change:** Marek gains Season Hand and learns that directing a benefit to the “right” target can worsen the underlying debt.

**Resolution:** the upland communities pool seed, labor, and public reserve in a negotiated settlement. The present harvest is secured, and the cost becomes visible rather than assigned to the poorest farms.

**Next question:** why are signatures being copied across unrelated contracts?

### Volume 4 — The City of Witnesses (Chapters 151–200)

**Central pressure:** false-witness cases flood the Witness Rooms after a record loan makes a coerced signature look valid.

**Starting relationships:** Marek and Neris are forced into joint fieldwork; Nim Rook becomes an unlikely source.

**Major pressure:** Seren Calder argues that refusal during a public emergency should be treated as endangerment.

**Power change:** Season Hand becomes reliable only when Marek listens to the recipient's actual need; the first sustained failure costs a witness their testimony.

**Resolution:** the Chamber establishes independent witness rotations and voids the most dangerous forged contracts. Nim learns that her sister appears in a legal copy with no living counterpart.

**Next-question:** why does the Tower deny that Hallow Ground, the locally known Unwritten Quarter, exists on its map?

### Volume 5 — The Unwritten Quarter (Chapters 201–250)

**Central pressure:** Hallow Ground residents begin losing work, rations, and legal protection as a citywide settlement searches for people who have already been removed from the rolls.

**Starting relationships:** Marek is distrusted by the Hollow Court; Neris has family ties to the quarter.

**Major pressure:** a local Hollow Court cell wants to reverse every mark by spreading the debt to outsiders.

**Power change:** Returnpath lets Marek split a live claim among consenting holders, but only after he accepts an obligation from Nim's missing-sister case.

**Resolution:** the quarter receives civic recognition, a living enclave is recovered, and a hard cell is brought into the public record. The people who were removed cannot all have their lost years returned.

**Next question:** what do foreign ports know about the same First Account?

### Volume 6 — The Coast of Other Ledgers (Chapters 251–300)

**Central pressure:** Saltglass lenders use a different calendar to evade Tower rules, and a trade blockade turns ordinary shipping into a settlement weapon.

**Starting relationships:** Ilya Senn distrusts foreign notaries; Marek begins to see that institutional rules are built around local habits.

**Major pressure:** the Compact and the **Saltglass Harbor Council** both profit from making translation errors look like borrower fraud.

**Power change:** Marek practices Returnpath across jurisdictions and learns that consent cannot be assumed from a shared alphabet.

**Resolution:** the ports sign a Tide Reckoning translation and inspection compact; trade resumes under public liability. A recovered founder seal contains a date from before the official Yearspire calendar.

**Next question:** why is a future season arriving in the wrong order?

### Volume 7 — The Year of Two Winters (Chapters 301–350)

**Central pressure:** a forced sequence loan produces two incompatible seasons in the Crown March, displacing refugees and making every food calculation unreliable.

**Starting relationships:** Lyra Dain enters as a seemingly helpful returnist; Marek and Neris disagree over whether to use a dangerous sequence loan whose hidden counterweight will fall on the refugees.

**Major pressure:** the Quiet Choir uses the emergency to close a local branch of possibilities.

**Power change:** Returnpath fails under scale; Marek cannot distribute a seasonal displacement without a consenting network, and pays with a permanent gap in his own recent memory.

**Resolution:** the communities break the convergence ritual and keep both seasons, accepting economic damage rather than a forced “correct” winter. Oren Pike is left unwritten but physically alive.

**Next-question:** who are the people in the Quiet North claiming a lost future?

### Volume 8 — The First Return (Chapters 351–400)

**Central pressure:** an expedition into the Hollow North meets people who say they are descendants of the Last Meridian and possess records of a world that never happened here.

**Starting relationships:** Marek and Neris share the expedition's danger but disagree over whether the returnist families are witnesses or a political weapon.

**Major pressure:** Lyra Dain uses the returnist families to justify closing the local future.

**Power change:** Branch Auditor lets Marek compare several plausible continuations, but he initially mistakes a well-supported lie for the safest outcome.

**Resolution:** the returnist families receive protection as people, not a return ticket. The expedition recovers a fragment of the First Account and learns that the tower's founders were survivors, not gods.

**Next question:** what happens when borrowed skills become a whole generation's inheritance?

### Volume 9 — The Borrowed Skills (Chapters 401–450)

**Central pressure:** craft loans spread through the Red Uplands, giving workers skills they did not learn while stripping guilds of their apprenticeships and histories.

**Starting relationships:** Neris opposes Marek's attempt to use a future cure without full consent; Pella Rusk brings students into the dispute.

**Major pressure:** the Compact tries to claim ownership of every inherited technique.

**Power change:** Marek uses Branch Auditor to compare provenance rather than power, and accepts that a plausible skill can still be ethically illegitimate.

**Resolution:** craft lineages receive provenance records, communities decide who may teach them, and the current workers keep their skills but lose the legal claim to own them.

**Next-question:** can Ysara's emergency charter suspend the entire city?

### Volume 10 — The Chancellor's Ledger (Chapters 451–500)

**Central pressure:** Ysara Venn declares a citywide continuity loan and freezes appeals, arguing that the basin cannot survive another district-level failure.

**Starting relationships:** Marek is offered promotion and protection; Neris refuses to join a citizens' council that has no real power.

**Major pressure:** the Chancellor uses a real threat to make dissent look like sabotage.

**Power change:** Marek becomes a skilled Branch Auditor and learns that public truth is a strategic resource that can be depleted.

**Resolution:** Marek releases the archive, coordinates a provisional citizens' audit council, and forces Ysara to accept independent review. Ysara survives politically weakened; the Quiet Choir gains the next crisis.

**Next-question:** who is A-0, and what authority does the signature claim over the archive?

### Volume 11 — The Quiet Choir (Chapters 501–550)

**Central pressure:** the Choir performs a convergence ritual that closes several plausible futures in one district, erasing people from records before they understand what happened.

**Starting relationships:** Lyra Dain forces Marek to choose between exposing her and preserving the returnists' refuge.

**Major pressure:** Edren Sable appears in person through a succession body and proves that the First Account is an active authority, not an old myth.

**Power change:** Marek begins Charter Weaver, assembling a voluntary compact among witnesses, doctors, wardens, and farmers.

**Resolution:** the local ritual fails, the returnists survive as a protected political community, and the hidden history establishes the Last Convocation, Edren's copied authority, and the existence of the Founder's Clause. The crossing year remains a sealed line in the First Account.

**Next question:** who should be allowed to authorize power now that the founders' authority is known?

### Volume 12 — The Inheritance of Power (Chapters 551–600)

**Central pressure:** the Crown of Continuation forms around a proposal to regulate all borrowing through a single council.

**Starting relationships:** Sera Holt opposes an immediate end to emergency loans; Marek's secrecy about a record that could clear him of an old audit error but expose Tomas's original claim damages his relationship with Neris.

**Major pressure:** people with inherited skills and year scars become a new political caste under both the Crown and Hollow Court.

**Power change:** Charter Weaver becomes usable, but only when Marek accepts collective liability.

**Resolution:** the city adopts a local consent rule, not a perfect law. Neris leaves the field partnership temporarily, and the Open Charter is delayed rather than declared from a speech.

**Next-question:** what has the Tower done to history itself?

### Volume 13 — The History That Owes Interest (Chapters 601–650)

**Central pressure:** record loans erase genealogies and legal witnesses across the Crown March, causing living families to lose claims to homes, inheritances, and buried relatives.

**Starting relationships:** Hesper testifies in exchange for a public record; Edren offers Marek a private restoration of his family's missing history.

**Major pressure:** the Crown uses the identity crisis to justify central record control. A faction presents a leaked fragment of the Founder's Clause and claims it can void every old loan at once, threatening the basin with an uncontrolled release of power.

**Power change:** Marek can coordinate multiple charters, but every reconstruction creates a new cost for the people whose records differ.

**Resolution:** families reconstruct legal identities without pretending the old records were innocent. The leaked fragment is authenticated; the First Account, its Founder's Clause, and its crossing-year entry are identified; the indiscriminate voiding attempt is stopped through public appeals and restitution.

**Next-question:** can a future generation be used as collateral in a war?

### Volume 14 — The War for the Next Year (Chapters 651–700)

**Central pressure:** three regional armies borrow future victories, creating battles that cannot be won by ordinary soldiers and a famine that will follow the peace.

**Starting relationships:** Marek and Neris repair trust by sharing the dangerous record; Tomas refuses to become a symbol.

**Major pressure:** the Crown uses a single emergency loan to bind military and civilian futures.

**Power change:** Marek becomes a full Charter Weaver and learns that a compact can fail even when its first signers are sincere.

**Resolution:** a negotiated mutual pledge disables the borrowed weapons and preserves civilian routes, but the war leaves a lasting regional scar. Marek and Neris choose equal partnership.

**Next-question:** what did the Last Convocation actually sacrifice to cross?

### Volume 15 — The Last Convocation (Chapters 701–750)

**Central pressure:** the team reaches a preserved crossing chamber where the founders' records, tools, personal effects, and sealed founding copy survive.

**Starting relationships:** Edren offers to restore the lost future to Marek personally; Neris demands that the offer be made public.

**Major pressure:** the choice between restoring a beautiful lost world and protecting the imperfect present.

**Power change:** Marek learns to use Charter Weaver across historical records, but cannot make a past obey the living.

**Resolution:** the group rejects restoration as an act of present-day ownership, opens and authenticates the sealed founding copy in a witnessed chamber, saves the historical evidence, and identifies the exact conditions that let Edren activate the First Account. They understand that the Founder's Clause must be voided, not rewritten to name a new beneficiary.

**Next-question:** can the districts hold an Open Charter while the tower is failing?

### Volume 16 — The City of Open Accounts (Chapters 751–800)

**Central pressure:** the Yearspire's locks begin to fail; each district must choose whether to abandon another district while the First Account grows unstable.

**Starting relationships:** Marek and Neris sign as independent witnesses; Tamsin finally tells him what she knows about his childhood mark.

**Major pressure:** Edren persuades frightened civic leaders that only the First Account can prevent a citywide collapse.

**Power change:** four districts learn to fund real effects out of present contributions alone, and Marek must surrender his personal claim to a future self before a compact can bind him to anything. Nothing here is the Open Account: a provisional common can gather contributions and hold narrow living-person pledges, but the charter cannot be sealed until the founding claim is released.

**Resolution:** four districts establish a public, voluntary year commons funded by present contributions, with a defined transition period for existing borrowers and no new extraction from absent, unborn, or unchosen futures. Narrow living-person pledges are permitted only where an independent witness room can hold the terms, and the whole compact is provisional. Edren takes the remaining central keys.

**Next-question:** can the other regions recognize the same charter before he closes the final future?

### Volume 17 — The Future That Refuses (Chapters 801–850)

**Central pressure:** the Quiet North, the Red Uplands, the ports, and Morrowfen each hold a different future claim; Edren's singular authority makes convergence physically possible.

**Starting relationships:** allies disagree about whether the returnist descendants of the Last Meridian deserve a vote in the final settlement.

**Major pressure:** Edren begins treating disagreement itself as a form of shortfall.

**Power change:** Marek makes the Open Account *possible* without completing it. Every affected community can now name independent witness rooms, agree to be bound by a finding it cannot later revise, and withdraw before sealing. The charter still cannot exist, because it can only be sealed where the founding claim is released.

**Resolution:** the independent witnesses win standing to seat an independent quorum inside the Deep Engine and to file a quorum challenge against Edren's claim to choose one future. The authenticated founding copy they carry is the evidence; the rooms that will rule on it are the thing Edren cannot own. Edren is stripped of the settlement authority he has been exercising by default, which opens the route to the engine. The final cost is known to everyone; no one is asked to trust Marek blindly.

**Next-question:** what will Marek refuse when Edren offers him a perfect life?

### Volume 18 — The Year That Is Not Ours (Chapters 851–900)

**Central pressure:** Edren activates the First Account and tries to close every future except the Last Meridian's approved continuation.

**Starting relationships:** Marek and Neris act as equal partners rather than rescuer and rescued; Tomas, Sera, Nim, Ilya, Hesper, and the returnists each hold a necessary piece of the final response.

**Major pressure:** the city begins to experience the first true year drought: plans become impossible, records conflict, and people can no longer rely on ordinary tomorrow.

**Power change:** Open Account is used at full civic scale, then deliberately dismantled as a personal power when the common charter is sealed.

**Resolution:** the tower is transformed without erasing the people whose lives were built around its promises. The First Account is released, borrowed powers fade, losses are named, and public witnesses replace the singular First Auditor.

**Final image:** the old year-marks disappear from the foundation, the tower's lamps burn with ordinary maintained fire, and its doors open to anyone who wants to read the first entry in public.

## Aftermath commitments

The detailed character and institutional fates are authoritative in `outline/ending.md` and must not be invented differently during drafting. The series-level commitments are:

- Ovid, Ysara, Seren, the Trust, the Crown, the Chamber, the Hollow Court, and the Saltglass Harbor Council all reach explicit, non-erasing endpoints.
- The character and institutional endpoints listed in `outline/ending.md` §4 and §5 are *processes*, not announcements. Trials, dissolutions, restitutions, the expiry of borrowed effects, and the first years of the rotating council are carried in the MR 435–438 epilogue, planned in `outline/ending.md` §8 and written in Volume 18's last two chapters. Volume 18 may not skip those years, compress them into a speech, or invent a different set of endpoints.
- Oren's survival after being left unwritten is not a restoration of the legal identity taken from him.
- The Open Yearspire permits narrow living-person future pledges, but never permits absent, unborn, or unchosen futures to be spent.
- The tower's transformation does not erase the people whose lives were built around older promises.

## Ending constraints

The final conflict must be built from established systems and relationships. No new cosmic enemy, god, or world-changing prophecy may be introduced. The resolution must answer the central mystery, pay off Marek and Neris's relationship, and change the institution rather than simply remove it. Existing borrowers' lives and consequences remain part of the world after the victory.
