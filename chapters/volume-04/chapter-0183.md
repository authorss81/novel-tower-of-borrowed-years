# Chapter 183 — A Man Who Tries

Margit Sova came up the ladder on the ninth of the fourth month of MR 416 with a chaff tin under her arm and a lease folded in three in the hand that has got worse over twenty-six years, and she put the tin on the table first, because the tin was the reason she had come, and then she put the lease down beside it, and the two of them together were the whole of what she had.

"I have held this pitch since the growth month of the year before last," she said. "A hundred and forty pounds of chaff a week in the season and a pitch on the low-ground road with a hard standing and a slope off it that everybody in this quarter wants and nobody else has got, and I have held it two seasons on a lease that is witnessed, and the lease is up at the end of this month."

"Renewed," said Zlata Hruska.

"Renewed," said Margit Sova. "And I have been to four counters since the sixth month of last year and there is no lease in this city that a woman can get renewed this month, and I know that before I say it and I have not come up that ladder to be told it."

She put her thumb on the lease and did not open it.

"I have held it two seasons," she said, "and the reason I have held it is a man of about forty came down the low-ground road on the second of the ninth month of last year and said five words to me at a window about a boundary between two market pitches, and I have not been able to use my own ground since and I do not know what that means and neither does he, and he said the same five words to somebody else in the fifth month of last year and they did not know about each other."

Nobody said anything.

"So the lease is a lease and a lease is witnessed," she said, "and I am not going to sit on this ladder and pretend I do not know that there are about nine people in this basin whose attestation is of record and every one of the nine has been produced once, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a person may be produced twice, and I am one of them, and that is not a complaint, it is a lease."

---

Marek Vale stood up off the bench.

He had been in the room eleven evenings in the fourth month and nine in the third and he had read four things out loud in it and had never once been asked to do anything else, and he stood up now the way a man stands up who has been waiting for a thing to happen to him for four months.

"No," said Margit Sova, before he had got to the end of standing up.

"You do not know what I was going to do."

"I know what every clerk in this city has done for two hundred years and I have watched you do the other thing four times, and I am telling you now, in front of the room, so that you cannot do it and say afterwards that nobody stopped you." She did not raise her voice. "You are going to say that you can be a witness. And you cannot, and everybody in this room knows you cannot, and the only person in this room who does not know it is you, and that is not a thing to be said at somebody in their own house."

"Everybody in this room knows it," said Marek Vale. "I will say it out loud as well, then, so that it is said at me instead of about me. I am a spent witness. A settlement marshal wrote it on the back of a printed notice about a standpipe on the eighteenth of the first month of this year, with a day in the head of it, because I asked her for it. A Deputy of the Chamber entered a finding on the thirteenth of the third month that no answer of mine is available to her as proof of any fact whatever, and I read that finding and I signed it, and my column on a sheet in a flat case in Bell Stair has had nothing in it since the last night of the ninth month of last year and I have had no pencil and nothing in my coat to write with.

"And on the thirtieth of the ninth month of last year I put my name in a box behind a comparison in a room eight feet by eleven, and it is the only thing I am actually qualified to do, and it is the thing that did it."

He put his hand flat on the table next to the tin.

"I can witness your lease," he said.

"You cannot," said Zlata Hruska.

"He can," she said. "That is not what I am going to stop. Sit down, both of you, because you are both going to be right and it is going to take nine minutes and I would like it done sitting down."

---

"Here is the whole of it and there are three halves and all three are true and none of them is a reason to do anything," said Zlata Hruska.

"First. A room in which instrument is witnessed is recognised by the region where a responsible party is named, and where the responsible party is a person the form requires an employer. There is no employer in this room. There has never been an employer in this room. So a paper witnessed in this room is good in this room and good nowhere else, and a lease renewed on it is a lease renewed on a piece of paper that is worth nothing at the district office and nothing at a counter on the causeway and nothing in a room in Bell Stair.

"Second. Even if it were worth something, he is spent, and a spent witness cannot be a second time, and if he stood in this room and watched a woman put a name to a lease then he would be a second entry against her name, and there is nowhere in this kingdom to put a second entry, and both of them would be standing in a room and neither of them would be in it.

"Third, and this is the one that is going to hurt, and I am going to say it and then I am going to stop talking for a while."

She put her hand on the rail, which is a bed frame, three bought brackets and one shoe-tongue of iron made in a lane.

"You are all three relying on the fact that this room is not recognised," she said. "You have all got your whole advantage out of that. Every one of you has come up a ladder in the last four months and got something out of the fact that there is no form on the wall and no name over the door and no employer, and I have said no to a copy, and no to a head, and no to a form, and no to a reach, and I have been right four times in one morning, and the price of being right four times is on the slate at the top of that ladder and it says *things you cannot do this month* without somebody to stand and say you did, and the head went on that slate at the first bell on the twenty-second of the second month and it is the only thing on the slate that has gone out of date, and it has gone out of date by a month, and nobody has washed it off, and I have not washed it off, because a head is not a fact about anybody and a slate is not a record and I said that in the second month and I am saying it in the fourth and it is still true. And there are eleven lines on it and none of them has got any better since the twenty-second of the second month.

"A lease is a lease. A renewed lease is a piece of paper about a pitch on a road. And the moment somebody wants that piece of paper to be good in a district office, this room is not the place it happens in, and the fact that it is not the place it happens in is not a fault in the room. It is the room. That is what a room with no employer is for."

---

Margit Sova picked up the lease and put it in her apron and picked up the tin and put it under her arm again, and she did it in the order she had done it in when she came up, which is a thing about her that nobody in the room commented on.

"There is a man at a counter on the causeway," she said. "Fifty-one. Thirty years. She does not read forms to people and she does not have to and she will not lend her book to anybody, and I have been in there three times this year with a question and got a sentence out of her each time and the sentence has never once been wrong.

"And I am not going to ask her to certify this, and I want it in the air in this room that I have thought about it for about a fortnight and I am not going to, and I am not going to tell anybody at that counter that I thought about it.

"Because the only thing anybody can certify is a page. Not a lease. A page. A copy of a page, true of a page, with a clerk's name behind it. And I do not have a page of anything and neither does the woman who holds my pitch, and I cannot get one, and the asking is the whole of what is wrong with it. In four months the lease runs out and the pitch goes to whoever is standing there with a witness, and it will not be me, and there is nobody I can be angry with, because the rule is not a rule anybody wrote for this."

"You want me to certify the lease," said Marek Vale.

"I want the pitch," said Margit Sova. "I have not wanted a witness once in my life. I have wanted a pitch since the growth month of the year before last, and I got it, and I have been told what I am by four men in a good coat at four windows, and not one of the four has ever offered me anything that would hold a piece of ground."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Say that again," said Zlata Hruska.

"I want the pitch," said Margit Sova. "I have not wanted a witness once in my life. And I am not going to be ashamed of having said it in a room and I am not going to be told by anybody in this lane that I should have wanted the other thing, because the other thing is the only thing anybody in this city has ever offered the two of us."

She went down the ladder with the tin under her arm and the lease in her apron and she was not thanked and she had not asked to be.

---

He walked down to the Counters' Walk in the middle of the afternoon because there was nothing else to do with the rest of the day and because the ditch is four hundred yards of new trench between two seed yards and one of the two yards is Zdenka Hrab's.

She was sixty-two. She had held that yard since her mother's. She had come out of her door on the twenty-eighth of the second month, looked at four hundred yards of new trench, and gone back in without saying a word, and she had not said a word since, and she did not know that there was a cost.

He stood at her gate for about a minute and a half.

She came out because a man standing at a gate in the middle of an afternoon is a thing that happens on that road, and she had a hand on the latch and she looked at him and she did not open it.

"Can I ask you a question," said Marek Vale.

"You can ask me a question," said Zdenka Hrab. "Whether I answer it is mine."

He had three things in his head and two of them were things you do not ask a person at a gate, and the third one was the only one available, and he asked it and he asked it well, because he has never in three years put a question to a person that he could not answer the answer to.

"This is a good yard," he said. "Is it."

"It is a good yard," she said. "It was my mother's and it is good and it does not need telling."

"That is true and I am sorry it took me a year and a bit to see the shape of it," he said. "I came up this road with a question and it was not that one and I have not asked it and I am going to go back down the road now. Thank you for the yard."

He went about nine steps and she said it to his back, which is a thing women of sixty-two do.

"The man who cut it," said Zdenka Hrab. "He is the one that comes and reads. I have seen him on the road twice since the twenty-eighth of the second month and he has not come in and I have not called him in and I have not got anything to say to him, and if you are him then you have wasted a walk and if you are not him then I have told a stranger about my yard and that is the end of it."

And that was the whole conversation and it had taken about two minutes and there is no form in this kingdom on which a person may be told that a bill is coming, and he did not tell her, and there was not one instrument in this basin he could have told her with, and in four years a man in a good coat can produce a document at a man who keeps a key and it will say that a class of the people of that ground accepted a winter, and the class will not be able to answer, and one of the two addresses on the line of accounts is a woman of sixty-two standing at a gate with her hand on the latch who has never signed anything in her life that anybody knows of.

---

At the bottom of the lane, in the room at the bottom of the lane, a woman of about thirty-four had trimmed the lamp and was standing in a room with a bench in it and a bin in the corner and a chair that nobody sat in, and a man came up the street with a chaff tin's worth of nothing in his hands and sat down on the bench for about a minute, and then got up and went home, and nobody asked him anything, and he was not thanked, and he had tried once and it had cost a woman of forty-one nothing that she had not already lost in the growth month of the year before last.
