# Chapter 159 — A Stall That Is Not a Stall

The pitch on the low-ground road that had been a chaff stall in the harvest month of MR 414 and had shut four days running and had not opened since was fourteen feet of stone edge and a canopy frame with no canvas on it, and the frame had been there since before the flood year, and there was a lockable box under the counter at the back of it, and the counter was the only part of it that still had a top on.

Margit Sova was there at the second bell on the twenty-first of the ninth month with a broom in one hand, because a man with a barrow on a market road will sweep a pitch whether or not he is holding it, and it is the cheapest thing a person can do about a thing they have lost.

"You have a lease," said Marek Vale.

"I have had a lease and the lease is finished and I am not able to renew it and I am not able to sell it and I am not able to give it away, and there are four men in good coats in this city who have told me all three of those things in about nine weeks, and I have believed all three of them, and none of them is the reason."

"Then say the reason."

Margit Sova put the broom against the counter.

"A lease is witnessed," she said. "That is all a lease is. Two people, a term, a pitch, and somebody who says the person holding it is a person and not a fiction. And a person cannot be a witness twice, because the first time says the word and the second time there is nothing left to say it with, and everybody in this kingdom knows that, and there is no form in this city or any other that will let a person be asked a second time.

"So my lease came to the end of a season in which I could trade and I cannot hold the ground, and that is not a hardship, that is an arithmetic, and I have done the arithmetic about nine times and it comes out the same every time and it is not going to come out differently in the ninth month or the first of next year.

"And I would like you to notice what has been done to me in four months, because I have noticed it and nobody else has. I have not been thrown out. I have not been sold. I have not been cheated. I have been spent. Somebody used up the only thing I had that was anybody else's, and it was four years old, and it was about a bin, and I signed it in a courtyard in the second month of MR 412 because a man from the water office wanted to know who put the bins out on the Counters' Walk and I told him, in a fair small hand, in a column."

---

The boy on the road had come down the market at about the second bell and was sweeping too, at the other end, and did not come over, and the market went on around the two of them for about four minutes before anybody said anything else.

"You have a list," said Marek.

"I have a list in a box under that counter and the box has a lock on it and the lock is a hasp and a padlock and the key is on a nail inside, and I have had that arrangement for twenty-six years and I have never once lost it and I have never once had to move it, and I want you to hear the whole of that sentence before you ask me the next thing, because the next thing is the one I have been waiting about nine weeks for."

"I was not going to ask you for it."

"You were going to ask me to say it out loud so that you could write it down." She turned round and looked at him. "I have watched you do that three times in six weeks. In a ditch. On a roof. At a rail. You go into a room with a piece of paper in your coat and you come out with four lines in your own hand and a date on them and no name, and then you go and look for a place to put them, and there is not one, and you keep them anyway.

"I am not going to be another four lines."

---

Marek Vale put his hand into his coat and stopped with it about half way, and took it out again, and put it flat on the counter.

"I want to write down what you have just said," he said. "I am not going to, and I am going to tell you why, because you have told me why in about nine words and I would like to be the first man in this basin to admit the mechanism out loud in a market.

"I want to write it down because I have had a year in which being able to prove a thing was the only thing I had. I found out in the third month of last year that a man's name was on a sheet in a drawer in the hills and I did not walk four miles, because I wanted to be able to prove it, and he found out four months later at a weighbridge from a bridge clerk, and when I gave him the paper he told me it was worth nothing, and he was right, and it was worth nothing because a piece of paper in a book of my own is not a room.

"And you have just told me the same thing about a bin, and the difference is that you have had it for twenty-six years and I have had it for one, and you have had it in a locked box under a counter and I have had it in my coat, and neither of us has a room."

Margit Sova picked the broom up.

"Yes," she said. "And now you have said it, so I am going to give you the thing I have instead of the thing you asked for, and I am giving it to you because you did not ask, and not because of anything else.

"And I am going to tell you first that it is not a list of names. People always assume it is a list of names. It is a list of what a pitch owes and what it was given and in what order, and it has been going in the same book since I was fifteen, and there is not one name in the whole of it, and there is no head at the top of any page, and there is no total anywhere in it, and I have never added it up and I could not if I tried, because a total is a figure I would be putting on a box under a counter in my own hand about about nine hundred people who have bought chaff off me in twenty-six years."

She took the box out from under the counter and put it on the top, and the hasp was stiff, and she worked it with the heel of her hand.

---

There were about nine sheets in it and they were thin and they had been folded and unfolded so many times that they had gone soft along the middle crease in the way a fold goes when a thing has been opened and shut about nine times a year for two decades.

And on every one of the sheets there was a column, and on the top sheet the column had nothing at the head of it.

"This," said Margit Sova, "is the second of six."

"Six of what."

"Six places in this basin where somebody has written a column and not put anything at the top of it, and I know that because the woman who keeps the wall list in Tallow Ward told me, at a counter, in the fourth month, when I went to ask her whether a column with nothing at the head of it could be produced at me, which it can, and which is why I have never asked for one."

"You have been at the shed."

"I have been at the shed and I was told no, and the woman who told me no was right, and I have not been back, and I am not going to, because I have four witnesses who are all people who cannot be found and one of them is dead and two of them are in a ward I have never been to and the fourth is a man who counts at a rail and is not a person you can call." She put the sheet down flat. "And I am telling you that I have known about this column for about a fortnight and that I have been carrying it in my head for a fortnight and that in four years there is going to be a man in a good coat who comes to this pitch and asks me what the column is for.

"And I am going to tell you what I will say to him, and you may write this down because I have not got anywhere to put it and neither have you, and I have thought about that for about a week.

"I am going to say: it is a column about what a pitch owes, in a hand that got worse over twenty-six years, and there is no head at the top of it because the only person who could head it is me and I have declined, and I have declined for the same reason the woman in Tallow Ward declined, which is that a head is the one thing on a sheet that a stranger can be shown without having to know anything else about it."

---

She put the box back under the counter and shut the hasp and put the key on the nail inside, and the market went on around the two of them.

"One more thing and then I have to open that bin," she said. "Not about the column. About the man in the good coat.

"On the fourth of the month he told me I was a witness to a thing. And on the second a different man told me I was a witness to a different thing. And both of them said the same five words in the same voice at about the same hour on the same morning, and neither of them knew the other one existed, and I have worked out since what that means, and it means there is a list somewhere, and it means the list has my name on it, and it means I am not a person in that building, I am a place in a queue, and I have decided that I am not going to be frightened about it, and I am going to be specific about it, and I would like to know what you are going to do with the specific."

Marek Vale stood at a pitch on the low-ground road with his hand flat on a stone counter and no paper in his coat.

"I am going to find out who else is on it," he said. "Not by asking the men. There is no form on which a man in a good coat may be asked what he has said to about nine people, and if there were, I would not use it, because the answer would be a document and a document is a thing that can be produced at you in four years."

"Then how."

"Because two of them have been told twice," said Marek, "and the only reason I know that is that one of them told me at a rail, and she told me because I stood at the rail for an hour and asked her nothing at all."

Margit Sova picked up her broom and looked at him for about four seconds.

"That is the first thing anybody in nine months has said to me about a bin," she said, "and it is a sentence about a woman at a rail and not about me, and I am going to take it, and I am not going to thank you for it, and neither of us is going to be thanked, and you may tell that to a man with a good coat if he comes.

"A man can be free when he signs a thing about a ditch and not free when somebody tells him what he is a witness to. And there is no form on either."

She went and opened the bin, and the market came in off the road and set up around a pitch with a canopy frame and no canvas on it, and there is not one word in this chapter about a column at the head of anything, because she would not have it and he did not ask her twice.
