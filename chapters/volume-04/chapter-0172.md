# Chapter 172 — Eleven Things a Person Cannot Do

Drazen Topic put the slate down on the rail at the first bell on the twenty-second of the second month of MR 416 and said the number and then, for the first time in eleven years, put something at the top of the slate.

He wrote it in a hand that was no better than the hand he had been writing numbers in all his life, and it took him about four seconds, and about nine people going past on a market road watched him do it.

Then he washed it off with his sleeve, which took a second, and picked the slate up and put the number up again.

"What did you write," said the woman who had been standing at the rail, who was about thirty and had a bundle on her arm and had been in the queue and not gone forward.

"A head," said Drazen Topic.

"On your slate."

"On my slate."

"You have had that slate eleven years and there has never been anything on the top of it."

"There has never been anything on the top of it," he said, "and I have never been asked why, and nobody in this city has ever told me to put anything there, and I want you to understand that I am not putting it there because I have been told to. I am putting it there because in about an hour I am going to write a list under it and I have counted things at this rail for eleven years and I am not going to write a list on a slate with nothing at the top of it, because a number with nothing at the top of it is a number somebody can say is a queue."

He put the number up again, which was the same number.

---

He had been asked the question at the rail and he had not answered it there, and he said so on the way, which is not his habit.

"She wanted to know what a person can do this month that she could not do last month," he said. "That was the whole of it. She had a bundle on her arm and she said it as though she were asking about a price."

"And you said you would find out."

"I said I would ask somebody," said Drazen Topic, "and I have been standing at this rail for about a quarter of an hour working out that I do not know anybody to ask, which is a thing I have known for eleven years and have never had to say."

Nim Rook came down the market road with a satchel and did not slow down, and he fell in beside her without asking, and about four ways later and without a street being named they were at the end where the steps go down and the water comes up, and there was a ladder up the outside wall of a dye house with three bad rungs at the top.

---

The room had a rail round three sides of it, four unmatched chairs, a lamp, a bed with mending on it, and a smell that had been in it since the second of the first month of MR 416 and that everybody in it had stopped noticing about the ninth day.

"That is the bin," said Zlata Hruska. "I have had three people ask me this month whether it is the dyer. It is not the dyer. It is a bin behind a chandler's on the Counters' Walk that cannot be moved, and it is here, and the reason it is here is four of us carry it up that lane on our backs and put it on the two steps so that the lane does not have to go round it in the dark."

"Since when," said Marek Vale.

"Since the second of this year," said Zlata Hruska. "In the first month the district put the bins out twice a week. In the second month it put them out once. And in the last week of the second month it has not put them out at all, and there is a notice on the chandler's door about it, and the notice is in the ordinary language of a form and it does not say why."

"Does a bin need a witness."

"A bin needs a lease," said Zlata Hruska. "Not a lease to own. A lease to be *put out*. The district holds the ground the bin stands on from the chandler and the putting out of it is a work, and a work has to be witnessed to be a work, and the district has nine people in this basin whose attestation is of record and every one of the nine has been produced once.

"So the bin does not go up the lane by itself, and the bin does not go into the low ground, and the bin sits behind a chandler's until somebody comes with a paper, and four women carry it up a lane on their backs and put it on the two steps, and I have asked for it to be carried once by a man and I have been told by the man that he cannot be asked."

Lovro Prazak came up the ladder with his hands wet, which is the way he came up it, and put a basket down.

"I have been up this ladder twice in three weeks for nothing at all," he said. "So if there is a list I will tell you the boat one and then I will go down."

---

"Go on," said Zlata Hruska.

"I hire out," said Lovro Prazak, "and I am hired out, and a man from the coast was in this lane in the ninth month of last year because he had nowhere to be stood, and he worked the low ground for a fortnight for a bed and he told me three things about boats and I have not had occasion to use any of them.

"The first is that a boat's hire is a lease. It is not a special one. It is a lease for a hull for a day, and it is witnessed, and the reason it is witnessed is that a man who has paid for a boat wants to be able to say in a week that the boat he paid for is the boat that is not there.

"And the second is that it can be done without a paper. Everybody on the water does it. You put the money in the hand of the man who rows and you get a piece of chalk on the gunwale, and if a man in a good coat comes along and asks you about the chalk you have nothing at all, and you say so, and he writes down that you said so and goes away.

"That is what I am telling you and I am not being clever about it. It is the only one of the eleven that can be got round, and I have been getting round it for nine years, and it is why a man with no name on anything can still go on the water in this city."

"Then the boat is not the problem," said Nim Rook.

"The boat is exactly the problem," said Lovro Prazak. "The boat is the only one you can get round, and the one I cannot get round is getting somebody back from it."

---

He said that last part before anybody asked for it, and then he said it again more slowly, because Marek Vale had not moved.

"Jelka Sondor went to the coast on the second of the first month," said Lovro Prazak, "on a boat, and nobody told her she was on a wall, and there has been no word from her and there is a carter who goes that way every second week. And the charterer's man will not take a charter without a witness, and a charter is a lease, and there is not one person in this lane who can be put in a box.

"So the coast is four hours away on a good day. It is four hours away on a bad day. It is four hours away and there is no way to go up it that does not want a piece of paper, and a man who rows for money will do it for a piece of chalk, and the piece of chalk is not a hire and the hire is not a charter and the charter is not a lease, and if the boat is not there on the day it was hired for then I am the man who cannot say it was there.

"And that is the whole of the thing. I would go. I have been thinking about going since the ninth of this month and I have been at this ladder twice about it and I have not said anything, because every time I have got as far as saying it I have worked out that the sentence I would have to say afterwards is *I went up there without a piece of paper*, and I would be saying it for the rest of my life and there is no form anywhere for me to put it on."

Nobody said anything.

"You have got a carter who goes that way," said Zlata Hruska.

"I have got a carter who goes that way and I have got a boat and I have got a piece of chalk," said Lovro Prazak, "and I have got a man of forty-nine who sleeps in a doorway and no name on any map, and I have worked out that those four things are enough and I have been carrying the arithmetic about for a fortnight and I am handing it to a room because I cannot carry it any further, and I am not going to be thanked, and I have never once been thanked for anything."

---

"So that is three," said Zlata Hruska, "and I am going to say the rest of them and I am going to say them badly, and I have a list on a slate and I have not written any of it down, and I want it written, and I do not want it kept, and those are two different wants and I am aware of it.

"Marek. Get the slate."

The man who counted at a rail turned the slate over and put it down flat on the table, and it was a slate and not a piece of paper, and it had a head on it in his own hand, and he had written the head at the first bell in front of about nine people on a market road and had washed it off and written it again.

He wrote the head again where Zlata Hruska could see it. It said:

*Things you cannot do this month without somebody to stand and say you did.*

"That is the head," he said. "That is why I wrote it at the rail this morning and I want somebody in this room to say that it was not there before, because in four years somebody is going to come down that ladder and ask when the head was put on, and the answer is that a man who counts wrote it himself at the first bell on the twenty-second of the second month and washed it off and wrote it again."

---

She named them one at a time and Odile Marn and Margit Sova between them named four of them before she had finished saying the first.

Hold a pitch. Cut a ditch. Let a boat. Have a bin put out on a piece of ground somebody else has hired.

Hire a horse, and move a body, and stand in a room twice, and sell a pitch on to somebody who will then not be able to hold it either, and have a lease renewed.

And the eleventh, which was the only one on the slate that nobody in the room could have named, and which Zlata Hruska gave in a flat voice at the end:

Be told, afterwards, what you signed.

"It is not true of a person who has not been produced," she said, "and it is not true of about half of us, and it is on the list because it is the one that has happened to every single person in this room including the three who are not on a wall, and I have been at a door for nine years asking people whether they were asked, and the answer is almost never no. The answer is *I did not know it was that*. And then they tell me the whole of it, and they tell me it to me, and it is the only room it has ever been said in, and it is not worth anything, and I am putting it on a slate because it is the true one and the other ten are arithmetic."

---

They read it out loud twice at the ordinary speed, and Drazen Topic read it because he counted, and a man who counts reading a list aloud is a man who cannot be accused of having written it, and everybody in that room had worked that out within about nine seconds of each other and nobody said so.

Then the argument, which took the rest of the night.

"That is a list," said Zlata Hruska. "That is the whole difficulty and you all know it and I am going to say it once. A list in a drawer is what a person is living inside, and I have said that to four people in nine years in the same words, and there is a woman of forty-one in this city with twenty-six years in a locked box under a counter and she is right and she is not going to be thanked either.

"And a slate is a slate. Paper is paper. A man in a good coat can produce paper at anybody in about four years and there is no form in this kingdom on which a person may be told that a piece of paper in a drawer was never a piece of paper in a drawer."

"You are not copying it," said Nim Rook. "That is the rule and it is easy and it is the only rule."

"It is not that easy," said Margit Sova. "It is easy for me. I cannot write, and a woman who cannot write cannot copy a list, and that is nine tenths of what a rule in this city actually is. The moment it is a matter of hands, it is a matter of whose hands. He has hands. She has hands. I have a hand that wrote nine lines into a deal table in the open in the first month and then got to the end of it by the twenty-seventh, and there is no form in this kingdom on which anybody may be told that they cannot hold a piece of chalk."

"So we read it," said Zlata Hruska, "and nobody writes it down again anywhere, ever, and anybody who wants to know what is on it comes up a ladder and it is read to them, and that is the whole of the arrangement and it is not a remedy and it does not stop the bin."

"It does not stop the bin," said Odile Marn.

"No," said Zlata Hruska. "It does not stop the bin and it does not cut the ditch and it does not hold the pitch and it does not fetch Jelka Sondor from the coast, and I am going to say the true thing about it and then I am going to trim the lamp, which wants it.

"A rota of days on a brick is not a list of what anybody owes. That is the whole of the difference and I have got eleven lines of the opposite on a slate and I have never once been able to work out how to say it properly. What is on the wall is a day against a name and a person saying she will stand in a room. What is on that slate is eleven ways a person can be stopped, and the second one is a person, and I would write it on a wall in chalk in about a quarter of an hour if my hand was any good, and the only thing that would stop me is that a wall in this city is the wall, and there is one wall, and the day somebody puts eleven lines on it instead of ten names and six days is the day there is no rule on it at all."

She trimmed the wick. The lamp came up and went steadier and the room was the same room.

---

At about the eighth bell four women went down the ladder and up the lane with a bin on their shoulders, because the district had not put it out in the last week of the second month and would not put it out in the third, and the chandler's door had a notice on it in the ordinary language of a form.

They went past the wall on the way, and the ten names in chalk were on the wall, and there was a day against every one of them, and the eleventh thing on the slate was the only one on the slate about a person, and none of the four women could read it, and it was on a slate in a room up a ladder and it was worth nothing outside that room, and the bin went up the lane on four backs and the lane did not have to go round it in the dark.
