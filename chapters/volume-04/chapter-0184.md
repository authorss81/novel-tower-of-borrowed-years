# Chapter 184 — Three Days on Ground Under Water

They took the sheet out of the flat case on the twelfth of the fourth month of MR 416, in the middle of the afternoon, on a bank of new clay a foot and a half out of the water, because it was the only dry place within a mile and Neris Kade said so before anybody asked her to and then sat down on it and put the case across her knees.

"You have not got a pencil," she said.

"I have not got a pencil."

"You had one on the second of the third month at the clinic and you had one in the copy room on the fourth of the first month and I have watched you take one out of your coat twice since the growth month of last year and it has not been in your coat since the ninth month of last year, and I have not asked you about it once, and I am not going to now. Here."

She put a pencil down on the clay between them, and neither of them said one word about that.

The sheet was as it had been on the afternoon a man in a copy room on the hill cut it, because paper does not change. Two columns. Two names at the head of them in the same hand, in the same ink, at the same size. A rule under them: no person may write in the column with the other person's name at the head of it. A line at the foot, cut and left blank, which neither of them had asked for and neither had ever named.

Kade's column had about four lines in it from the first of the seventh month of last year and one from the thirteenth of the third month of this year.

Marek's column had nothing in it at all.

---

"You are going to write in yours," said Neris Kade.

"I am going to write in mine."

"I am not asking what in. I want to say the thing about the cost first and I want to say it in the open, on the ground, with the water where it is, because I have never said it out loud to anybody and I have thought about it since the second month, when a rule about a room came out of a drawer a night and a day up this river, and it has got to be said before I put a number on this sheet.

"In nine months a man in a good coat can produce this sheet at either of us." She did not look up. "I do not know whether it is nine months or less. I know that it is a sheet with two names at the head of it and that in four years a piece of paper can be produced at anybody in this kingdom, and this one is a piece of paper, and it is in my coat and it has been in a flat case between two pieces of card with nothing on the card, and there is nothing whatever in this world that stops a man who has it in his hand.

"And if I write on it, then the day it is produced there will be two columns and two names and a rule under them, and I will have written in mine, and he will not have written in his or he will have written in his, and either way it is two people and a rule and no way anybody in the world can tell which of us decided what.

"I have been in four of them." She said it without any weight at all. "Four places in this basin with a column and nothing at the head of it, and I know how many there are because I have been in four of them and I am not going to say the number out loud in the fourth month on a bank, and a man in a good coat can take a line off any one of them in a moment and there is no line on this sheet he can take off, and that is the whole of the difference and the whole of the difference in the whole of this.

"So I am going to use it," said Neris Kade, "and I am telling you the cost out loud first, and I am not going to be thanked for the telling and neither are you."

---

He wrote in his column, and it took him longer than a line should take, and the first thing he wrote was not about a person.

*The tools of this district went into the store on the nineteenth of the second month and came out of it on the twenty-seventh of the third. The store is on the low-ground road. The key is on a nail inside the door and the man who keeps it does not have a form for it. A person can walk to that store and stand in the doorway.*

He read it back to himself in his own head and did not say it out loud, and then he said one more line under it, because there was room and because he had had four months to think of one thing to say and this was the one.

*A period is a fact about a shed. Deputy Tolek entered one in a book in a building on the causeway side on the thirteenth of the third month and said it was the only answer she could use out of eleven, and she said out loud in that room that it took him three years to work out and her four seconds, and I have thought about that for about a month and she was right and she was not being gracious about it and I would not have wanted her to be.*

He put the pencil down on the clay.

"That is the first thing I have written in that column since the last night of the ninth month of last year," he said. "And it is a period and not a person, and I did not choose it because it was safe. I chose it because it is the only kind of answer that went into a book in this basin in five months that anybody can use, and it took a woman of forty-four with a hand that is worse than it was to tell me that in a room, and she told me because I had not given her the easy thing first."

Neris Kade read her own column and then read his, which she is allowed to do, and said nothing about it, and put the sheet flat on the clay with the wind on it.

---

"Three days," she said. "I want three days on this ground and then I am going to say a number."

"Say the number now."

"I will not say the number now. I will say the shape of it and you can tell me whether it is the right shape, and then I will say the number in the fifth month when the water is off it, if the water ever comes off it."

She put a finger on the sheet without touching it.

"The account of this ground is on a line of district accounts. A record was sealed on that line in the third month of last year and it is alive and it is going to carry a season through the fourth month. And the season does not come off the low ground. It comes off the line.

"And a line is a border between two pieces of ground. And a border has two ends and a season falls on both of them, and the low-ground end of this particular one belongs to a man of fifty who keeps a key and has a thumb on a piece of paper. And I have been out to this bank four times since the second month and the other end of the line is a yard belonging to a woman of sixty-two called Zdenka Hrab, and it is a good yard, and it was her mother's, and I found that out about two hours ago from the man who reads papers to people standing at her gate, which is a sentence I would not have believed in the growth month of last year."

"What do you want to do with that."

"I want to know how many holdings are on the line," said Neris Kade. "That is my trade. A field examiner is a person who goes out on a piece of ground and says what is on it. That is the whole of what a field examiner is and there is no other part of it and there is no form in this kingdom on which anybody may do it without doing it. And if I go down that line and count what is on either side of it, then at the end of it there is a number, and I put the number in my column, and the number is a fact about a piece of ground that a person could stand on and be shown.

"And then in the fourth month somebody's office has a number about a line instead of a guess, and the season goes where it goes and not where the book happens to have put it."

---

"Not this month," said Marek Vale.

"You do not get to say no. You do not have a vote and I have said that on this sheet since the seventh month of last year, in a copy room, and you have agreed with me every time."

"You are right and I have no vote and I am going to say the whole of it anyway, because I have been thinking about it for three days and I have got nothing else."

"Then say it."

"Two days ago a woman of forty-one came up a ladder and told me that she wants a pitch and has not wanted a witness once in her life. That is on the air in that room and every person in it heard it and it is the truest sentence anybody has said in this basin in a year, and I have not been able to put it down since, and I have been thinking about your number the whole time.

"If you write a count of the holdings on a line, it is a figure about a line. A figure about a line is a figure about a district. And a district is a line in a book in this city, and the day that number is on a sheet in your coat it is a district, and a man in a good coat does not have to do anything dishonest to use it. He has to say *how many*, and put the number in, and it is a true number, and it will be in four years at a counter in this city, and there will be nothing on the page to say how a woman of thirty-odd with no office worked it out and what she was standing on when she did.

"And one of the holdings on it is a yard belonging to a woman of sixty-two who has never signed anything in her life, and she came out of her door on the twenty-eighth of the second month and looked at four hundred yards of new trench and went back in, and she has not said a word since, and she does not know there is a cost, and there is no form in this kingdom on which anybody may be told that a bill is coming.

"And your number would be the first thing anybody has ever written down about her. Not a cost. Not a name. A count. And I would rather she were never counted than counted by a person she cannot see, in a column she cannot read, on a sheet with my name at the head of the other side of it."

---

Neris Kade did not answer for a while, and the wind came across the bank and moved the corner of the sheet, and neither of them put a hand on it.

"You are not wrong," she said. "I have been walking round this basin for three years telling people the thing that happened to them is not their own fault, and I have never once in three years been the person who decided what somebody was going to carry. I know exactly what that looks like from the outside, because I have said it to a great many people and I have been right every time and being right has never once made it smaller.

"So say the other half, because you have not said it, and I do not think you have noticed that you have not said it."

"The other half is that if we do not put the number on it, somebody in an office puts the number on it," said Marek Vale, "out of a book, without either of us, with a head on it, and it is wrong in some way we cannot check and it lands on somebody who did not agree to be landed on, and there is nothing whatever about that number that anybody in this basin could have stopped."

"Yes," said Neris Kade.

"That is the whole of the disagreement and it is about a woman of sixty-two and her yard and neither of us has ever spoken to her and neither of us is going to."

"It is not about a policy," she said. "Say that part out loud, because you have not said it either, and we have been circling it for two days as though it were about a policy and it is the only thing in three years we have never had."

"No," he said. "It is not about a policy. We agree about every policy in this basin. We agree that you ask first and that a list is a piece of paper and that a person is not a column and that the room asks and does not require. We have agreed about all of it since the seventh month of last year and we have never once used the sheet, and the reason we have not used the sheet is not that we disagreed about a policy. It is that the sheet is the one thing we have and we have been frightened of it for four months, and this morning on a bank you frightened me out of the use of mine by being right."

"I did not do that on purpose."

"I know you did not," he said. "That is why it worked."

---

The argument was not settled, and it was not settled because of anything either of them said, and the truth about the thirteenth and the fourteenth of the fourth month is that the low ground had a foot and a half of water on it and had had for about a fortnight and that a boundary cannot be walked under a foot and a half of water, and that a boundary you cannot walk is a boundary you do not know the length of, and that a person who is a field examiner and does not know the length of a boundary she is examining is not a person anybody should be asking for a number.

They tried it twice anyway. They got a stake in on the first of the two days, four feet long, driven with a maul out of Havel's store because Havel is a man who keeps tools and will lend a maul, and they put it in on the second day and by the second afternoon the bank on the low-ground side of it had gone soft under the driving and the stake leaned, and on the third morning it was in the water at an angle with nine inches of it out and neither of them said anything about it and both of them looked at it.

"You have not got a line," said Marek Vale on the afternoon of the fourteenth.

"I have not got a line," said Neris Kade. "I have got a stake in a foot and a half of water and a good yard belonging to a woman of sixty-two at the top of a road, and a man of fifty at the bottom of it with a class on a piece of paper, and about eleven men who have been on the lanes since the twenty-eighth of the third month because there is nowhere to put clay."

She wrote one line in her column, and she read it out loud twice at the ordinary speed, and the rule under the two names says nothing about reading out loud, and it is the only thing in this business that has never needed a form.

*A line of accounts crosses ground. Two addresses at the ends of it are known. The number of addresses on the whole of it is not known and has not been written.*

She put the pencil on the clay and left it there, and Marek Vale picked it up and put it in the inside pocket of a coat that has had nothing in it since the ninth month of last year, and neither of them said one word about that, and they walked back up the road to the store to give the maul back, and the water on the low ground was a foot and a half deep and still and going nowhere in particular, and the fifth month is when it goes off, and when it goes off this is all still here.
