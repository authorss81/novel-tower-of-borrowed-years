# Chapter 158 — A Fortnight on a Named Ground

The season works for the district on the Counters' Walk were on a trestle in a seed house off the low-ground road on the twelfth of the ninth month, and they were nine lines cut in a board, and they were made by the district and belonged to no office, and the eighth line had a name against it and the ninth was a well and the ninth was empty in the same way the ninth line of a works board four miles and eleven switchbacks up a road was empty, which is a shape and not a subject.

Line four was the Counters' Walk ditch.

Marek Vale had come down the road at the first bell and had not gone up to the ditch, which was the first thing he had done right in three months, and the second thing he had done right was not to say so on arrival.

"You are not going to read me anything," said Odile Marn.

"No."

"You have had a sheet in your coat since the sixth of the month and you have not read me a word of it and you have been in this building for a quarter of an hour and you have looked at a board."

"I have looked at line four."

"Then you have looked at it properly, which nobody has done in four years." She wiped her hands on her apron. "There is a thing I have to say first and I am going to say it in one sentence and then I am going to work all day and you can stand there.

"I have been told nine times since the sixth of the month that I am a witness. I have not been asked one question by any of the nine. I have got two children, a wall with a course in it that does not run, and a ditch that is a line of wet grass, and I have not been asked one question, and I have thought about that every day for a month and I have come to a conclusion about it and the conclusion is that it is not an insult. It is a queue. There is a man at a window with a list and my name comes up and he says the next thing, and the next thing is never a question because a question is not on the list."

---

"You want me to ask you what you need," said Marek.

"I want you to ask me what I need and then I am going to say it and then you are going to tell me that you cannot do it, and I want that in that order, because I have had four men in good coats in six weeks and not one of them has asked and all four of them have told me what I am, and I would like one of them to tell me what I am not."

He asked her what she needed.

She thought about it for about seven seconds, which was longer than he had expected and about four seconds shorter than he had hoped for.

"I do not want fuel," she said. "I want you to understand that, because I have had four men in good coats and two of them had fuel on a cart and one of them had money and one of them had a form, and I do not want any of it, and I have two children and a stove and a season behind me.

"I want a fortnight on this ground. Not on the yard above it and not on the ground across the hedge. This ground, this month, so that the second sowing is not the sowing of a woman who has spent nine weeks being told what she is a witness to.

"And I want that ditch cut. That is the second thing and I am telling you it is not the first thing, and I have known that for four years."

"I can put a fortnight on this ground," said Marek Vale, "and I am going to tell you how and then you are going to say whether you want it, and I am not going to do it before you say.

"There is no form. I want that said first and in those words. There is no form in this kingdom on which a man with no office asks a person to be given a season, and there is no form on which a person says yes to one, and I am not going to invent one, and I am certainly not going to use the paper in your hand, because that paper is the reason you are standing in this room instead of sitting in it.

"What there is, is a board. The district made it and the district owns it and no office in this region knows it is there, and it has nine lines cut in it, and line four is the Counters' Walk ditch, and the ninth month is the harvest month and there is a season works in force on that board, properly made, properly witnessed, in the ordinary way, and it is in force now and it will be until the last night of the month.

"And I can point that at one holding on this board instead of the whole line, for a fortnight, and it will work, because it is in force and I am not making anything up and I am not inventing a power, I am only doing the one thing I can do with something that has already been granted.

"And the cost of it comes out of the ground it is put on. Not this season. This season is now. Next season on this holding, and on no other, because the effect is pointed and I will point it. I do not know what that will be. I have been in this work three years and I have never once known what the cost of a thing like that is until about nine months afterwards, and there is no form on which I could tell you today what it is going to be, and I am telling you that so that in nine months you will not be told it by a stranger.

"So. That is the whole of what I have, and it is a fortnight, and it is on this ground, and the price of it is this ground in the year after, and the ditch is not in it."

---

Odile Marn did not answer for six or seven seconds, and Marek Vale stood in a seed house and found out that he had done it in the wrong order again — the mechanism first, the cost third — and that it had only come out right because she had made him say the cost and had then waited.

"There is a man at the head of this road," she said, "who has worked the ground above mine for thirty years and who came in the seventh month of MR 411 and signed a piece of paper about a wall because I asked him to, and he signed it in a fair small hand and he does not know what he is in and neither do I, and I have never once asked him because I did not want to be a woman who has been told something in a season and goes and asks her neighbours about it in a wet week.

"Which one of those two things is the cost."

"I do not know," said Marek. "I have told you three times that I do not know and it is the true answer and it is the whole of what I have got. I know what a rule is. I do not know what a season weighs when it is put on a piece of ground by a man with no office who has asked one person and not the other, and if I knew that I would be able to tell you that the man at the head of the road is not going to be touched, and I cannot tell you that, and you have to decide it without me."

"Then I am going to have to decide it," said Odile Marn, "and I want you to understand that I have been told that the cost comes out of my ground, and not out of his, and I am going to go and stand at the end of his lane this evening and look at the wall on it and decide, and if I cannot decide then I am going to come back here tomorrow and tell you, and you will wait."

"You do not have to come back. You can decide not to have the fortnight."

"I can do that as well," she said, "and I have, and I have done it for a good ten minutes, and it is the first decision I have made in a month that was not made for me at a window. Yes. Put it on the ground."

---

Marek Vale put his hand flat on line four of a board on a trestle in a seed house and slid it about four inches along the board until it was against the sixth holding on the second rank, which was not the line, and did not say anything, and the ninth month being what it is, the ground came up.

That is the whole of it and it took the length of a breath to do and it is not a thing a man can be trained at, and there is no form on which it may be described, and Marek Vale did not describe it, and there is no office in this kingdom in which anybody has ever been told that it happened.

What a man in a seed house would have said is that the ground came up. It is nine days past the ninth month and the second sowing went into the first week and the second sowing is thin everywhere on the low ground and it is not thin there. The drill went through it at the fourth bell and did not have to be lifted. There is no weed in it. A man of fifty-two who has worked that ground for thirty years looked at it for a moment and then went and sat down on a seed bin and did not say anything for about a minute, and then said that it was early, and then said nothing else about it for a week.

And the ditch was a ditch.

---

"You said *this ground, this month*," said Marek.

"I did."

"The line says *the Counters' Walk ditch*."

"Then the line says the ditch and the ditch is a line of wet grass that has been there since the rains of the year before last, and you have just done four hundred yards of ground behind my wall and you have not touched it, and I am not going to say thank you for that and I am not going to be ungrateful about it either, because it is a fortnight and it is real and I did not have one.

"And I have been down the lane tonight and I have looked at the wall at the end of it and I have decided, and I am not going to tell you what I decided because you will want to write it down."

"That is correct."

"I do not care that it is correct," said Odile Marn. "I have spent a month being told things by men who are correct."

"Two things," said Marek. "The first is that I asked you, and the second is that I did not know what I had until after I had done it, and I have to tell you both of those in that order or the first one is worth nothing."

"Ask me the second thing then."

"The cost of it comes out of the ground it is put on," said Marek Vale. "Not this season. This season is now. Next season on this holding, and on no other, because the effect is pointed and I pointed it. I do not know what that will be. I have been in this work three years and I have never once known what the cost of a thing like that is until about nine months afterwards, and there is no form on which I could tell you today what it is going to be, and I am telling you that so that in nine months you will not be told it by a stranger."

Odile Marn stood at a trestle in a seed house and looked at a man she had met once in a ditch.

"Right," she said. "That is the second time in a month that somebody has told me the true part and the good part and has not bothered with the order."

"And the third thing is the ditch."

"The third thing is the ditch," she said, "and I am going to say the sentence I have been saying in my head since the second of the month and I would like it said in this building.

"A fortnight is not a ditch."

She picked up her apron and shook it out and hung it on the back of a chair.

"I have got a fortnight. I did not ask for a fortnight. I asked for a ditch and I got a fortnight, and in nine months or nine years one of us is going to be sent a bill for the fortnight, and it will not be you, and it will be on this ground, and there is no form in this kingdom on which I can be told that it is coming or not coming, and I would like it noted that I asked for a thing and got a different thing, and that the different thing is better, and that I am going to have to be a person who says that out loud over and over in the next year, to every one of the people who were told the same nine words at a window."

She went back to the bench, and the count went on, and the lamp burned behind her until the seventh bell, and Petrik said a number and did not write it, and Hana Belic wrote it on the bench book with a string across the top of the page and nothing at the head of it, and the wall at the end of the long bench had a sheet of oiled board on it with the days on it and no keeper's name, and about nine people were in the building and as many again on the road outside, and the second sowing on the Counters' Walk came up in a fortnight that nobody in this kingdom is going to be able to explain, and the ditch was a ditch, and the ditch had to be cut, and there is nothing whatever in this basin that can cut a ditch.
