# Chapter 167 — The Wall

The wall at the end of the lane by the dye house is nine feet of brick with a different brick above the line where the rain has been running, and half a dozen people lean a coat against it, and on the morning of the twenty-seventh of the first month of MR 416 somebody wrote a day against the top of it in chalk.

It went down the wall over about two hours. That is the whole of it. There is nothing else in it except what was said on a lane in the cold.

Margit Sova wrote it with a lump of chalk off a kerb and her hand, and her hand is a hand of forty-one that has got worse over twenty-six years at the same job and it is the hand that wrote nine lines of a list into a deal table in the open on the twenty-third of this month, out loud, to nobody.

Nobody gave her money and there was no money and that was said out loud by a man of about forty in one breath and she said that it was correct and she was not going to be paid and would not know what to do with it if she were.

---

"Here is the whole of my reason and I am going to say it once," she said, "and then if anybody asks me a second question about it I am going to say the first thing again, in the same words, and that is a thing I learned from a man with a pencil about four years ago and I have not got a better way.

"There is a woman on that wall who cannot write. That is the whole of it. Her name is Borka and she is about sixty-one and she has been in this lane nine years and she was in the lane before there was a lane, and if I write her name she has a day against it, and if a child writes her name she has a day against it exactly the same, and there is not one thing in the difference.

"And I have written one list in my life. Twenty-six years of what a pitch owes. And I showed it to one man on a market road in the ninth month of last year and he did not write it down, and I have thought about him four or five times since, and I would like there to be a second thing on a wall in this city that is not a list of what anybody owes."

The woman called Borka watched a boy of about nine write her name against a day in chalk. She watched the whole of it and she did not say that it was wrong and she did not say that it was right either, and afterwards she went inside, and she has never mentioned it, and the boy was paid in kind.

---

"You are all going to ask me what it says," said Zlata Hruska, from the two steps, "so I will say it before you ask, and then nobody has to waste a minute.

"It is a rota of days. A day against every name, in chalk, going down. And that is all it is.

"And do not put it together with the rota at the shed in Tallow Ward, and I can hear two of you about to, and you must not, and I will tell you why, and the reason is that one of those is a rota of hours and this one is a rota of days, and a man at that counter in a good coat could take a line off a rota of hours in a moment and there is no line on this wall that anybody can take off, because the whole of it is chalk and the whole of it can be rubbed out with one sleeve.

"That is not a defence of one and a condemnation of the other. I have never been in that shed. I have been told there is a woman in it who has never been thanked and I believe it and it is not my business."

She put her hand flat on the brick.

"And this is not a column with nothing at the top of it, and I want that said by me and not by a clerk in about four years, because somebody in a year or two is going to come down this lane with a piece of paper and ask what the wall is for, and the correct answer is that it is not a column, and the reason it is not a column is that a day is written against every single line on it. Anybody may walk along this wall and say *that is the twenty-ninth*, and be right, or be wrong about it in front of the other nine. That is the difference between a column and a rota and it is the whole of the difference and it took me nine years and a rail to work out."

---

Zvonko Maras, fifty-eight, who drove a coal cart and had signed about a roof over a coal bay in the second month of a year six years ago because a man came with a printed sheet and there was a boy in the household, stood with his arms folded and would not have a day against his name and said so twice.

"No."

"You do not have to give a reason and nobody is going to ask you for one."

"I will give a reason because I have one and because I have got nothing to be doing on this lane all morning." He looked at the wall and not at anybody. "I have read that sheet. Not this one. The one from the bay. And it says a roof. And I have had a roof over a coal bay since I was twenty-two years old and I have never once been under another roof that was not that roof, and I do not know what I am standing behind and I do not know what it cost and it is too late to find out and I am not going to stand in a lane and add a day to it.

"Somebody in this lane is going to come and tell me one day that I was a witness to something in a bay I was working in, and if my name is not on that wall then there is one less thing to argue with. That is the reason and it is the correct one and you may write it down if anybody wants it and there is nobody here to write it down."

That was correct, and nobody argued with it, and his name is not on the wall, and it is not going to be, and on the twenty-ninth a man of about forty asked Zlata Hruska whether the wall was everybody or nearly everybody, and she said that it was nobody, it was a rota, and that there was a man in this city who had a roof over a coal bay and had said no, and that his no was on the wall in the only way a no ever gets on a wall, which is by not being on it.

Drazen Topic, thirty-four, who took a count at the market rail each morning and had signed about a boundary between two market pitches in the sixth month of MR 413, put his own name on it himself, and said the reason, which was that a man who counts ought to be counted, and that was a joke, and four people in the lane laughed and two of them did not.

Jelka Sondor, twenty-six, a laundress, who had signed about a bin behind a chandler's in the growth month of MR 412, went to the coast on the second of the month and has not come back, and there is no form on which a person may be told that they are on a wall, and nobody knew that she was on it at all.

---

"I am on this wall and I want it said out loud why," said Odile Marn. "I am on it because I can read and write and I asked to be, and I have been on a shed list in this city and on a queue slate and in a box in a good coat's coat, and I have not asked anybody for any of it and this time I did ask.

"Being on this wall is the opposite of being a witness. A witness is a person whose name goes on a paper about something else. This is a person putting her name against a day and saying she will be standing in a room, and there is nothing on earth anybody can do to me with that, and I would like it noted that I know there is nothing on earth anybody can do to me with that, because a man in a good coat told me on the sixteenth of this month, on a bank, in one breath, that the box gets filled in.

"That is a true thing he said and it is about boxes and this is not a box. I have got a piece of chalk and a wall and a week, perhaps, before somebody rubs it out and writes it again, and I could be wrong about that, and I have been wrong about most things in the last year, and I would rather be wrong on a wall than right in a queue."

---

They got to the bottom of the wall about the second bell and there were ten names on it and about six days, and three of the days were the days Nim Rook was in the city and not on the road, and the other three were days the women in the lane settled between themselves, and one of the six was put up and rubbed out and put up again inside a quarter of an hour because four people did not agree about it.

And by the tenth name the letters had gone from a tradeswoman's hand to a different thing.

It was not that they were less careful. They were less careful, and that is what a hand does over twenty-six years, and anybody who has kept a book in the same hand for twenty-six years has a page in it somewhere that they cannot read, and most of them have never looked.

Margit Sova stood back from the wall and looked at the bottom four lines and put her hand flat on the brick under the last one, and did not say anything about the first nine.

"That is the end of what my hand does," she said, "and I have got to tell you about that instead of anything else, because I have been writing on walls in my own head since the ninth month of last year and I have not got many words left.

"The next one is not going to be mine. And I have not got anybody to hand it to, and I am not going to sit down on those two steps and choose one of the people in this lane, and I am not going to put a name on it and leave it, because a name on a wall with no hand against it is a name somebody else has to be responsible for, and that is the entire machine and I have been inside it for four months and I know where the wheels are.

"So I am going to say the thing out loud on this lane instead of finding out about it in a room in the second month, and the thing is: I have written ten lines and I have got to the end of my hand, and the day I do not write it is the day somebody in this lane is going to be asked why not, and there is no form on which anybody may be told that they cannot hold a piece of chalk."

Nobody wrote anything down, and there was nothing to write anything down on, and by the fourth bell the light had gone and the chalk had got into the pores of the brick and would still be legible in the fourth month, and none of the ten names on that wall has ever been thanked for anything, and the wall is not a remedy and it does not un-spend anybody, and about nine people in this basin are spent and four of them cannot be found and one of the four is dead, and none of that is on a wall and none of it is going to be.
