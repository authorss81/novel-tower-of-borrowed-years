# Chapter 187 — The Fifth Time the Seat Is Empty

The board of five sat on the twenty-second of the fourth month of MR 416 in the second-floor hearing room in Bell Stair, which is the room it uses, and it was the fourth sitting of this business that anybody had been in, and it had not sat at all in the sixth, seventh, eighth or ninth months of last year, and it had not sat in the first, second or third months of this year, and the reason it had not sat was not on the agenda of any of the three sittings that did happen.

There were five places at the table and there were four people at it.

Bel Harrow presided and was not the Chair of anything and could not vote in a money matter, which is the rule and is nineteen years old and is the reason the money in this kingdom is decided by four people and not by five. Sedge was there for the Chamber and had been on that hill long enough that he was the only man at the table who had been at all of the four sittings. Aldis Kern was there for the ward in his own name, from a step, in the way he came to everything. Zorka Vendl was there for the Fund.

The fifth place was empty, and it had been empty seven times, and this was the eighth, and there is a minute in a drawer in the building in which the words *in the ordinary course* are inside quotation marks and have been for years, and nobody in the room could say who had put them there.

"There is nobody in the fifth place," said Harrow, in the way a man says a thing he has said seven times.

"There has never been anybody in the fifth place in my time," said Sedge.

"That is not the same statement and I would like the second one written down as well as the first." Harrow wrote it down. "Go on, Mrs Vendl. Term two."

---

The work of the morning was done by terms, and term one had nothing in it, and term two was a notice.

Perpetua Ohl had come up the hill at about the third bell and had not asked for anything for nineteen minutes, which anybody who has been at a counter for thirty years can do and nobody else in this city can do, and at the end of the nineteenth minute she had said that she wanted to be heard about a thing she was not going to say she was heard about, and Harrow had written that down too.

"I am not a member of this table and I am not a clerk of the Chamber and I have not got a vote and I am not asking for one, and I have not sent anybody up this hill about this and I have not asked anybody to," said Perpetua Ohl. "I have been at the tower's public witness counter on the causeway side for thirty years. I enter other people's words every working day and I keep a book of them and I have about three hundred used sheets tied with string under the counter and I have never once been asked whether the people in that book were free when they signed."

"Then say what you want," said Harrow.

"I want the board to pay for a notice."

---

Nobody said anything, and Harrow waited, and a notice is the least difficult thing anybody has ever brought into a room, and it took the chair of a table of five nine seconds to work out that it was not the least difficult thing at all.

"Money," said Harrow.

"Money," said Perpetua Ohl. "Paper and the setting of it and the nailing of it up in Rainmarket and on the low-ground road and on the notice board by the chandler's in a lane that has no name on any map you can look at, and I am not going to say a figure because I do not have one, and I am not going to be given one for the same reason I do not give a man from a carting road one, and you know the reason, Mrs Vendl, you were at the counter in the ninth month of last year and a carter asked you the same question I have asked about nine times in five months."

"I remember the carter," said Zorka Vendl.

"I want it printed in the ordinary language of a form," said Perpetua Ohl, "and I want it to say four things, and I have got them written out in my own hand and I have not brought the paper up the hill because I am not going to have a piece of paper of mine in this room and I am going to say them out loud at the ordinary speed and I would like somebody to write them down who is not me.

"One. An attestation is not about a paper. It is about a line, and the line is a thing that stands in the ground or on a roof or at the back of a shop, and a witness on an attestation is a witness to every paper that touches that line while the signer lives, including papers that did not exist when the signer put their name down.

"Two. A signature is a name against a thing. It is not a fact about a moment. It does not say when and it does not say what anybody was doing, and the form takes a month and not a day and that is the form and not the office.

"Three. There is no box on that form, in this printing or in any printing anybody in this kingdom has ever had, on which a person is asked whether they were free. There never has been one.

"And four. Somebody has told about nine people in this basin that they are witnesses. Four of them have been told in this basin. Any person may come to the counter on the causeway side and ask whether their attestation has been produced, and the answer will be given, and I will give it, and nobody is to be charged for asking and there is no form on which a person may be refused."

She stopped.

"That is the whole of it," she said, "and I have been at a counter for thirty years and I have never once put four things on a counter and had them printed and nailed up, and I have not been thanked for the book and I am not going to be thanked for this, and I would like the minute to say that I said so in advance."

---

"Term three," said Harrow. "A notice is a piece of paper that nobody signs. Is there anybody at this table who objects to the word."

Nobody said anything, and Aldis Kern got up off the step, which takes a man of his age about four seconds and which he did not hurry.

"I object to the ward," he said.

"The ward is not being asked for money."

"The ward is being asked to carry it," said Aldis Kern, "and I am the ward at this table and it is my own name on the place and not the ward's, and that was arranged by somebody in about the ninth year and I have never minded it.

"I have been on a step since the flood year. About nine people stopped coming into that yard in the first year of a register that is not a law and has never been a law, and about nine more stopped in the fourth month of last year, and the two lots cannot be told apart, and I have said that in this room before and I am not going to say it again this morning.

"And a notice is a good thing," he said. "A notice about what a person is a witness to is the best thing anybody has put in front of this table in four sittings. And I am going to vote against it and I am going to give the grounds and I am going to give them in my own words and I would like them written down in my own words, and there is a rule about that and I would like somebody to look it up.

"The grounds are that this yard is in Tallow Ward and the gate is at the Gate and the people who stopped coming in it live on low ground in a different quarter of this city, and a notice nailed up in Rainmarket and on the low-ground road is a notice in Rainmarket and on the low-ground road, and I am not going to be the man who carries a notice in his own name for a quarter he does not live in and has never lived in. That is the whole of my ground and I have got nothing else and I am not ashamed of it."

"It is written down," said Harrow, "in your words, and the finding carries it whether you are in the majority or not."

---

"Term four," said Harrow. "I am going to ask one question and I am going to ask it of the member for the Chamber, and I am asking it because I have been on this hill for a long time and I would like somebody to say a thing about a form out loud in a room with a minute in it."

He turned the chair about a foot.

"Sedge. A four-line form is being set in a shop on Ropewalk and about nine hundred of them are going out, and I have been told so by a person in a corridor and I have not been told it in a room, and I am asking you now as a matter of order and not as a matter of opinion, and I would like the answer to be short."

Sedge looked at him for a moment.

"There is no minute on it," said Sedge.

"That is the answer I wanted and it is the answer I expected, and I am going to ask the second half of it and I would like the same shortness." Harrow put his pen down. "Is there any business before this table about the form."

"No," said Sedge. "There is no minute, so there is no business before the table, and a thing with no minute is not a thing a table can be asked about, and I have been on this hill long enough to know that a thing can be printed nine hundred times a week in this city and there is nothing whatever that any of us at this table can do about it, and I am not going to pretend to a person in a corridor that I can."

"Would you minute it now if it were asked for."

"It has not been asked for," said Sedge, "and if it is asked for by anybody at this table then it is a business of this table and I will write it down, and if it is asked for anywhere else in this city then it is a thing somebody has heard in a corridor, and I have been in this work a long time and I know what a corridor is worth."

Nobody asked him anything else and that was the end of term four.

---

"Term five is a money matter and I do not vote in it," said Bel Harrow, "and that has been the rule for nineteen years and it is the single most useful rule this table has, and I am going to say why in one sentence and then I am going to stop saying things: because if the Chair voted on money then the Chair would find out what a thing cost before the four people who have to pay for it had decided whether they wanted it, and there is a kind of man who can only be trusted in a room where he cannot vote, and I have found out which kind I am the expensive way and I am not going to be talked out of it."

Zorka Vendl had the paper in front of her and had read it once and had a finger on a line of it.

"I am not voting against this," she said, "and I want it in the minute that I said so, because Aldis has just given grounds and I am not going to be the second person at this table today who only says the half of it that is inconvenient.

"The Fund's business is a fund. I have got a line for notices in it and the line is not large and the line is for notices that somebody will act on, and this notice is a notice that four people out of nine will act on and five of them will not, and I have worked that out on a piece of paper at the counter on the second of the fourth month of last year and I have not said it out loud in a room since and I am not going to say it now either, and I am voting for it, and I would like the minute to say that the Fund's line is not large and I have said so."

---

The vote was taken and it was three and one, and the fifth place was empty, and it was the eighth time, and the minute said so, and the minute also said the words *in the ordinary course* in quotation marks, and there is a drawer in a building in Bell Stair in which that minute is and nobody has opened the drawer in about four years.

And the board of five paid for a notice.

It was set in the ordinary language of a form, and it was in type, and it was well made, and it went up on the wall by the seed house in Rainmarket and on a post on the low-ground road and on the board by the chandler's in a lane that has no name on any map a clerk in this city can look at, and most of the people in this quarter can read it.

And a notice is a piece of paper that nobody signs, and a notice carries nobody anywhere, and in four years a man in a good coat can produce it at a clerk in a good coat and it will say four true things, and there will be nothing in this kingdom that can take it out of the book, and the five of them who did not act on it are not going to be told about it, and neither are the four who did.
