# Chapter 192 — A Form Cut in a Copy Room

The copy room in Bell Stair shuts at the sixth bell and everybody in it works to that and nobody in it has ever been asked to work past it, and on the fourth of the fifth month of MR 416 the room was cleared at half past five so that a man could lay a forme on the stone without hurrying, which is a thing a clerk does four times in a year and never on purpose.

There were three people in it when the door was shut. The clerk of the Chamber for that building, who had come out of the copy room itself two years ago and had no office above it that anybody could name. A man with no office who had come up the hill because a boy of about nine had carried eleven words and no reason to a door on the causeway side and had been paid in kind and had not been asked about anything.

And, at about the quarter to the sixth, a man with a minute under his arm who put his head round the door and said the second-floor room was not sitting and had not been sitting since the twenty-second of the fourth month and would not sit this month either, and asked whether anybody wanted to know that, and was told no thank you Ivo, and went away.

"Ten thousand a year to say a room is not sitting," said the man with no office, when the door was shut again. "I am not saying it to be unkind. I am saying it because I have been in this building for two hours and you have not said one word about what is on your stone and I have a rule about that which is that I do not touch anything, and I have not touched anything."

"You are not going to touch anything," said Sedge, "and you are going to read out loud, and I am going to cut type, and if I have cut it wrong you will not be able to tell me and I will not be able to tell you, and that is the arrangement and it is the only one there has ever been in this room."

---

There were five pieces of type in a case on the bench and they had come down off Ropewalk on the twenty-ninth of the fourth month in a flat wooden tray with a day on the wrapper, wrapped in a piece of common paper with a printer's string round it, and the string had been cut and not unwound, because a tray in a print shop is not a parcel.

Sedge did not unwound it either. He put the wrapper on the bench with the day on it facing up, and took the five pieces out of the case, and set them on the stone.

*The name.*  *The line.*  *I will speak to any paper touching this line while I live.*  *The fourth, which takes a month and not a day.*  And under a rule, five pieces of type the width of a hand: **the paper.**

The fifth piece was not a sentence. It was two words in type and a ruled space under them about the length of two fingers, and the space was for a person to write in, and the words above it were the only words on the whole sheet that told a person what they were being asked to do.

"It is a blank," said Marek Vale.

"It is a blank with a head on it," said Sedge. "That is the difference and it has taken nineteen years and about four hundred copies and a quarter of a sheet each, and I am not going to tell you the woman who did it did it for a bad reason, because she has never said so and I have not asked her, and I have read the minute about it twice and the minute does not say why either."

He squared the five pieces with the heel of his hand, twice, the way a man does a thing he has done ten thousand times and does not need to look at.

"It was cheaper," he said. "That is all. A line is a line and you do not consult anybody about taking four inches out of a sheet, and she was twenty-four and in her first year and there was four hundred of them going out and the season was not going to wait. I have had the whole of that in a drawer in a print shop on Ropewalk in date order since the third month of a year nineteen years gone and there is a day on the wrapper of it, and I have read the minute and I have not got a better reason than you have."

---

He imposed it at about ten minutes to the sixth, and it took him longer than that, and Marek Vale read the sheet out loud twice at the ordinary speed because that is what is done before a name goes on a page and it is the only qualification he has got, and both times he got to the fifth piece and both times he stopped, and the second time he stopped for longer.

"Read it," said Sedge. "Read the words. That is what the instrument says and the instrument is not going to say anything else while I am standing here."

"The fourth line takes a month and not a day," said Marek Vale, "and the fifth is a blank with two words over it, and if I fill it in I am writing the name of a paper, and there is no rule on this form about what happens if I do not."

"No," said Sedge. "There is not, and there never was, and it went out in about four hundred copies with a blank and the rule and everybody signed it, and I have had the counter book for the ninth month of the year it was cut and every page in it is a page where that blank is empty."

He locked the forme up and pulled one sheet, and the press in the copy room is a small one and it is for repeats and it had not been used for a forme since the spring, and the sheet came off grey and slightly soft on the back and it was perfectly legible.

"There is the fifth line," said Sedge. "In type. Nineteen years after a woman took it out of a forme to save a quarter of a sheet, on a stone in this room, at ten to the sixth bell on the fourth of the fifth month of MR 416, and there is nobody in this kingdom who can be asked whether that is right."

---

"Can I ask you whether that is right," said Marek Vale.

**"I have not said one word to either of you about whether it is right, and I am not going to, and I would like it noticed that I did not."**

He said it without stopping work, the way a man says a thing he has said before in a copy room in a different month about a different sheet, and he did not look up, and after about four seconds he put his hand flat on the stone on either side of the forme and said the rest of it, which he had not said before and which was the other half of the whole of it.

"There is a sheet in a flat case in this building with two names at the head of it and a rule under them that neither may write in the column with the other person's name at the head of it," he said. "I cut that in the seventh month of last year. There is a line at the foot of it and it has been blank since the day I ruled it and neither of them has ever said what it is for and neither of them has ever asked me.

"So I am the one person in this kingdom who has twice been asked to cut a line and has not said what the line is for, and today I have cut the second one, and the reason I am telling you that is not humility. It is that in about four years a man in a good coat can produce this sheet at a clerk in a good coat and the two of them will go in a file together, and I would like the file to contain the fact that I said nothing twice and not once."

---

Nobody said anything for a while, and the copy room was the shape it always was, with the light coming in from the north side and the presses along that wall and the shelves of wire, and at about a minute to the sixth a man came up the hill out of the street and was let in by a boy of about nine who was paid in kind and was not asked about anything.

Sedge gave him the sheet and a penny and he went back down the hill, and the man read the sheet, and then read it again, and then worked.

"The fourth line takes a month and not a day," he said. "That is the form, and it is the oldest fact in this business and I am not going to argue with it. The month is there because the instrument is a line and a line does not have a day in it, a day is a moment and a moment is a paper, and that is why a line is a month and that is the whole of the first four lines and it is correct.

"The fifth one is a blank and the two words over it are the paper. And I have got about ninety seconds and I am going to work it out loud because I would rather be wrong in front of you than right in a room in four years.

"A person signs this at a counter. They sign it about a line, and the line is in the ground, and the line is there whether or not there is a piece of paper anywhere near it. The witnessing is what the paper is for. And the paper that this attestation ends up on is a thing that has not been written yet, because the attestation is what the sealing is done on top of, and a person cannot stand behind a page that is not a page.

"So if the fifth line is a blank, then nobody can fill it in. Not a clerk, not a hearing officer, not a printer, not a marshal. Every attestation signed on this form since the fourth of the fifth month of this year is going to have an empty space in it, and the empty space is going to be the whole of the fifth line, and a man in a good coat is going to be able to write anything at all into it in about four years, including a page, including a person, including a thing that was never written."

He put his hand flat on the grey sheet.

"And that is worse than the version we have had for nineteen years," he said. "I want that said in this room, and by me, and I am not going to be in this room in four years. The version we have had names a line and a line is a thing in the ground and you cannot lie about a ditch. This version has a hole in it the size of two fingers and the hole is the point, and the hole is where a person is supposed to write the name of the piece of paper they are standing behind, and there is no piece of paper."

---

"There is a way to fill it in," said Sedge.

"There is not."

"There is, and I have known it for about a minute and I would like you to hear it from me before you have spent a night on it." He took his hand off the stone. "You name the paper twice. Once before, with the line, in the room, at the counter, with the person standing there. And then again on the same sheet after the paper is written, in the fifth line, with the name of the paper in it.

"A witness is a person who was in the room. A person who signs a fifth line after the sealing is not a witness to the sealing, they are a person who was shown the paper afterwards. And that is not a small difference, and it is the only difference there is, and it is the difference the whole of this business has been not making for nineteen years."

"And the cost," said Marek Vale.

"The cost is that every person in this basin is spent from the day they first sign one of these," said Sedge. "Not from the day they are produced. From the day they sign. Because they will be asked twice, and a person asked twice is a person produced twice, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a person may be produced twice, and you told a room over a dye house in the fourth month of last year that that is the whole of what a spent witness is.

"So I have cut the fifth line back in and the price of the fifth line is that every witness in this kingdom is spent the moment they stand up, and the way anybody stands up twice after that is by somebody asking them to, and there is no form on which anybody may be asked, and there never has been one, and there is not going to be one in your lifetime or mine."

He turned round and got a stub of chalk and wrote on the wrapper the day was already on.

*Five lines. The fifth names the paper. The paper does not exist when the paper is signed, so the sheet is signed twice, and a person signed twice is spent, and the cost of the fifth line is that every witness in this basin is spent on the day they stand up. No form on which a person may be asked to stand up twice. Clerk of the Chamber. Fourth of the fifth month, MR 416.*

"There is a day on that wrapper," he said, "and there is no name on it, and that is deliberate, and it is the only instrument I have ever made with a cost on it and nobody's name at the head of it, and I would like it recorded that I noticed the arrangement and that I did not think of it first and I have not got anybody to thank for it."

---

At the sixth bell the room shut and both men went down the hill, and the forme stayed on the stone with the chase locked and the five lines in it, and the chalk was on the wrapper, and a clerk of the Chamber had cut a line of type back into a form after nineteen years and had said one sentence about whether it was right, and was not going to say it again.

On the counter on the causeway side, about two hours later, in a window a man in a good coat was working in, a young man in a coat from a good shop asked a question about a form and was told, in the ordinary way and without any particular feeling, that it was four lines and a month, and did not know it, and would not have checked.
