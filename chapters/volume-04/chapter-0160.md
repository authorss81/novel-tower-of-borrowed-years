# Chapter 160 — The Counter-Signature

The fourth of the four rooms behind the Threshold was eight feet by eleven and it was not a hearing room and it was not a witness room, and it was the room people were put in when they had to stand and wait, and it had a table planed soft down the middle and a rail round three sides of it and a window in it with a wire across it and a bell on the keeper's side that was not rung for time.

The counter had shut at the sixth bell on the thirtieth of the ninth month of MR 415 and the causeway side of the tower was empty, and there was a lamp in the room and the lamp was on the table because a table lamp is a lamp that can be looked at and a wall lamp is a lamp that has to be found.

Nim Rook had the certified copy in a flat case between two pieces of card, and she had it on the table with the card beside it and not on it, and there was a sheet of plain paper in front of her with a line ruled on it and a box at the foot of the line, and the box was about two inches by one and a half and it had nothing at the head of it.

"There is no head on the box," said Marek.

"There is no head on the box," said Nim Rook, "and there has not been a head on it since I found it, and I have not had it cut, and I want you to understand that I have had nine days in which to have a piece of paper cut with something at the top of it and I did not, and that is the only decision I have made in nine days and I made it in about four seconds."

---

"You are going to have to tell me what this is for," he said, "and I am going to ask it badly, and I am telling you that in advance so that you can answer it the way you answered the four men."

"I am going to stand in a room on the fourth of the next month and say that I am a witness to a thing I was never told the name of and that I do not consent to it," said Nim Rook, "and I am going to say it in about nine sentences and then I am going to hand in a piece of paper, because I have been in four hearings in this city and a person who makes a speech is a person with a grievance, and a person who hands in a piece of paper is a person with a document.

"And the document is that copy. Because the copy is true, and true is the whole of it, and it is true that a name is in a column in a roll in this city with nothing at the head of that column, and there are about nine names in that column and nine people have been told in the last four months that they are witnesses, and I am one of the nine, and I have never been asked one question by any of the nine.

"And that is the only argument I have got, and it is a good one, and it is worth nothing in a room."

---

"A certified copy is a true copy," said Marek Vale. "It is taken on a press and a printer cannot set a copy that does not match the page, because the first thing a proof shows a printer is that the type is wrong. So the copy is true of the page. That is not a small thing and it is not nothing, and you should understand that you are not carrying a forgery.

"And true of a page is not the same as good in a room, and the reason it is not the same is that a copy has nobody behind it. A copy says *this is what the page says*. It does not say *this is what the page means*, and it certainly does not say *this is a person*. To put a copy in front of a room that is going to decide something, somebody has to stand behind the comparison, and standing behind the comparison is an act, and the act has a name, and the name goes in a box, and the box is the one on your paper with nothing at the head of it."

"That is a counter-signature," said Nim.

"That is a counter-signature. That is the whole of it. I read papers and I put names against them, and I have been doing it since I was twenty-one, and it is the one thing in this city I am actually qualified to do, and there is no office in this kingdom that will do it for me, and no office that will stop me either, and both of those are true at the same time and they have been true since the flood year."

"And what is the cost of it," said Nim Rook, "and I am asking that now and I am going to ask it again in a minute if you do not answer it the first time, and I have asked about four men in six weeks and two of them told me the cost and one of them lied about the cost and one of them said *it is only a formality*, and I would like to know what the cost is before anybody moves any ink."

---

Marek Vale did not answer for six or seven seconds, which was the correct length of time and which he noticed, and then he answered it in the order he understood it, which was the wrong order, and which he had worked out a quarter of an hour earlier on a stair in a room over a chandler's shop.

"A chain that is false-witnessed walks to the person who certified it," he said. "Not to the man in the good coat. To the living clerk who put a name against it in good faith. That is the rule and it is the rule that put a clerk in a ward in the flood year and nobody has ever been able to take it back, and I am not telling you the rule to frighten you, I am telling you the rule because you are entitled to be frightened of it and I am not going to do that.

"And what comes off a certifying clerk I do not know. I have been in this work three years. I have watched a chain land once, on a man of twenty, and it came off him in nine days and he is alive and his left hand is a nine-year-old's and it is not his. I do not know what it comes off me. I am telling you that I do not know."

"Then tell me what you think."

"I think it is my word," said Marek Vale.

---

Neris Kade came into the room at about half past the sixth bell and she had not been asked to and she had a coat on over her work clothes, and she stood at the rail on the side with the three feet of it that is not a rail, because a rail round three sides of a room is a thing you stand against and not a thing you sit on.

"I heard there was going to be a counter-signature in here tonight," she said. "I did not hear it from a person. I heard it the way everybody in this city hears things, which is that somebody in a good coat was talking in a yard about a piece of paper and did not notice a woman carrying seed out of it. I have come to watch and not to be asked, and I would like both of those said out loud in this room before anything else happens."

Nobody said them out loud. Nim Rook looked at her for about four seconds and then said, "You are not being asked," and Neris Kade said "No," and that was the whole of the business between them.

Marek Vale did not turn round.

"Neris. I am going to ask her a question and I want you to know that I have not asked it yet, and I am not going to ask it until she has been told what it costs, and if you want to say something to me you have a minute and I am not going to stop you."

"I have about nine sentences," said Neris Kade, "and I am going to use one of them, and you are not going to like it, and it is not an argument and it is not about a policy.

"You have not told her the moment."

He turned round then.

"That is the fourth time you have said a true thing to me that I already know," he said.

"It is the fourth time you have known it a few seconds before I said it," said Kade. "That is the whole of what I have got and I am not going to say the rest of it in this room, because the rest of it is that you have spent three years being careful and tonight you are going to be quick, and I have watched you be careful in nine or ten rooms and I have never once watched you be quick, and I do not know which of the two I would rather see in a man in a room with a pen in his hand."

She put her hand flat on the rail and took it off again.

"That is two sentences and I said I was going to say one. Go on."

"I am going to ask her now," said Marek.

---

He told her.

He told her the cost as he understood it, which was that a chain that is false-witnessed walks to the living clerk, and that he was the living clerk, and that the entry would be false-witnessed in the strict sense because there was a material term missing and the missing term was what the column was for, and that he did not know what the column was for, and that a person who certifies a thing whose subject they do not know has not witnessed anything.

He told her that he had read a copy and not a page. He told her that the copy was true and that true was not good. He told her that he thought the price was his word, and that he did not know, and that "I do not know" was not a defence and was not going to be a defence in the room on the fourth of the next month either.

And Nim Rook listened to the whole of it standing up, with her hands on the back of a chair, and she did not interrupt him once, and at the end she said:

"Ask me."

"I am asking you. Do you want this to happen."

"No," said Nim Rook. "I want a great many things and none of them is that. I have not wanted that since the flood year.

"And I am going to say yes, and I want the true reason said out loud in this room, and the true reason is not that I think you are going to be all right. I think you are not going to be all right. I think you are going to be a man whose word nobody takes, for as long as that paper stands, and that paper is going to be in a room on the fourth of the next month and in a chest after that and then in a file, and there is no telling how long a file is, and I have been in files.

"And I am saying yes because on the fourth of the next month I am going to stand up and say that I am a witness I never agreed to be, and if I say it with a document it is a claim, and if I say it without one it is a woman having a bad morning, and I have been a woman having a bad morning in this city for three years and it has never once been a finding.

"So I am saying yes, and I am not grateful, and I am not going to be, and there is nowhere to minute it, and I have known that since I was sixteen."

---

Marek Vale shook the ink. It was a bottle and it had been on a table for about an hour and the skin had formed on the top of it, and he had to break the skin with the nib and that took about four seconds and everybody in the room watched him do it, which is what happens when a person is about to do the thing that the rest of their life is arranged around.

Then he had to pick a pen, and there were two pens on the table, and one of them had a split nib, and he picked the other one, and that is a thing that is going to be in a file about him for as long as anybody keeps one.

He wrote his name in the box at the foot of the line. The box is about two inches by one and a half and the name went in it and there was room at the end of it and there was room at the top of it and nothing at the head of it.

And then he came to the date, and he had a date to write, and the thirtieth of the ninth month was the day he was standing there, and the line at the foot of the box wanted a date and the box at the head of the line wanted nothing at all.

And he wrote *the ninth month*.

---

"You can only give a month," said Nim Rook.

"Yes."

"That is the form."

"That is the form," said Marek Vale, "and I have known that since the sixth of the sixth month when a woman of fifty-one untied a bundle of about three hundred sheets under a counter on a causeway, and I have had about four months to do anything about it and there is no other date in this kingdom that a person can put in that box, and there is no form in this kingdom in which a person may be given a different one."

He put the pen down flat and squared the sheet with the edge of the table and turned it round so it faced her.

"Read it," he said. "Read the date out loud, twice, at the ordinary speed. That is the rule in a room where a person is dealing with their own paper and it is the only rule anybody in this city has that does not cost anything, and you are going to want to know whether it took, and I am not going to tell you. Read it."

---

Nim Rook read the date out loud, twice, at the ordinary speed.

It was the same date and the same room and the same lamp, and there was nothing in the room that had changed and nothing in the room that had not changed, and the certification was good. It was a counter-signature. A certified copy with a clerk's counter-signature against it is good in a room in this kingdom, and the chain that carries it runs from the copy to the roll to the page to the column with nothing at the head of it, and the column is a column that no person in this city can name, and the person behind it is a name in a fair small hand, and there is nobody at the end of it at all.

She said it twice and it was good.

And that is the whole of what happened in a room eight feet by eleven behind a hall with six intake windows, and there was no bell, and there was a wire across the window, and it was about four seconds.

---

Marek Vale stood at the side of the table and found that he could not remember whether he had eaten at the first bell, and that he had, and that the room was exactly the colour it had been, and that his coat was on a nail on the back of the door where he had put it himself.

He put his hand on the table to steady himself and the table was solid and did not need it, and he understood inside a few seconds what had happened, and it was not a light in his eyes and it was not a pain anywhere. Nothing came up. There was no ink on the paper at all.

He had signed, and a chain had walked to him, and what it had taken was not a year and not a season and not a scar.

It was that the room did not take him.

He had been in this city for three years in which the only thing he had ever had was that a person in a room would hear a name and a date and a number out of his mouth and it would go into the file. He had built a book of four things on that. He had put a sheet about a haulier's road into a coat because a name and a date was a thing that could be spoken.

And at about the seventh bell, in a room eight feet by eleven, in the time it takes to read a date out loud twice at the ordinary speed, a certified copy became good, and Marek Vale did not.

---

Neris Kade came off the rail.

She did not come across the room. She came a foot off the rail and stopped, the way a person stops at a counter.

"I am going to say two things and then I am going to go and stand in the hall until the counter opens," she said, "and neither of them is going to be about how you feel.

"The first is that the certification took, and it took, and I have watched it take, and that is a thing that has not happened in this basin in about nine years and it is going to be in a room on the fourth of the next month and it is going to hold. And you did that, and nobody is going to be able to say it was not you.

"And the second is that you have just made yourself a person who cannot prove that he did it. In about four years a man in a good coat is going to come into that hearing and ask who stood behind the comparison, and there is going to be a name in a box with a month on it and no day, and the room is not going to take it, and there is no form in this kingdom in which *I did that and here is my word* is a thing a man can say in a room and be believed.

"You have put yourself in a file as the man who is not believed. And I am not going to say one word to you about the ninth of last year and about four miles and about not walking, because you know, and because saying it would be cheap, and because you did it anyway and I would have done the same and we are not going to discuss that in a room."

---

Nim Rook put the copy back into the flat case between the two pieces of card and squared the card on it, and then she did not put it in her coat.

"You are going to carry that yourself," she said.

"Yes."

"Then say the other thing first, because I have been on a roof and in a ditch and at a rail since the sixth of the month and I have been owed a sentence for three years, and the sentence is mine and I am going to have it in this room and not on a stair."

"I know what your price is," said Marek Vale.

"Say it."

"The moment you find out anything at all about a name of mine, you tell me."

"You set that on a stair in the flood year when you were sitting on the bottom step with a wet page on your knee and I was three steps above you with my boots wet through, and you said it and I made you say her name twice, and you have had three years," said Nim Rook, "and you have told me nothing.

"Not the bundle. Not the four lines. Not a type face with a different shoulder on the letter *l*, which I heard about from a factor in a print shop nine days ago. Not a hand in a landing a night and a day up this river, which I found out because I went and asked a man in a good coat about it on purpose and he told me because he thought I was somebody.

"You found out about nine things about me in four months and you told me none of them, and you told me none of them *on purpose*, and that is the part I cannot get round, because I have known you three years and I have watched you do it to other people and I thought you did it because you were frightened and tonight I have found out that you do it because you would rather be able to prove it later.

"That is not the flood year. The flood year you were too slow. You are not too slow any more and that is worse."

"Yes," said Marek Vale.

"Good," said Nim Rook, "because now there are two of us who know it and one of us is going to be in a room on the fourth of the next month saying a thing out loud, and the other one is going to be standing at the back of it, and I am not going to look round, and if anybody asks me who stood behind the comparison I am going to look at the man in the good coat and wait for him to find the paper in his own hand."

---

The year turned in the night and there is no bell for it and no office that keeps a calendar anybody in a lane has ever been shown.

The lamp burned until the lamp went out, which on that table was about two hours. The copy went out of the room in a flat case between two pieces of card with nothing written on either of them, carried by a woman of twenty-two who had not been thanked and had not asked. The sheet stayed on the table until the keeper came at the first bell and found it and put it face down on a pile of other sheets, and nobody in that building has ever been shown it and nobody in that building will ever ask about it.

Marek Vale stayed in the room until about the first bell, on purpose, because he did not want to be in the lane, and there was nothing in the lane.

A certified copy with a clerk's counter-signature against it went into the world on the last night of the ninth month, and it was good, and it was the first of its kind in this basin in nine years, and in a room behind a hall with six intake windows a man who had read other people's papers out loud four hours a day on the days the shed was open sat on a bench and worked out that he had spent the one thing he had, and that it had not been taken from him, and that it had been given away by him, in about nine seconds, with a good pen, in the ninth month, with no day on it.
