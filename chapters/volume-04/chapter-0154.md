# Chapter 154 — Five Lines

The print shop on Ropewalk in Bell Stair had the door up at the third bell on the second of the eighth month because the weather was in that state where a shop with a press in it is better off open, and the smell was ink and oak and the particular heat that a shop gets when a compositor has been standing at a case since the first bell.

Iveta Roslak was forty-three and she had the shop, and she had had the shop for nineteen years, and there was a forme unlocked on the stone and a compositor's stick on the bench with about nine pieces of type in it in a row and a proof on the drying rack face down.

She did not stop working when a man with a wet sleeve came in and stood at the end of the stone, which is what a printer does, and after about nine seconds she put the stick down and wiped her hands on her apron and said:

"You are not here about the prices, because the prices are on the board and the board says what I do not do."

"I am here about a form."

"Then you are here about a form and you will have come up that hill for it, and I will tell you before you ask that I have printed about nine hundred things in this room and I remember the ones that were unusual and I do not remember the ones that were not." She went over to the rack and turned a sheet over with the back of her hand. "Say the form."

---

Marek Vale put his hand into his coat and took out a leaf of cheap paper that had been folded in four for about a month and been folded in four again on a causeway, and he opened it on the corner of the stone where there was a clear space, and he put his finger on the third line.

"I will speak to any paper touching this line while I live," he said.

The shop was quiet for the length of one pull of a press.

"Sit down," said Iveta Roslak. "No. Do not sit down, you have not got that far with me. Stand there. I am going to do three things in the order I do them and you are going to watch all three and then you are going to ask me one question and I am going to answer it and I am not going to like it."

She went into the back room and came out with a flat wooden tray about the size of a sheet of paper, and she put it on the bench, and there was nothing in the tray but paper, and the paper was in three sizes and it had been cut, and the edges of it were not cut with a knife, they were cut with something that had been oiled.

"Printers keep one," she said. "That is not a secret and it is not an archive. You cannot set type you have never seen a proof of, because the first proof is the only place a printer finds out that two pieces of type that look the same are a quarter of an inch apart, and a quarter of an inch in a form is a quarter of an inch on every sheet for the rest of the run. So every shop keeps one of everything it has ever set, in date order, and it goes in a tray, and it is the most useless thing in the building and it is the reason a shop can be trusted."

She went through the tray.

The sheets went past for about four minutes. They were a price list, and a docket, and a form for a chandler's guild with a seal on it, and two things in a hand she did not know, and a form for a bond warehouse that had a hole in it where a seal goes, and then she stopped.

She did not take it out. She turned it round so that it faced him, and put her thumbnail on the corner of it, and left it in the tray.

"That is the first one I ever set," said Iveta Roslak, "and it is the ninth item in the tray in date order, and it is nineteen years old this month, and I set it in the third month of that year with a stick and a galley and no compositor, because I was twenty-four and I had had the shop about a year. I have set that forme eleven times in nineteen years and nobody in this kingdom has ever asked me why."

---

It was the same form.

The same paper, which was worse, because the paper was the paper. The same type, which was not the same type, because some of it had been re-cut by a different house and had a slightly different shoulder on the letter *l*, and that is the kind of thing that is in a tray and not in a discussion.

It had five lines.

The first was the name. The second was the line. The third was in type and said the same twelve words it said on nine hundred forms in a bundle under a counter on the causeway. The fourth was a date in a month and not a day.

And the fifth said *the paper*.

"There," said Iveta Roslak. "That is the whole of your four months, and it is not in your form, and you have been up a hill in the rain about a forger, and there is no forger, and there is a compositor."

She let that sit for about four seconds, which is about nine times longer than a printer usually lets anything sit.

"I cut that line. That is my cut. I was twenty-four. I have had this shop for nineteen years and I set that forme in the third month and I remember the afternoon because the light came through that window and I could not see the last two pieces of the line without shading the paper with my hand, and that is what I remember about it, and I want that in whatever you are going to do with it.

"I cut it because it was a half-sheet. There are five lines and you want four, and the four are the ones anybody ever filled in, and the fifth was type and not ink, and it had been type since before I was born and nobody had ever written on it. So it was a length of metal in a forme that made the sheet four inches longer than it needed to be, and every copy of every sheet cost a quarter of a sheet of paper more, and there were four hundred of them going out that month to counters and offices and kitchens all over this region.

"And I did not consult anybody, and I want to be exact about why, because in about four seconds you are going to ask me who I consulted. I did not consult anybody because there was nothing to consult about. A line is a line. You are not removing a right. You are not removing a person. You are taking four inches out of a sheet and putting them into the next form in the rack, and what comes off the sheet is a line that was always empty, and I have had nineteen years to find out what I did and I have not found out what I did."

---

"Tell me what you did," said Marek Vale.

"You know what I did, you have been looking at it for about nine seconds."

"I have been looking at it for about nine seconds and I have not been able to say it, and I have been able to say a great many things for a living."

Iveta Roslak picked up her stick and turned it over in her hands and put it down again.

"Cutting the fifth line did not make the form cheaper to read," she said. "It made it cheaper to print. Those are different and I did not know they were different at the time and I have known every day since.

"Before I cut it, that form had one job and the job was to say that a person would speak to one thing. The paper was on the form. You wrote the paper down. If a man brought that sheet up to a counter in the fourth month of MR 416 and wanted to use it against a water account, a person at the counter could look at the fifth line and see the water account's paper on it, and could say *that is not the paper*, and the man would go away.

"And there is no paper now. The form says *I will speak to any paper touching this line while I live* and the line is a ditch. So the paper can be anything at all, this month or in nine years, and there is nothing on the form for anybody to look at and say *that is not the paper* about, and I did that in an afternoon with a knife and a rule, and I have thought about it in a room of my own about nine times a week for nineteen years, and there is nobody in this kingdom I could say it to."

She wiped her hands on the apron again although they were dry.

"And I am not going to stand here and tell you that I did not know what it was for," said Iveta Roslak. "I did not know what it was for. I do not know what it was for. There is no version of this in which a woman of forty-three in a shop on Ropewalk stood at a forme in the third month of a year nineteen years gone and knew that a line of type she was taking out was a person's whole willingness.

"And that is not a defence and it is not a confession, and if you have come up this hill to find somebody to be angry with then I am the worst candidate in this city and I have been the worst candidate in this city for nineteen years and I have not made a single thing worse by being angry at myself, which is a thing you may put in whatever you are writing."

---

Orren Vaas came in at about the fourth bell with a hood down and rain on the shoulders of a coat that had cost more than the shop, and he said good afternoon to both of them and to the press, which he does.

"Roslak. I want nine hundred of the four-line one and I am not going to tell you why and you are not going to ask me, and I will pay the trade price and not the shop price because I have bought paper off you twice and you have never once shorted me."

"You are not buying nine hundred," said Iveta Roslak. "You are buying nine hundred of a form I have not set since the ninth month of the year before last, and I do not keep type, and getting nine hundred of that forme set again in this shop would cost you about what the shop is worth, and I have told you the price and you have not argued with it, which means you knew that before you came in."

"I did not know that."

"You knew it in the lane," said Roslak. "And you have not argued, and I have told you the price, and that is the end of that, and I am not going to be the person who asks a paying customer what he wants nine hundred of a paper for, because a shop that asks that is a shop that is not a shop."

Orren Vaas looked at the proof in the tray for about four seconds and then at Marek Vale, and said:

"Mr Vale. You will not want to hear this from a factor and I am going to say it anyway, and it is one sentence, and I have never said it to you before and I am not going to say it twice.

"A woman asked me in the second month whether I could find somebody to print a form that had a line on it. I said I did not know anybody who kept type. That was true. I did not ask her which form, because I have been in this trade nineteen years and a person who does not say which form she wants has usually worked out that saying it makes it a conversation."

He put two coins on the bench for the price of a docket, which nobody had asked him for, and went out into the rain.

---

The compositor came back in at about the fifth bell and looked at the tray and looked at Iveta Roslak and said nothing at all about a proof being face up on the bench with a man's finger on it, and she put it back in the tray face down and went through the next nine items in date order without hurry, and the press pulled eleven times, and the eleventh sheet came off wet and went on the rack on top of about nine others, and not one of them had a line on it about which paper it was for.
