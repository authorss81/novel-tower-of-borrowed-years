# Chapter 163 — A Room With a Rail Round Three Sides of It

The counter on the causeway side had a rail in front of it that was not for leaning on, and on the seventh of the first month of MR 416 at about the second bell Perpetua Ohl put the book on the stone under the brass plate, opened it, and read four names out of it, and shut it again before he had got as far as the second one.

"Write those down in your own hand and do not ask me to check them, because I will not check them and there is nothing to check them against."

He wrote them down. He wrote down what she read and he did not ask for a second reading, and the four were:

Odile Marn, thirty-one, of the Counters' Walk, a line of wet grass between two seed yards, in the growth month of MR 411.

Jelka Sondor, twenty-six, of the Counters' Walk, a bin behind a chandler's, in the growth month of MR 412.

Zvonko Maras, fifty-eight, of the Rainmarket low ground, a roof over a coal bay, in the second month of MR 410.

Drazen Topic, thirty-four, of this city, a boundary between two market pitches, in the sixth month of MR 413.

"Four," said Perpetua Ohl. "That is the whole of what I have got, and I have said in front of this counter that there is no form on which a challenge may be refused, and I have never refused one, and I am not going to be asked to refuse this one, because you have not brought me a challenge. You have brought me nothing at all. You have stood at my rail and said four words and I have said four names."

"Then tell me what it costs them and I will not write it down."

"I am not going to tell you what it costs them, because I have never met three of them and I have met one of them, and what I am going to tell you is what I told you on the fourth and I am telling it twice and it is the only thing I have ever said to this counter that I have said before.

"Every one of those four is a person who was produced. And every one of those four is a person who is a person because something went wrong, and there is not one of the four that is standing in front of me today for a reason that had anything to do with being willing.

"The third of those four signed about a roof over a coal bay in the second month of a year six years ago because a man came to the bay with a printed sheet and said the bay was to be let and the roof was the line and there was a boy in the household. He signed it in a fair small hand and he was free and he did not know then what the roof was, because a roof is a roof, and that is not a thing anybody says to him now.

"And that is the reason they are the four, and there are not five more, and I am going to go back to my counter and I am not going to say anything else about it this month."

---

He did not go up the river again. He went to the ninth of the first month, which is five days after a hearing and two days after four names were read out to him under a brass plate by a woman who would not let him keep the book, and it was a morning of flat light with the wind coming off the low ground, and a woman of twenty-two met him at the fourth window at the first bell and said nothing at all and turned and walked.

She went out through the arch and across the bridge and along the towpath for about half a mile, and then she left the towpath and went up between two walls, and at no point did she look round to see whether he was following her, and at no point did she slow down, and Marek Vale understood inside six or seven seconds that if he had not been keeping up she would not have waited and would not have come back.

"There are about four ways in from the towpath," said Nim Rook, "and I am going to use one of them, and I am not going to name the street, and you are not going to ask me, and if you ask me I will say *yes* and then I will not tell you, and that is worse for you than the other thing."

"I am not going to ask you."

"I know that. I said it so that you would have to hear yourself say it, and you have, and I am not going to remark on it."

---

There is no name for it. Not on a roll, not on a rate sheet, not on a plan of this city that a clerk in Bell Stair could pull down off a shelf, and no office in this basin has ever been able to produce one on demand, and two offices have been asked in the last four years and one of them answered.

The people who live in it call the ends of it by what is at them. There is the end by the dye house. There is the end by the wall where the rain comes off, which is four feet of brick with a different brick above the line and half a dozen people who have put a coat against it. There is the end where the steps go down and the water comes up, and there are two steps and a landing and a gate that opens onto nothing, and the gate is open in the first month and shut in about the fourth.

The lanes are wet to the fourth step. There are nine or ten people in them at any hour and about nine hundred in the week. A woman of about thirty-four was standing at the top of two steps with a bucket in her hand at about half past the first bell, and the bucket was not for water.

---

The room was over the dye house and it was reached by a ladder that went up the outside because the inside stair had been a different shape of stair in a different century, and Marek Vale went up it behind Nim Rook and arrived in a room about nine feet by eleven with a rail round three sides of it, and the rail was not the rail from any room behind a Threshold and was not the rail off a table in a counting house in Rainmarket, and it was made of something that had been a bed frame.

Three of the brackets were bought. The fourth was a shoe-tongue of iron, and it was made in a lane by a man who does that, and it did not match the other three, and it was the one that had been put in first.

"I put the rail up in the ninth month of MR 414," said Zlata Hruska, "and there was a man in this room for two days and a night in the ninth month of MR 414 and there was nowhere for him to sit, and he was not a bad man and I am not going to say anything about him, and when he went I went and got a bed frame off a man in the low ground and I put three sides of it up myself over four evenings, and I have never once stood on this side of it, and you may stand on it if you have come to be stopped."

She was thirty-four. She took in mending, and there was mending on the table and on the bed and on a shelf, and a lamp with a wick that wanted trimming, and the lamp was the only light in the room and the room had a shutter and not a window.

"You are the man who reads papers out loud," she said. "I have known that since the sixth of the month and I have known it in the way everybody knows things in this city, which is that a woman said something in a lane."

"Yes."

"Then sit down, and I will tell you what I have, and then you will tell me what you have, and then I am going to say no to about a half of it, and I am telling you the half in advance so that you can use the time while I am saying it."

---

"I have been asking one question at that door since I was twenty-five years old," said Zlata Hruska. "That is nine years.

"A person comes in here with a thing on a counter somewhere, and they have been told something about a paper, and they are frightened, and they want somebody to say that it is not so. And I say one question, and the question is *were you asked*, and I ask it the way you would ask somebody whether they had brought bread.

"And then I tell them the truth, which is that I do not know, and that nobody knows, and that there is nowhere in this kingdom to put it, and that if they go and stand in a room and say *I was not asked*, then a man in a good coat will ask them what they signed, and they will tell him, and that is the end of it.

"And then I ask them whether they would like a cup of something, because it is that time of the day.

"Nobody has ever asked me what question I ask. You are the first person in nine years to ask me what I ask, and you asked it and then you sat down, which is the right order, and I noticed the order, and I am not going to make anything of it."

She picked a sleeve up off the table, looked at the cuff, and put it down again.

"I have never written it down. Not one of them, not once. I know what you are about to say and I have said it to four people in nine years and I have said it the same way every time: a list in a drawer is what a person is living inside, and I am not going to build another one, and I do not want a form, and I do not want a head at the top of anything.

"And I am going to say the other thing now, because you have come up a ladder and you cannot go down it in a hurry, and it is the reason I am going to let you keep talking.

"There is no form on which anybody may be asked to be entered as saying they were free. I have asked four times. I asked a man at a counter in the second month and he said no and he was right. I asked a woman in a good coat in the third month and she said there is no such thing and she was right. So there is no box for it, and there is not going to be a box for it, and every one of us in this city is going to go on being asked whether we signed something, for ever, and the answer is going to be about a ditch, and that is the whole of the machine and I have watched it work on nine or ten people in nine years and I have never once been able to stop it and I have never once been asked to stop it."

---

"What I am offering is a room," said Marek Vale.

"I know what you are offering. A man in a good coat came up that ladder in about the fourth month of last year and offered me the same thing and I let him stand on the other side of the rail while he said it, because I wanted to see how long it took him to reach the part about money.

"So I am going to say the three conditions now and they are not mine, they are the conditions of a room, and I have had nine years to work them out and I worked them out by watching men come up a ladder.

"No employer. Not a person, not a share, not a guild, not a ward, and I know that there is a form in the records office a night and a day up this river that wants an employer written on it, and if you have put an employer on this room then it is a different room and I have not got one and I am not going to pretend to have got one.

"No surety. Not for anything. If a thing in here is going to stand behind another thing, then the standing has to be a person in here, and it has to be their own name, and it does not have to be anybody's idea of them.

"And nothing on the door. No board, no plate, no paper, nothing with a word on it, and no word on it about a witness either, and no word on it about a room.

"And I am telling you before you ask that the third condition is the reason the first one is possible. There is no office in this city that can be shown a door in this city. That is not modesty, that is geography. And that is why a room here cannot be an office's room, and it is the only reason."

---

"Then it is worth nothing," said Marek Vale, and he said it before he had decided to say it, and he heard it go out and he did not take it back, and she looked at him for a moment.

"Yes," said Zlata Hruska. "Say the rest of it, because you have got more sentences in you than that and they are all the true ones, and I have heard nine men say the first one and stop."

"A paper witnessed in this room is good in this room and nowhere else. I have been up the river and I have seen the standing instruction and it is four lines long and it wants a word at the top of it, and there is nowhere in this city to write a word that means this room, because there is no name for the lane and there is no roll and the roll is not anybody's to make.

"And a thing that is good in one room and nowhere else is not a witness, it is an opinion. And you cannot be one either, because you are not in a book, and if a settlement is ever sealed on anything done in this room then every name in this room has to be named by an office, and there is no office that can name you, and there has never been, and two of them have been asked in four years and one of them answered.

"So the room is worth nothing on the day it opens and it may be worth everything or nothing in four years, and I cannot tell you which, and I did not come up this ladder to be able to tell you which."

Zlata Hruska put the sleeve down.

"That is the first time in nine years," she said, "that a man has come up that ladder and told me the price before I have told him the price, and told me the price correctly, and told me that he could not fix it, and did not ask me to help him fix it.

"I am not going to thank you for that. I am not going to thank you for anything and neither are you going to be thanked, and there is nobody in this lane who is thanked for anything, and if you are here nine days you will find out that that is not bitterness. It is that a person who gets thanked in this lane gets a form afterwards, and I have watched it happen once and I was the one who did the thanking."

---

"There is one more thing and it is the last one and it is about you," she said.

"I am not going to be in it."

"I know you are not. I worked that out inside a few seconds of you coming up the ladder and I have been waiting to say it out loud and I am going to say it now, because you are going to make a room and you are going to be pleased with yourself in it, and you are a man whose word this basin does not take, and a man whose word this basin does not take standing in a room is worse than a window, because at a window people expect to be asked things and get told things, and a man in a rail saying *I cannot help you today* is a thing that a person in this lane has been getting all year.

"You can do one job. You can read. You can read a thing out loud to four people in a room with a rail round three sides of it, and reading it out loud in this room is not a witnessing, and it is not evidence, and it does not have to be, because in this room there is nothing being proved. That is the entire benefit of this room and it is the whole of what it is for. A person can say a thing out loud here and be believed, and believed means *heard*, and it does not mean entered.

"Read. That is the job. And I am not going to be a keeper of anything, because a keeper is a person with a name at the top of a thing, and I have not got one, and I am not going to have one, and the day somebody needs me to be a keeper is the day this room is a different room and I am not in it."

Marek Vale looked at the rail for a while.

"A hand," said Nim Rook from the ladder, from about halfway up it, where she had been for the whole of it. "A hand in chalk. That is what it is. A week of it in a lane and nobody can produce it at anybody, and I have been thinking about that on this ladder for about a quarter of an hour and I have not come down and I am not going to come down and say it, and Marek, you are not to write any of this down."

"I am not going to write any of it down," said Marek Vale.

"Say the reason, because you have given me one on every roof and in every ditch since the flood year and I have never once heard you not give one."

"I am not writing it down because a thing that only exists in four heads is the only kind of thing in this city that a man in a good coat cannot produce at anybody, and if I put it in my coat it is a piece of paper in a book of my own, and a piece of paper in a book of my own is not a room. I have learned that from a woman with a broom in the ninth month of last year and I have not been able to put it down since."

Nim Rook sat down on the ninth step, which is not a step, it is the ladder.

"That is the first useful thing anybody has said on this ladder," she said, "and I am not going to say anything else about it, and I have come up here to tell you that I am going to be in this room on the fourteenth and the nineteenth and the twenty-fourth, and those are the days I am in this city and not on the road, and you may put me on a wall if there is going to be a wall, and I will tell you now that I have not been asked and I am not going to be asked, and if anybody asks me whether I was free I am going to look at them until they go and do something else."
