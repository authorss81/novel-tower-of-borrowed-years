# Chapter 152 — The Ditch She Signed For

The boundary ditch between the two seed yards on the Counters' Walk had been cut in the growth month of MR 411 by four men and a boy and had silted up by the rains month of MR 414 and was, on the ninth of the sixth month of MR 415, a line of wet grass with a hedge on both sides of it, and the wall at the head of it had been taken down and built again in the same season, in the same place, out of the same stone, and it had been mended once since, on the near side only, with a course that does not run.

Odile Marn was standing in it with a spade that had a worn socket and a straight handle and a piece of label tape round the shaft about nine inches from the top, where a man who cannot read a label any more and cannot be asked about it keeps the tape because the label was on it when he bought the thing.

She was thirty-one and she had two children asleep in a room over a seed shop and she had been standing in the ditch since the second bell because a man had come at the first bell and told her she needed to be somewhere at about the fourth.

"You are the second one," she said, before anybody had said anything.

---

Marek Vale had come down from the causeway with a wet sleeve and a slate he had not taken out, and behind him at about nine paces came Neris Kade, who is a season examiner for Rainmarket and the uplands and is not a member of anything and cannot vote anywhere, and who had been sent to the same ditch on the same morning by a records office a night and a day up the river without being told why, and who had made up the nine paces deliberately and was not going to explain it.

"The second one what," said Marek.

"The second one to stand in this ditch and tell me I am a witness." She turned the spade over and looked at the label tape. "The first was on the sixth of this month. He had a good coat on and he was very polite and he said my name and he said *the district water account* and he said *it is only a formality* and then he asked me if I would like to hear the terms and I said no."

"You said no."

"I said no to hearing the terms," said Odile Marn. "I did not say no to the thing, because you cannot say no to a thing that has already been said about you in a room you are not in, and I have known that since I was seventeen and I have never found a form that improves it."

Nobody said anything for about four seconds, and in the gap a cart went along the Counters' Walk on the other side of the hedge and did not slow.

---

"Then here is what it is," said Marek, "and I am going to say it in about nine sentences and you are going to stop me at the one that is wrong, and I have been told that is the only way to do it and I do not believe it and I am going to try it.

"You signed a paper. The paper said, in type, that you would speak to anything about a line while you lived. The line was this." He put his hand out level over the wet grass without touching it. "You signed it in the growth month of MR 411. A man from the water office was getting signatures for the ditch and the wall, and you had two children and a wall you wanted mended, and you signed it, and the wall was mended in that same season, and the ditch was cut, and it has silted up.

"Somebody has now put your name in the third of five boxes on a sheet about a district water account. You are a witness to a paper about water. You have never read the paper. There is no line on the sheet you signed that names the paper, and there is no line on the new sheet either, and the two sheets do not have anything in common except your name and the word *line*."

"So it is about the ditch," said Odile Marn.

"No. That is what I have come to be able to say and I have been wrong about it twice in about nine minutes and I am saying so."

"Then say the true one, because I have a spade and a wall and I am going to be here until the rain comes, and I have had four men in good coats in this ditch since the seed month and I have not once had the true one."

---

"It is about a person," said Neris Kade.

Odile Marn looked at her for the first time.

"It is about a person who signed a thing about a ditch," said Kade, "and the ditch has nothing to do with it, and the paper has nothing to do with it, and what is on the paper is your name, and your name is on it, and the only question anybody can ask you about the paper is a question about your name."

"What question."

"Whether you were free when you signed the ditch," said Kade.

The rain came then. It was not a large rain; it was the kind that gets into a coat at the shoulder and down the back of a neck and settles in about nine minutes and is in the ground before a person has decided whether to go home.

"There," said Odile Marn. "You have both said nine sentences to me and neither of you has said what I asked, and I asked it at the second bell and it has taken you a quarter of an hour and the rain has got in.

"What am I to do. Not what is it and not what did you come to tell me. What am I to do, this morning, with a spade and a wall, and a thing I am now a witness to, and no way of being a person who is not a witness to it."

---

Marek Vale put his hand into his coat and stopped with it about half way, and then took it out again, and put it flat on the hedge instead.

"You can go to the counter on the causeway and bring a challenge," he said, "and a challenge is free, and anybody may bring one, and there is no form on which a challenge may be refused. I was there at the second bell and I am telling you that as a fact and not as a comfort, because a challenge is a thing you bring and not a thing that is given to you."

"And then what."

"And then a person reads it out, and asks four questions, and the fourth one is *is there a person behind it who is in this room*, and if there is not then the line does not go in."

"That is a rule in a shed in Tallow Ward," said Odile Marn. "I have heard of it. My cousin carried a paper to it in the growth month of last year and came back and would not talk about it for about nine days and then said that the woman who keeps it does not ask you anything you did not come in and say."

"Your cousin is right and so is the woman and neither of them can help you either."

"Then what am I to do."

"You are to decide whether you want to be a witness," said Marek, "and I have to tell you that there is no form in this kingdom in which a person may be entered as saying they do not consent, and I have to tell you that in about four months a man in a good coat is going to say that you have consented, and he will be able to say it, and the paper he says it on is the paper you cannot read and the box you are in is a box that does not say what it is."

Odile Marn stood in the ditch and let that sit for about as long as it takes a woman to decide whether a man has told her something or has got a piece of paper in his hand.

"You have said that to me as though it is the worst of it," she said. "It is not the worst of it. The worst of it is that I would have signed the ditch paper again, and I would have signed it without a spade, and I would have been free, and everybody would have been able to say that I was free, and there was nobody in that ditch on the sixth of this month to ask me."

Nobody said anything to that either, and there was nothing to say to it, and Neris Kade, who had come down that road to be in a ditch, looked at the label tape on the shaft of the spade for about nine seconds and then at the far hedge, and did not say the true thing, and Marek Vale noticed her not saying it and did not use it.

---

"What I have got," said Odile Marn, at about the fifth bell, "is this."

She went up the bank on the near side and came back with a folded sheet under her coat, and she had had it since the sixth of the month, and she gave it to Marek and did not let go of it until he had it, and when he had it she put her hand flat on it in the air for a second, the way a person does to a thing they are about to miss.

"Copy," she said. "A man brought it and a woman copied it in a book and gave me that and said keep it, and I said where would I keep it, and she said that is a good question and did not answer it.

"So I have been carrying it since the sixth of the month, and I have a spade and a wall and two children over a seed shop and I have nowhere in this kingdom to put a thing I do not want, and that is not a complaint, that is a floor and a chair, and I have had both of them since I was seventeen years old."

Marek Vale turned the copy over.

It was a single leaf of a printed form. It named a district, and a season, and a rate that had not come, and in the third of five boxes at the foot it named a woman, and her name was in a fair small hand, and the fourth line of the copy at the bottom said what the fourth line of every one of these says.

"She has not signed anything about the water," he said. "This is a copy of a sheet and the name in it is a copy of a name."

"I know it is a copy. She told me it was a copy, and she was decent about it, and she told me that a copy is the same as the thing only in a room and that I should not take it out of one." Odile Marn took the spade out of the ditch and set it against the hedge, and stood with her hands empty. "I want to say one more thing and then I am going to mend that course of stone, because it is going to come down in a winter and it is my mother's side of it and nobody else will do it.

"Nobody has ever asked me whether I was free. Not that day and not this one. The man on the sixth asked me if I would like to hear the terms. He did not ask me one other thing. And you have been on a causeway and in a shed and in a printing shop and up a road eleven times and every one of you has asked me what I signed and not one of you has asked me what I thought I was doing."

"There is no form on which that question can be asked and put anywhere," said Marek.

"I do not want it anywhere," said Odile Marn. "I want it asked."

She got the spade and went at the course of stone on the near side, which does not run, and she was not going to be thanked, and neither of the two people standing in a wet ditch was going to say one word to her about the two and a half years it has been since a man from a water office came along a hedge with a printed sheet, and there is no form in this kingdom on which anybody may be entered as saying they did not agree to it, and a wall that was mended in the growth month of MR 411 is a wall that was mended.
