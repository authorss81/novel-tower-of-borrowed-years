# Chapter 186 — Two Things to Certify

On the morning of the nineteenth of the fourth month of MR 416, at about the sixth bell, a settlement marshal stood at the top of a flood bank in Rainmarket for as long as there was a reach to be on, and a foot and a half of good clay stood in a foot and a half of water, and she had been right about it since the sixteenth of the first month and she had never once been wrong about it, and nobody went up and told her so, and she went down the bank at about half past the sixth and did not write anything down.

The next day, Zlata Hruska took a slate down to the public witness counter on the causeway side, and she did not carry it under her arm, and she carried it the way a person carries a thing they are not sure they are allowed to put on a table.

Perpetua Ohl had the book open and did not close it, which she has never done for anybody in thirty years.

"I want a day put on a thing," said Zlata Hruska.

---

"I am not going to guess which thing," said Perpetua Ohl.

"You are not going to guess it because you have not got the time to guess in and I have got about a minute before the queue starts, so I will give it to you in the ordinary language of a form.

"There was a finding on a door-shutter at the end of my lane. It was on the inside and it was on the outside and it was about a foot and a half by four feet. It was read out loud once, standing on two steps, to nine or ten people, on the twenty-sixth of the third month of this year. A man came up the outside of a dye house with a pot on the same afternoon and painted over it, because he had eleven shutters on that side and was going down his own list and nobody had told him and nobody could have told him.

"And there is no copy. The inside went with the outside. There is no third one anywhere in this kingdom. And the reason I want a day on it is that every instrument in this basin takes a month and not a day, and I have got a month and not a day, and a man in a good coat can read a month, and a man in a good coat cannot do one single thing with a day that has not got a month somewhere in the same room."

Perpetua Ohl set the pencil down flat and left her hand on it.

"I cannot do it," she said, "and I am not going to pretend that I have a reason I have not got."

"You have got a reason. Everybody in this basin who says no has got a reason and half of them have not got one and the other half have got it in a drawer. Say yours."

"Three," said Perpetua Ohl, "and the first is the true one and the other two are because I would like them to be true.

"Certification is a clerk's act. A clerk puts a name against a page he has read. I am not a clerk, I have never been a clerk, and there is nothing I have ever entered in this book that anybody could call a page, and I have entered other people's words every working day of my adult life and I have never once been asked to stand behind one.

"The second is that there is no page. A shutter is a board about four feet by two with paint on both sides. It was not a page and it is not a page and it cannot be produced at anybody by anybody including me, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a person may be entered as having read a thing that was read out loud to about nine people standing in the open.

"And the third is the one I would like to be true and am not going to pretend is. I have a book under this counter of other people's words that I have kept since a man asked me about his mother in the ninth year of my time here, and about three hundred used sheets tied with string in three turns, and every one of them was said by somebody at this counter and none of them was said by me, and if I put a day on a painted shutter this month then I will have put a day on the first thing in thirty years that I did not hear at this counter myself, and I do not know what happens to a book when the first thing in it is something you made up from being asked. I have not got a form for that either. I have got thirty years."

Zlata Hruska stood at the rail for a moment with the slate under her arm.

"That is three reasons and two of them are new and I have not got a use for any of them," she said, "and I have been up a ladder for nine years asking one question at a door and I have never once thought to ask a counter for a day, and I have come to the right counter on the first morning I have tried, and that is a thing about me and not about her, and I am not going to say the rest of it in a lane in front of a queue."

"Take the slate away," said Perpetua Ohl. "You have had it out four times this year and you have never once written anything on it, and I have never asked you why, and I am not going to ask you now, and I have a hundred and forty a season to think about and it is not mine."

---

There is one clerk in this basin who can put a name behind a page and there are a great many people in this city who know it, and on the twentieth of the fourth month he came up a ladder in a lane with no name on any map that a clerk in Bell Stair can look at, and two people had come up it before him.

Zlata Hruska told him what she had been told at a counter and he said that was correct and that it was worse than she had been told, because a counter at least enters things, and then she told him the rest.

"I have a day in my head," she said, "and I have had it for a month, and it is the twenty-sixth of the third month, and I was standing on two steps and I read it once and I did not read it twice, and there is a woman of about thirty-four in this lane with a good hand who asked me why I did not read it twice, and I told her that a wall does not read things twice and that the second time is when you are keeping something.

"And the paint went on within the hour. And the people who heard it heard it once, and one of them was a man with a pot who has eleven shutters and did eleven shutters and said he was sorry, and I told him not to be sorry to me about a thing that was mine, and he went down the ladder.

"Nobody can put a day on that. Not a hearing officer, not a counter, not you, and I have asked."

---

"Marek Vale," said Zvonko Maras, from the inside of the rail, where he has stood every time he has been in this room since the first month of this year, "I have got something in my coat and I have had it in my coat for six years and I would like it read out."

He took it out and put it on the table.

It was a sheet of the paper. Four ruled lines, no boxes. The name. The line, which was a roof over a coal bay on the low-ground road. A printed third line in type: *I will speak to any paper touching this line while I live.* A date, which was a month and not a day.

Everything about it was correct. It was well made and it was in type and it had been filled in at a counter by a man of twenty-two in the second month of a year six years ago, and there is no defect in it and there never has been, and the only thing the least of anybody in this manuscript has ever wanted to say about it is that it names a roof and not a paper.

"I want you to put your name behind it," said Zvonko Maras.

---

Nobody in that room said anything for about the length of a sentence.

"I have asked for one thing in this basin in four months and I have never said what it was," he said, "and I said in this room in the third month that I would like somebody to read it out, and that was the whole of what I wanted then, and it is not the whole of what I want now and I am not going to pretend it is.

"I have a boy in that household. He was a boy. He is a man of about twenty-nine and he pulls a truck out of that bay at the first bell and he has pulled it out of that bay every morning for six years and he has never asked me one question about anything and I have never given him one.

"And a sheet with a clerk's name behind it is a thing a man in a good coat can produce at anybody in about four years. That is the whole of what I have learned in four months and I have learned it from four people in four rooms and every one of them was right. And the boy is entitled to ask me. He has been entitled to ask me for six years and he has had no way of asking me, and I have not been able to give him a way, and I have not been asked by anybody to try.

"So certify it," said Zvonko Maras, "and let it be a page in this kingdom that says a man of fifty-eight is a witness to a roof, and let him put it in front of me at the first bell one morning, and I will tell him what I can, which is not very much and is the truth."

---

Marek Vale said yes in about four seconds, and he said it out loud, and he gave the reason before the pen was anywhere near the sheet, and then he heard the four seconds go by and said so.

"A boy of about twenty-nine is entitled to ask," he said. "That is the reason and I gave it before I had a pen and I have given reasons before the pen before, and I heard the four seconds, and I would like it noticed that I heard them, because I have spent four months telling a man in a good coat that four seconds is not long, and there is nothing whatever to be said about four seconds from a man who has never been on the other side of one."

He read the sheet out loud twice at the ordinary speed, because that is what a clerk does before he puts his name to a page and it is the only qualification he has got, and then he wrote his name at the foot of it and it took a second, and there was no ink in the room so he wrote it in a pencil stub she keeps by the door for the lamp.

"Here is the cost and I am going to say all of it and then I am not going to be thanked for any of it," he said.

"One. A counter-signature is the institution's act. It is not mine. I have no office and no standing and I have a marshal's notice on the back of a printed standpipe notice that says a spent witness has no surety and the standing is refused, with a day in the head of it, and I asked her for it. A Deputy of the Chamber entered a finding on the thirteenth of the third month that no answer of mine is available to her as proof of any fact whatever, and I read that finding and I signed it, and I have not gone looking for a way round it since and there is not one.

"Two. And this is the half that has cost me four months and I want it in the air in this room: on the thirtieth of the ninth month of last year I put a name in a box behind a comparison of a copy against a page in a room eight feet by eleven, and the certification was good, and the copy was good, and the room did not take me. And the reason is that when a chain is false-witnessed it walks to the living clerk who countersigned in good faith, and I countersigned in good faith, and there was nothing whatever wrong with my hand or my eye or my honesty on that afternoon, and I have had four months to find a way of making that not matter and there is not one and there is not going to be one.

"Three. Tonight that sheet is a true page with a clerk's name behind it. It will be good in this kingdom in a way nothing in that lane has ever been. And I am still a man whose word no room will take, and I will be that tomorrow, and being a clerk tonight has not changed it, and it is not going to change it the next time either, and I am not going to get a third letter from anybody saying so, and I have had two."

He put the pencil stub back by the door.

"Four. The paper is not false. That is the whole of it and I have had four months to be angry about it and I have not managed it. It is well made and it is correctly filled in and it is in type and it names a roof and not a paper, and a man of fifty-eight signed it at a counter in the second month of a year six years ago and he was not asked one question about it and there is no box on it anywhere in this kingdom on which anybody could have asked him, and there never has been, and there is not going to be one in your lifetime or mine.

"And I have just put a name behind it anyway, because a boy of about twenty-nine is entitled to ask, and that is the best reason anybody in this basin has given for anything this year, including mine."

---

Zvonko Maras took the sheet off the table and looked at the foot of it for a while.

"I did not want that," he said.

"I know."

"I have been in this room five times in three months and I have said no to a wall and to a copy and to a chair and to a man with a list, and I have said no to a great many things, and four of them were in one morning, and I have come up here today and asked for the one thing that makes me a person on a page in a place where a man in a good coat can find me, and I did it knowing, and I have said yes knowing, and I would like it noticed that I said yes knowing, and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to be told I was brave."

He put it in the inside of his coat, on the left, over the heart, which is where a man puts a thing he intends to be asked about.

"He will ask me at the first bell," he said, "or he will not, and either way I have got it in a coat, and there is a woman in this lane of about thirty-four who told me in the second month that a wall in this city is the wall, and there is one, and I put myself on it this morning with a pencil and a slate, and I would do it again and I would like somebody in this room to be angry with me about it and there is nobody, and that is the correct arrangement and it is not a hardship."

Nobody was angry with him about it. It was about the seventh bell by then, and four people went down a ladder into a lane with no name on it, and one of them went down it carrying a page that was true, and one of them was a clerk with no office, and the room asked and the room did not require, and nothing in it was entered anywhere.
