# Chapter 165 — What a Marshal Can Do With a Form

The ladder outside the dye house went up the wall about eleven feet and the top three rungs were bad, and a boy of about nine who lived at the end of the lane took messages for anybody in that part of the city and was paid in kind and was not asked about anything, and he carried a message up on the seventeenth of the first month of MR 416 and came down again without knowing what it was.

Seren Calder came up that ladder at about the second bell on the eighteenth, in the same coat, and stood on the other side of the rail for a moment looking at the room the way a person looks at a thing they have been told about and are now seeing.

"The rail does not meet the wall on that side," she said.

"It does not."

"That is on purpose."

"It was on purpose. It was like that when I came."

"Then I am not going to touch it." She came about two inches inside the rail, which was the first thing anybody had done in that room that was not a form, and put her hand on the table where the mending was and took it off again without disturbing anything. "Now I am going to ask you three questions and I am going to ask them in the order I ask them and I am going to write nothing down, because a marshal's questions are not evidence and I have never pretended otherwise.

"How many people are in this room on an ordinary day."

"Between two and six, and on a day like this one, four."

"And are all four of them named in a book that an office could open."

"No."

"All four?"

"None of the four," said Zlata Hruska. "Not one. Not me and not the three. And I will tell you the three, because you have come up a ladder and you did not have to, and because it is a fact and not a favour: a man of nineteen who is a carter's boy and has a bad chest; a woman of about fifty who is a dyer and has been a dyer for thirty-one years and is in a book in a different city; and a man of about forty who came here in the ninth month of MR 415 because he had nowhere to be stood, and he is asleep now, and he is not a witness to anything and has never been asked to be."

---

"And the fourth," said Calder. "You."

"I am not in a book."

"Then the first question has its answer and I am not going to say the answer out loud, because it is not news to anybody in this room and it is not the thing that decides this."

"Then what decides it."

"The surety," said Seren Calder. "And I am going to tell you the door first, because the door is the reason I am standing in a room over a dye house in a lane that has no name, and I would be a fool if I did not tell you about the door and let you enjoy it.

"A settlement in a public emergency may accept a witnessing that is not otherwise recognised, provided the witnessing was held in a room the marshal has been in. That is the standing authority. It is old, it is narrow, and it is the only way on earth that a paper witnessed in a room like this one can be worth anything, and it is real, and I have never used it, and I am telling you that it is there because if I did not you would spend a month working out whether I had noticed it.

"So the door is open. I put my hand on that table. And here is what is behind it.

"A surety is a person. A person is named. The naming is done by an office, and the office takes the name out of a book. And there is no book that has any of the four of you in it, and there is no form on which an office may make one, and I have asked for a roll for this ground twice in four days — once of the district office and once of a man in a good coat at the counter on the causeway — and I was told both times that there is no district, and both of those men were correct, and the second of them was kind about it and the first of them was not, and I did not correct either of them in front of anybody."

She put her hand on the rail and looked down the lane through the gap where it did not meet the wall.

"Which means that any settlement I seal on anything witnessed in this room is void on the face of it. Not refused. Void. And a settlement that is void is a settlement that has to be sealed again, and there are about four days left in which the ground will take a wheel, and there is no second time in a fortnight and I have known that since the sixteenth and I have said it to nobody until now."

---

"So the room is not dangerous," said Calder. "I want to be exact, because I have been in four rooms in four days and in three of them I was told that the danger was people finding out about each other in a place with no name, and that is not what this is.

"This room is not dangerous. This room is useless. And useless is worse than dangerous, because a dangerous room gets closed and it is over, and a useless room keeps four people standing in it at the top of a ladder being useful to each other, and that is a thing I have had to look at twice in nineteen years and I did not like it either time and I closed both and I was right to close both and I am not going to pretend that closing one was a kindness."

Nobody said anything, and there was a mending on the bed that had been on the bed for about a quarter of an hour and had not been touched.

"I am not going to blame anybody in this room, and I am not going to be thanked, and I would like the two of those put in that order in your own heads and I am not going to check."

---

"And you," she said, and turned round. "You are not a witness to a bank and you are not a surety, and I have had that from the moment I came up the ladder, and I would like to know why, because in four days I have not found one man in this basin who is on a board and is not standing here, and every man I have asked about it had a reason that was about a form, and I have run out of patience with reasons that are about forms."

"I am a spent witness," said Marek Vale. "I produced a comparison on the thirtieth of the ninth month of last year in a room behind the Threshold and the chain walked to me and what came off me is that no room in this kingdom will take my word for anything for as long as that paper stands. It is not a year and it is not a season. It is the only thing I had and I gave it away in about nine seconds with a good pen and I was not careful and I have not been able to think of one thing to do about it since."

Calder stood quite still.

"Say the last part again."

"No."

"Say the last part again and I will do a thing I have not done in four days and I will do it now and it will take four seconds and you will not like it." She took a notice out of the inside of her coat. It was a printed notice about a standpipe on the low ground and the back of it was blank. She wrote on the back of it with a pencil stub, and she wrote quickly, and she put the day in.

"There," she said, and gave it to him. "That is what the marshal's own book says about a man. Day, month, year, the words *spent witness, no surety, standing refused*. And I have never written one of those and I have never had cause to, and I want you to understand what has just happened to you and I am not going to do it kindly.

"Every person in this basin can put a month in a box. You did it. I seal four or five of them a week and every one of them has said a month on it for longer than either of us has been doing this, and there is no other date in this kingdom that a person can put in that box, and nobody has ever printed a second one. There is a date in this basin the size of a thumbnail and it is the same size on every paper in it, and I have been the other way round since I was twenty-six.

"That is not a complaint about you. That is the whole of the difference between my piece of paper and yours, and I have got a day on it, and you have got a month, and in about four years a man in a good coat is going to produce that paper at somebody, and it is going to say a true thing, and there is nothing whatever in this kingdom that can take it off the book. You asked me for it. You would have got it anyway in a year and you would have got it with a name on it that somebody else chose."

She put the pencil back in her coat.

"I am not going to say that I did you a favour, because you have not had one."

---

"The other thing," she said, "and then I am going down that ladder, and I have to be on the reach at the fourth bell.

"You will not get a settlement out of this room. I am telling you that as a fact and not as a threat, and I am not going to come back and shut it, and I am not going to send anybody, and I would like all three of those said out loud in this room before I go.

"Which is that if anybody in this basin finds a way to seal a settlement in six days on sureties that an office can name, on a bank that has to be up in four days, then I will seal it, and I will do it on the standing authority, and I will not read the price box, and I will not read it whatever is in it, and I will not have anybody tell me afterwards that I did not know what was in it.

"I have said the opposite in four rooms in four days, and I have said it to a man with no office and to a woman with a book and to two people who did not give me their names, and I meant it every time and I would take it back now.

"That is the whole of what I have. Take it or leave it. I am going down."

---

She went down the ladder backwards, which nobody does, and at the bottom she stood and looked up at the rail for a moment.

"Tell me the word for what I have just done," she said. "The room, not the bank. I have been doing this nineteen years and I have never had to name the act, and I have a file at the fourth bell that is going to say *the room was considered*, and I would like the verb to be a real one and I would like it to be mine and not yours, because if it is yours then I have taken your word and I have spent enough people's this month."

Zlata Hruska did not answer her, and she said so, and Calder said that she had not expected an answer and had asked anyway, and went and did not look back at the wall.

Marek Vale went down the ladder after her and stood in the wet at the bottom of it and found that he could not tell which way was the towpath, and that it was not that he had forgotten — he had never been able to find it coming in, because a man who is taken up a ladder at a wall and put down one does not get a direction, and there are about four ways out of that lane and every one of them looks the same at the foot of a wall.

The boy of about nine was sitting on the two steps and watched him do it for a while, and then he got up and went ten paces in one direction without saying anything, and Marek Vale followed him, and the lane went the right way.
