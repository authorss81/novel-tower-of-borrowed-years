# Chapter 153 — Two Names at the Head of It

The room on the first floor of a building in Bell Stair that a records office had put them both in was eleven feet by nine with a table planed soft down the middle, and it had a rail round three sides of it and a window with a wire in it and a bell on the keeper's side that was not rung for time, and the keeper was a man of about thirty who came in at the second bell, wrote four lines in a book, and went out again without saying which of them was a day.

There was a sheet of paper on the table between them and neither of them had put it there.

"You said nine sentences to that woman in a ditch," said Neris Kade, "and every one of them was true, and I have been sitting in a room in Bell Stair for two days working out which of the nine was the lie, and there is not one lie, and that is what I have been sitting here for."

"There is not one lie."

"There is not one lie and there is one thing missing out of all nine, and I did not say it in the ditch, and I have not said it since, and I am going to say it now and I am going to be about nine seconds doing it and then you are going to do the thing you always do, which is to be right about the mechanism."

"I will not stop you."

"You read her a paper."

---

"I did."

"You stood at the top of a ditch and read a woman with a spade a description of what had been done to her name in a room she has never been in, and then you told her what the room at the end of it would do, and then you told her what a man in a good coat is going to say about her in four months, and you did it in that order, and it took you nine sentences, and at no point in any of the nine did you ask her what she wanted to do about it."

"I told her what her options were."

"You told her what her options were. That is a clerk's word for what you did and you did not use it and I have been waiting two days for you to use it." She put her hand flat on the table, on the sheet, not on the paper. "You decided what she was going to carry. You decided it in nine seconds, on the bank, on a wet morning, on the grounds that you were the one who knew what was in a room, and you were, and she was not, and that is precisely the shape of the thing that has gone wrong in this basin for three years and neither of us has ever once been the person it happened to."

Marek Vale did not answer for about nine seconds.

"That is fair," he said.

"It is not fair, it is correct, and you are going to do something with it now that is worse."

"I am going to say the other half and then I am going to do something with it, and you are going to hate the other half, and you are going to be right to."

---

"Put a second name on it," said Marek, "and then in about four years a man in a good coat can produce this sheet at a counter in a shed in Tallow Ward and say that the field examiner for Rainmarket and the uplands is a person who stands in rooms and writes down what people say, and there will be about nine people in that shed who will look at me and then look at you, and I will have put you on a piece of paper that has no office behind it, and you will not be able to get it back, and I have watched a yard do that to itself once already and I was the man who asked the question that made it possible."

"That is the other half and it is correct," said Neris Kade, "and I have been in four rooms this year where a name on a sheet was the whole of what was wrong, and I want you to notice that you have just described a cost to me, and you have not asked me whether I will pay it, and I have been standing here for nine seconds waiting for you to notice that."

"I noticed it at about the fourth sentence and I have been holding it since."

"Then do not hold it. Ask me."

---

He asked her.

He asked her badly. He asked her in the voice of a man reading a term out of a form, and he gave her the shape of the thing and the size of the thing and the four years, and he did not say what he wanted.

"You have asked me a question about a cost," said Neris Kade, "and you have told me the answer in the same breath, and the answer you want is no, and I am going to give you something else, and I am going to give it to you because you asked, and I want it noticed in this room that there is nobody here to notice it.

"I will have my name on it. And I want it at the top of the column and not in it, and I want yours at the top of the other one, and I want the sheet ruled so that neither of us can write in the other's column, and I want the rule written on the sheet in one line, and I want the line to be true on the first morning and on the four-hundredth morning, and I want it cut in Bell Stair by a man who will not tell either of us whether it is right."

"That is not a method. That is a rule about a piece of paper."

"It is the only method either of us has," said Kade. "You cannot stop a man in a good coat producing a sheet. You can decide what is on it before he gets there. And a column with nobody's name at the top of it is a column that a man in a good coat can put any name into, and I have watched about nine people try to be careful with a piece of paper and every one of them made a sheet with nothing at the head of it, and there are six places in this basin in that state at this moment and I know the number because I have been in four of them."

"Six is closed. The sixth was hung on a nail by a man in a seed shop in the ninth month of last year on purpose and it may not be a seventh."

"Then it is not a seventh," she said. "It has two names at the top of it, and that is the entire difference, and that is the entire difference in the whole of this."

---

They cut it in the copy room at about the fifth bell, and Sedge cut it, because Sedge cuts things and has been on that hill long enough that twice in it somebody has not wanted to sign something.

It was a good sheet. The paper was the paper the copy room had. Sedge ruled two columns with a straight-edge and a pencil and then went over both in ink, because a ruled line in pencil on a sheet that is going to be carried about for a year is a line that is gone in about the ninth week, and he did not need to be asked about that either.

He wrote the two names at the head of the two columns in his own hand, which is a small clerk's hand that slopes slightly to the right, and he did not ask which name went on which side, and he did not ask which of them was the examiner.

Then he put the knife down and stood there.

"Sedge."

"I have cut it," said Sedge. "That is what I do and I have done it and it is a sheet of paper with two columns and two names on it and a line under the names, and I have not said one word to either of you about whether it is right, and I am not going to, and I would like it noticed that I did not."

He squared the sheet against the edge of the table with his knuckle and turned it round so it faced the two of them, and the two names were at the head of it in the same hand, in the same ink, at the same size, and there was a rule under them in the same hand, and the rule said that no person may write in the column with the other person's name at the head of it.

And underneath the rule, in the space a form has at the foot for whatever a form has at the foot for, Sedge had cut a line and left it blank, and neither of them had asked him for it, and neither of them named it, and nobody in that copy room said the word *column* about it at all.

---

They began on it on the same afternoon, which is a thing that happens and is not supposed to.

Neris Kade wrote four lines in her own column on the ditch and on the two seed yards and on the wall and on the man in the good coat, and Marek Vale wrote nine lines in his own column on the same afternoon about a counter on the causeway and a bundle of about three hundred sheets tied with string and a woman of fifty-one who had said a sentence out loud for the first time in thirty years, and neither of them wrote in the other's column, and at the sixth bell they compared the two columns and found that they had eleven lines between them about four people and that one of the four was a woman with a spade and one of the four was a man of twenty-two whose name was not on either column because he was not in the room.

That one is for later, and it is not later, and neither of them said anything about it at the time.

"You have not written a sentence in your column that is about her," said Kade, at the sixth bell, with her chin about four inches off the paper.

"No."

"She is the one you went up a road for."

"She is the one I read nine sentences to," said Marek. "What she wanted to do about it has not happened yet, and I am not going to write down in a column what a person wanted before anybody has asked her, because that is the thing the wall list in Tallow Ward is for and it is bare and it is bare on purpose and there are six of them."

"You are going to have to go back to that ditch."

"I know," said Marek Vale, "and I am going to go back to that ditch, and I am going to ask her, and I am not going to have read her anything at all first, and I have never in my life done that in the correct order."

The sheet went into a flat case between two pieces of card, and the card had nothing written on it, because a flat case with a name on it is a flat case a man in a good coat can produce, and the sheet has two names at the head of it and a rule under them and a line at the foot with nothing in it, and there is no office in this kingdom that has ever been told that any of this is a method.
