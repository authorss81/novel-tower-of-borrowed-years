# Chapter 157 — Told Twice

The queue on the causeway side of the tower on the second of the ninth month of MR 415 was about nine deep at the first bell and about forty at the fourth, and there was a rail in front of the counter that was not for leaning on, and a woman of fifty-one who had kept that counter for thirty years had put a boy with a slate on the end of it at about the second bell and had told him what to do, which was to count.

"You count the people standing at the rail," said Perpetua Ohl, "and you count them again at the fourth bell, and you count them again on the third and the fourth, and you put the number on the slate, and you do not put anything at the top of the slate."

"There is nothing to put at the top of the slate."

"That is why I have told you not to."

The boy was about eleven and he was the son of a woman who does the tower's laundry and he was being paid four coppers an hour, which is less than the counter pays, and he did it, and on the third and fourth of the ninth month he counted a queue four times and wrote four numbers on a slate in a hand that got worse over four days, and there was nothing at the top of the slate, and nobody ever asked him to put anything at the top of the slate.

---

"There is a man from the water office came down a lane in Tallow Ward in the third month of last year and counted the heads in a yard at four bells for four days on his own initiative," said Marek, "and got a number, and told nobody, and has not been back."

"I know who you mean. I do not know his name." She put her pencil down. "I know that he did it, because a woman in a seed house told me about nine days later and asked me whether it was a thing people did, and I said I did not know, and she said *somebody ought to*.

"Somebody did, three months after he did it, and she told nobody, and I have thought about that about four times a day since the second of this month, and I have not been able to do anything about it except what I have done, which is a boy with four coppers an hour and no head at the top of a slate."

"You have told somebody."

"I have told a man with no office, which is not the same as telling somebody," said Perpetua Ohl, "and I would like you to hear the difference in that sentence, because in about four years somebody is going to come to this counter and say a woman of fifty-one counted heads on a causeway and wrote them on a slate, and the answer is that she counted heads and did not write the count where a person could be shown it, and the second half of that is the only part of it that was a decision."

---

Here is what the four numbers were, and the boy is not to be asked about them and does not remember them in order.

The queue at the first bell on the second of the ninth month was about nine. At the fourth bell it was about forty. On the third it was about thirty, and on the fourth it was about forty-four, and on the fifth it was about twenty-two, which is a number that means nothing at all on its own and is the reason a boy with a slate is better than a man with a good coat.

And the number that mattered was not any of those five.

It was that one woman had been in that queue on the second and had been told that she was a witness to something, and had come back on the fourth and had been told that she was a witness to something else.

---

Marek found it because he did the thing he has always done, which is to stand at a counter and look at the sheets, and there were about nine of them out on the ledge where they are laid while they are waited on, face down, and he turned one of them over and put his hand flat on it the way you put a hand on a form you have read four hundred times.

The ink came up on the paper in sparse lines, the way it comes up, and it was not much, and it was not a sentence anybody had written.

> The same name is the witness and the party.

Then the headache came up the back of his neck the way it does, and the light in the arch of the causeway went narrow at the edges and stayed narrow for about nine seconds and then let go, and he found that he was hungry in a way that had no cause he could name, and that he had eaten at the first bell, and that he could not afterwards remember eating it, or the room he had slept in, or whether he had put the latch down on the door of it.

He sat on the second step of the causeway for about the length of a bell.

It was not a lie. That was the thing he sat with. It was not an omitted page and it was not a figure that did not balance and it was not a hand that did not match. It was a true sentence about a sheet that was entirely proper, in type, correctly filled in, signed by a person in person at a counter, and the true sentence was that the man in the third of five boxes was the same man as the man in the second of five boxes on another sheet, and a man cannot stand on both sides of a line, and there is no form in this kingdom in which anybody may be entered as saying that he was asked.

And there was no way on earth to prove it. He could not produce it. He had a slate in a flat case in Bell Stair and about eleven lines on it about four people, and a sheet of nothing in his coat, and a head-count in a boy's hand with nothing at the top of it.

---

So he did the only thing there was, which was to go and look at the sheets on the ledge with his eyes.

There were nine. Nine of about forty. And the second of the five boxes on seven of them was the person behind the paper and the third of the five boxes was a witness, and on seven of the nine the person behind the paper and the witness were two different names, and on two of the nine they were the same name.

And the same name on two of the nine was not a coincidence either, because a man who is behind a paper and is also the witness to it is not a man who has been chosen twice; he is a man who has been *used* once and has had his name read off the first sheet onto the second, and the second sheet is the one that matters, and there is no form on which anybody may be told that the first one happened.

And the two were the same two people.

He found it by the hand and not by the rule, because a rule would have told him that a witness and a party cannot be the same name, and there is such a rule, and it is the second of the four questions at a counter in a shed in Tallow Ward, and it is not the second of the four questions at a counter on a causeway, and nobody has ever made it be.

---

Her name was Margit Sova and she was forty-one and she had been in the trade twenty-six years, and her chaff stall on the low-ground road had shut four days running in the harvest month of MR 414 and had not opened since, and she was at the rail at the fourth bell on the fourth of the ninth month with a fold of paper in her hand and about nine other people behind her and a boy counting her.

"You are on two sheets," said Marek Vale.

"I have been on four."

"Two of them are on this ledge."

"Then I have been on two of them here." She did not open the fold of paper. "And I have been told four times since the sixth of the month that I am a witness to a thing, and I have said the same nine words four times, and the nine words are *I signed about a bin and not about water*."

"Then you have been told twice about the same sheet."

"No," said Margit Sova. "I have been told four times and I have only ever been told about two, and you have just worked out the other half of that and you have worked it out in about nine seconds and you have not asked me a question, and I am going to say the rest of it because I have been standing at this rail for an hour and there is nobody in this queue who is going to be more useful to you than I am going to be in the next four minutes.

"The first man was in a good coat and he was from the water office and he told me on the second. The second was also in a good coat and he was not from the water office and I do not know what he was from, and he told me on the fourth, and he was on the same morning as the first, and the first was at the third window and the second was at the first, and neither of them looked at the other one, and both of them said *it is only a formality*, in the same voice, the way a man says a thing he has been saying all morning."

She turned the fold of paper over once and did not open it.

"And here is the part I have not said to anybody and I am saying it at a rail on a causeway because I have four witnesses and one of them is counting me.

"I did not know what he was talking about the first time. I have a pitch that cannot be held and a lease that has run out at the end of a season and there is nothing I can do about a lease. That is how I know I was a witness. I did not know at the second. I knew at the third, and at the third I could have gone and found out who the second man was, because that is what a person does, and I did not do it, and I want you to know that I decided not to and that it was not because I was frightened of him.

"It was because if I find out he has been to four people, then I am a person who has been to four people, and a person who has been to four people is a *line*. And I have a box under a counter with twenty-six years in it and there is not one of those years that anybody could use against me, and I was not going to be a line on the fourth day of the ninth month on a causeway in front of a boy with a slate."

The boy looked up.

"I am not a line," said Margit Sova. "Put the number."

He put the number.

---

Perpetua Ohl came out from behind the counter with the book under her arm and shut it, and she looked at the nine sheets on the ledge, and she looked at the woman at the rail, and she said the one sentence she had said in nine years to a man in a good coat, which she had said to a man looking for his mother:

"There is no form on which a person may be told that they have been spent, and you have been spent, and I cannot enter that, and I cannot refuse it, and I am not going to look at the two sheets on that ledge a second time, because the moment I look at them twice they are a thing I have looked at twice."

And she went back behind the counter, and about nine people went on standing at a rail on the causeway side of a tower, and a boy of about eleven with four coppers an hour wrote a number on a slate with nothing at the top of it, and nowhere in this kingdom, on that morning, on a causeway, in a ledger, in a basket on a landing a night and a day up the river, or in a proof in a tray in a print shop on Ropewalk, was there one word about a woman of forty-one and a bin and what it is to be told a thing twice by two men who do not know of each other.
