# Chapter 179 — The Fifth Room Says No

The rota on the wall at the end of the lane by the dye house ran out on the nineteenth of the third month of MR 416 and nobody wrote it again for two days, and the two days were not an argument and not a decision. The chalk had gone into the pores of the brick the way chalk does into a brick that has rain running down it, and a woman of twenty-two put a sleeve against the top line on the morning of the twenty-first and a third of the day came off on her cuff.

"Read it," said Zlata Hruska. "Read what is left and tell me whether you can tell which day it was."

The woman of twenty-two read it and said she could not, and she was right, and she had been a copy-room assistant for a year and a half and could read a bad hand at a worse speed than anybody in the lane.

"Then nobody is going to be able to, and the day goes off that wall a week before the name does, and the name is the part that is any use. That is not a fault in anybody's work. That is what chalk is. I have known it since the twenty-seventh of the first month and I did not say it then because I did not think anybody would be here in the third month to ask."

---

Behind the Threshold were four rooms, three of them with a business and the fourth eight feet by eleven, which is the room a page gets compared against a paper in. The one up the ladder over the dye house was the fifth room in this basin in which anybody had ever sat down on purpose to be asked a question, and the only one of the five with nothing written at the top of a door.

The lane had about nine people in it by the second bell and what took the rest of the morning was not an argument. It was a list of things asked for, one at a time, by people who were right.

The first was a shutter. One had been leaning against a wall nine feet off since before the rota went up, and somebody said that if the rota was on the shutter the rain would not get at it, and four people agreed with that at once.

"Yes," said Zlata Hruska. "Move it. Bottom edge out so the water runs off the front and not behind it, and a stone on the bottom so a wind in the fourth month does not put it in the lane."

And it was moved, and it was the only thing anybody got that day, and it was the least important thing anybody asked for, and nobody thanked anybody, and it was correct.

---

The second was a head. A man had come down the lane on the seventeenth, asked what day it was, been told, and written it on his hand; he was back on the low ground now and going to be asked again, and he could not come up a ladder, and a copy of the wall would have told him without anybody climbing anything. Zlata Hruska said no to that first, in four sentences, and would not be moved.

"Put a word at the top of it, so that when a man from a district comes down here in the fourth month and asks what that is, there is a word. He will be told *days*, and he will write down *days*, and he will put *days* against a wall somewhere in a book, and a day is a head."

Zlata Hruska put her hand flat on the brick under the shutter.

"No. There is not going to be a word at the top of it.

"A column is a thing with a head on it, and a clerk can take a line off one in four seconds and nobody here can say no, because a rule for keeping something is a rule about a person. The day somebody in this lane writes a word at the top of that wall saying what the wall is for, the wall is a column, and the column has a keeper. I told a room in the first month of this year that a keeper is a person with a name at the top of a thing, and I am not going to be one in a lane as well.

"A rota of days has no head. Anybody can walk along it and say *that is the twenty-ninth* and be right, or be wrong in front of the other nine. I said that on this lane on the twenty-seventh of the first month and I am saying it again because he has come from the second lamp to ask for one."

He did not argue. He said he had wanted it for a reason and was not going to give it, and she said that was correct and was not going to ask him for it, and that went no further and was correct.

---

The third was a form, and it came from a woman of about thirty-four with a hand that could write, and it was the best question anybody asked on that lane in two months and the answer was no.

"I want a form for it. I do not care what is on it. I want a piece of paper with a day and a name and the room on it, so that when a woman comes up here with a lease and no witness she can take it down to a counter, and a man at a counter can read a paper. If it is not a form then a woman with a lease has nowhere to go with it, and she will not come up at all."

"A form is a piece of paper that a person signs," said Zlata Hruska. "That is the whole of what a form is in this kingdom, and a form nobody signs is a notice, and a notice carries nobody anywhere. So a form for this would be a thing a woman signs in a room in a lane, and then she has signed a second thing about a day and a room, and in about four years a man in a good coat can produce it at her, and it is a second paper about her on a line that has one on it.

"And there is the other half. A form a woman is handed is a form a woman signs. I have been at a door for nine years asking people one question and have never written one of them down, and my whole advantage is that I am in no book. I am not handing that up so a lease can get a witness.

"So there is no form. The bin still goes up on four backs. A woman with a lease still has nowhere to go. And there is a slate up that ladder with eleven lines on it and a head on the top of it, and none of the eleven is a thing I have made better by saying no."

It was over before the third bell.

---

Then the hand came up, which the lane had been putting off for four months, and had been putting off in the end by a woman of forty-one who said on a lane in the first month that the day she stopped writing it was the day somebody would ask her why not. She was not there.

Margit Sova came down the lane at the third bell with a basket, put her hand under the bottom four lines, and the lane stood back a foot and waited.

"Four days," she said. "Not a fortnight and not a season. I have four days of writing in this hand and then I am finished, and the difference between finished and no is that finished is a day somebody can point at."

"Then somebody else does it," said the woman of twenty-two, and put her hand out, and Nim Rook took it, looked at it, and took it back and wiped it on her coat, which is what a person does before writing on a wall.

She had been a copy-room assistant in Bell Stair for a year and a half before she went on the road, and nobody in that lane had known it, and she said so in the flat voice she says everything in, and then said the thing about her hand before she was asked.

"I am going to say this once and if I am asked I will say it again in the same words.

"I have spent a year and a half copying other people's words onto paper. I am better at this wall than she is." She put a hand flat on the brick without touching the chalk. "And a woman who is better at writing a wall is a woman who will want to correct it. The day I see a line that is wrong I am going to want to say so, and the day I say so the wall has got a person in charge of correcting it.

"So here is the rule, and it is not going on the wall, because there is not going to be a word at the top of it. The hand does not correct. Anybody's hand. If a name is wrong somebody says so out loud in the lane, the person holding the chalk rubs it out and writes it again and does not ask anybody whether they may, and nobody watching says *that is wrong* to anybody but the person holding the chalk. Somebody tell me that is obvious."

"It is not obvious," said Zlata Hruska, "and it is the right rule for the wrong reason. You are all listening to her say she will not correct anybody because she is ashamed of wanting to. The reason is not shame. The reason is that nobody's hand corrects anything. A wall that can be corrected has a person on it who can correct it, and that is a person in charge of it, and that is the head at the top of it that I have refused twice this morning."

She took the chalk off the kerb where it had been sitting in the mortar for two months and gave it to the woman of twenty-two, who put the day against the top and wrote ten names under it, and about six days, and one of the six went up and came off and went up again inside a quarter of an hour because four people did not agree about it. There was no eleventh name and nobody has ever asked.

---

The fifth thing anybody wanted was not a thing anybody asked for, and it was put on the lane by a man of forty-nine with wet hands who had been up that ladder four times since the twenty-second of the second month about a coast.

"Take somebody. Not me. I am not offering and I have not offered since the twenty-second of the second month. Somebody in this lane, on a boat, with a piece of chalk on a gunwale, and it will not be a hire and the hire will not be a lease, and I will row.

"And I am not asking the room. If the room sends a man then the room has a reach. A reach is an area. An area is a district. A district is a line in a book in this city, and a man in a good coat can write one down in four seconds, and the day he does this room is an office and the ladder is a door of an office."

"Then somebody goes on their own account," said Odile Marn.

"Yes," said Zlata Hruska. "Somebody goes on their own account and this room does not send anybody and will not hear about it, and if he comes back with something in his hand nobody here will ask him what it is."

He said that was correct and went down the ladder, and he was gone on the twenty-second and caught the carter out on the twenty-third, and the chandler's door still had a notice on it in the ordinary language of a form.

---

The carter came up the lane at the second bell on the twenty-sixth, before anything else happened there that day, and Zlata Hruska took him on the two steps and let him say it, and it was said once and it is not written down anywhere.

"I am telling you what she said on a quay, and not telling you anybody told her anything, because she was not asked here. She said it nine or ten times and the same way each time, and the first thing she said was that she is not the one who worked it out and is not going to be made into the one who did.

"A man came to that coast in the second month of this year with a printed sheet and asked her about the bin. She told him it was a bin and he wrote it down and went away, and it took her a month to work out that it was a thing and not a conversation, and she has been a fortnight trying to say it to somebody who is not paid to be told."

"And she says a woman up a ladder in a lane has been at a door asking people one question for nine years, and that she has been doing the same at a coast for four months, and that she has asked a dozen at four doors and has not written one down, and she is not going to give you the dozen because you would put them somewhere."

"Who asked her for the dozen," said Zlata Hruska. "Not you. Anybody in this lane."

"Nobody in this lane has ever asked anybody for anything," said the carter, "and that is what she said about this lane, and she said it kindly, and she said that in four days on that quay not one person asked her for a thing, and I am not telling you she is a saint about it, because she is a woman of twenty-six who has been talking to herself since the second of the first month."

Nobody said anything for a while. The bin was on the two steps behind them and there was no word at the top of the wall.

"And she is not coming back."

"No," said Zlata Hruska. "I know, and I am not going to say why she is not, and if she has told you she has not told me, because that would be me asking her a second question, and she has had nine of them off strangers this year and one was a man with a printed sheet and he is the reason we are all standing here.

"The room asked and the room does not require. That is the whole difference between a room and an office, and it has taken me nine years and it took a man of forty-nine a basket and a piece of chalk to say the other half of it out loud this month, and I am not going to have a chair in this room that a person has to come and sit in. So the fourth chair stays where it is, and we are three bodies and a gap this evening, and I said in this room on the seventeenth that the gap is a person and that it is the correct size, and I have had it confirmed from four hours away by somebody who does not know I said it."

---

And on the afternoon of the same day a man came up the outside of the dye house with a pot and a brush to do the shutters, because he had eleven on that side of the building and was going down his own list, and this one was the fourth, and nobody had told him what was on it and nobody could have.

He was on the second from the bottom when a woman of about thirty-four came out onto the stair that comes up through the building and stood on the landing above him and did not come down.

"That is a door-shutter. There is a thing written on both sides of it in pencil, about a foot and a half by four feet, and it is going under your paint."

"Who are you," said the man.

"It does not matter. You are fourth on your own list and there are ten above and below you, and I watched you do the other side in the first month. Tell whoever hired you."

"There is nobody to tell," said Zlata Hruska. "That is why it is on a shutter and not on a board in a room, and you are the first person in nine years to make me say a true thing about why. A finding is a thing somebody wrote because they could not get it anywhere else. I wrote it on my own door in pencil on both sides, and I put it on the outside as well because the door was going to be painted in about nine days, and there is nobody in this kingdom I could have asked to leave it alone, and I put it on the outside knowing it would be painted over, because the two sides are one finding and when the paint is on there is not one of them left.
"So paint it. You are not doing anything wrong and I am not going to stand here and ask you not to, because if I ask you not to then somebody has asked you, and a thing somebody is protecting is a thing somebody owns."

---

She read it out loud in the lane that afternoon, standing on the two steps, once, to nine or ten people, and she did not read it twice, and when the woman with the good hand asked why not she said that a wall does not read things twice and neither does she, and that the second time is when you are keeping something, and the paint would be on within the hour, and that was the answer to whether she was keeping it.

The man with the pot came down the ladder at the seventh bell and said he had not been told there was writing on it and was sorry about it, and Zlata Hruska said he had been told to do eleven shutters and had done eleven shutters, and she was not going to have a man doing a job be sorry to her about a thing that was hers.

And the shutter is the fourth of a list of eleven on the outside of a dye house in a district that is not on any map a clerk in this city can look at, and there is no word at the top of it and no name and no finding, and there is no third copy anywhere in the kingdom, because the one on the outside went under and the one on the inside went with it, and there is no form on which a person may be entered as having read it, and about nine people in a city did read it.

And on the wall at the end of the lane there is a day against every name and about six days and a shutter leaning against it with a stone on the bottom, and the hand that wrote the second set of days is not the hand that wrote the first, and the rule is that the hand does not correct, and it is in four heads and not on the wall, and nobody owns that wall, and the fourth chair is still empty.
