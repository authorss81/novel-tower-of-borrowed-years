# Chapter 22 — The Difference Column

By the second bell on the twenty-third the rain had stopped for good, or long enough, and Tallow Ward was doing the thing a ward does for four days after a flood, which is standing about looking at what it has.

The Gate tank was full to the rim, the screen was holding, and in the low kitchens somebody had found a door that had been under two feet of water and was carrying on with it as though this were an ordinary week. Four of them said the same thing, which was a variation on *you'll want a good coat for the winter*, and a man who owned the door said he would see to it, in the voice of a man who had been saying it since the ninth, and it was not true, and he was not going to stop saying it either.

The second junction was at the top of the ward where the levee met the low ground, and it had a pump-house door and a slate on the wall and a standpipe, and the slate was there because nobody in the Warden's office had ever thought to take it down.

Marda Senn had been at that slate since the sixth of the rains, forty-one times in all, in chalk, in two columns. The left was what the meter gave and the right was what the four yards took, and between them she had chalked the difference in a third space with no heading over it, at every change of watch, and it had never been less than a quarter of what the meter gave and on the sixth and the eighth had been more than half.

And on the twenty-third the difference column was ruled through.

Both lines, all the way down, in a single stroke, and under the ruling, in the squared letters the Warden's office used when it wrote on somebody else's property: *out of use.*

There was a date under that. The seventh. And under the second ruling, in a different ink, a second note. The thirteenth. *Out of use — reset.*

And there was a lamp on the pump-house step, and a Warden's coat on the standpipe, and no Marda Senn.

---

He went up the hill because a Warden's clerk was the only person in Morrowfen who kept a number that the water office would not read, and because that fact had been true for thirteen days and he had not once done anything about it.

The Warden's counting office was on the second floor of the tower, above the copy room, and it was two rooms and it smelled of wet wool, and it had a duty roster on the wall with forty-one names on it in a Warden's hand. Thirty-nine of the forty-one were in.

The junior at the desk was about nineteen and had a cold and did not want to talk to a Chamber clerk. Marek stood in the doorway.

"Marda Senn's counts. The ones that go to the account."

"They go to the account. That is what they are for. Fourteen days of them in a bundle and a slip with a number on it. There is one for the second junction and one for the low kitchens and one for the Underbank standpipe, and I do not know why we have got one for the Underbank standpipe, nobody has ever read that one."

"Which clerk takes them."

"The account desk." He went and got a slip out; it had four columns, a total, and a foot line for *delivered to account desk* and *received, name*. "Last night, at the change of the watch, she had a bundle of eleven days of the second junction, and she carried it down herself, and she asked for the watch because she wanted to ask the account clerk something, and she said she would come back up and finish the low kitchens. She has not come back up."

"Which clerk."

"Grale. Nobody else touches the junction counts."

"Grale," said Marek, from the doorway. "I know the name. He has been on that tray longer than I have been in this hall."

"Then you know more than this room does, clerk, and I have said four things and you have asked me two of them."

---

The account desk was four desks in a row on the north side of the second floor, and Grale was about fifty and had been taking ward counts for a long time, and Marek had known him for four years and had never once had a reason to be in that part of the hall.

Grale was not there.

The desk had a jacket on the back of the chair and a tray of returned slips, and a boy of twenty-two was writing on the returned slips, and Marek asked him what had happened to Grale and the boy said, without looking up, that Grale had gone down to the removed records with a bundle, and would be back, and that he was not to be disturbed.

Down to the removed records.

Marek stood in the corridor outside the account desk and worked out, in about four seconds, that the accounting floor had no lift, that the removed records were three floors below the account desk, and that a man of fifty carrying a bundle of ward counts had gone down at the sixth of the night and had not come back up, and that a Warden's clerk carrying the same counts had gone down before the light and had not come back up either.

He found them on the cold stair.

She was at a landing with her coat off and eleven days of her own hand in a roll under her arm, arguing with a Gallery clerk about the proper form of a request, and she was winning.

"—it is not a request for a record. It is a request for a schedule, and a schedule is not a record, and if you put it in the book as a record I shall be told I asked for a record, and a record is closed to the ward—"

"The ward is not a person," the Gallery clerk agreed, patiently. "Put your name."

The Gallery clerk was about seventy with a key on a string and no interest in ward water whatever, and the room behind him was long and cold and smelled of old paper and wax, and it went a long way back into the dark, and there were shelves.

Marek came down the last four steps.

"Marda—"

"Not him," she said, without turning round. "He is the clerk they offered the ward as its own voice at the door on the twenty-second, in front of three hundred and eleven names, and he is welcome to stand on my stair."

"I came about the counts—"

"He is also going to stop standing on my stair, because I have got the bundle and the clerk here is going to look at a request slip, and if you stand there looking grave at me it will be taken for a Warden and I will lose it." She handed the slip back. "There. One page. My name, my office, the day, and what I want read. He will go and look. He will not be able to tell me anything, and that is the correct behaviour, and I would like a clerk of the day to witness that I said so in advance."

The Gallery clerk read the slip, and said, "Removed," which was the answer to where it lived, and then, in exactly the tone of a man who has said this eleven thousand times: "Twelve years ago or more. If it is not in the index it is in here, and it is not in the index, and I could not tell you which year it went out and neither could my predecessor, and that is not a mystery, it is a filing system."

Then he looked at Marek, and Marek understood that he had been weighed and found to be a person who could be addressed.

"You may stand at the door if you like. Nobody does that. It is the worst view in the tower, it is very cold, and you will not learn anything, and I say that as a courtesy and I would go back up if I were you."

"I have to be in a hearing in a fortnight," Marek said, which was a lie, said because it was the sentence that makes a man move.

"You can stand at the door as long as you like. Not inside. Inside you require a request, and I have one, and it is hers, and it is for a schedule and not for a record, and if I let a second person in on the strength of your say-so I would be doing something I have never done, and I am seventy-one."

Marek stood on the landing for two hours and eleven minutes.

He did not go in. He was not asked to, and he would not have, and the reason he gave himself afterwards — that a man who goes into a room where a document is being read is a man who has entered the document — was a good reason and was not the reason. The reason was that he had four things in a book in his coat, and he did not want to be holding them within thirty feet of a room in which the Trust kept the things that could not be certified, in a building where a copy is a certification and he had made the private half of that book on his own stair at four in the morning.

From the landing he could smell the wax. Twice somebody came out with a tray and went past without looking, and the second time he saw that the trays were flat boards with a waxed cord and no labels, and that was all he learned.

At the fourth bell Marda Senn came up the stair with her coat on and the request slip in her pocket, and he walked down with her because it had begun to occur to him that she was going to be difficult about being walked down with.

She was not.

"The branch is above the yard line," she said, at the turn. "There is a take-off at the second junction chamber, above the ward meter, and it goes up the hill to the Crown Terrace north gardens, and there is no meter on it, and there never has been, because when it was cut the water office said the gardens were on a separate account, and the gardens are on a separate account, and the meter for the gardens is a quarter of a mile the other side of the hill, on the far side of the take-off."

"So the difference is water the ward's line cannot see."

"The difference is water the ward's line cannot see, and it is on the Crown Terrace books, and I have known that since the sixth and I could not get anybody in this building to write down the sentence *there is a take-off above the meter*." She stopped on the landing and turned round. "And here is what I have been sitting with all morning. It did not begin with the freshet. I said freshet to you on the sixth because freshet is what everybody says, and I have been wrong about that twice and I have never been wrong about a number. Read the ninth and the eighth. Six hundred and forty, six hundred and thirty-eight, six hundred and thirty-six, six hundred and thirty-eight. The step is on the second. Everything before the second is noise. On the second of the rains, at about the fourth bell of the night, the left column went up by two hundred and something hundreds of pints in a single entry, between one watch and the next, and it has not come down since."

"And what happened on the second at the fourth bell of the night."

"Nothing happened on the second at the fourth bell of the night. I was at the second junction at the second bell and I left at the third to help the low kitchens, and the next time I put my hand on that slate was at the second bell on the third, and I have gone back over the two columns every hour since, and the two hundred came in between the third bell of the night and the second bell of the morning, and I can give you the watch to within a bell and I cannot give you the cause, because I was carrying pails."

---

The warden's roll is struck at the change of watch, by the senior Warden on duty, in his own hand, and it is a roll of forty-one names and the hours each of them stood, and it is the only document in Morrowfen on which a person can be made to stop existing while still being alive.

Marek got back to the Gate yard at the sixth bell to find a man of about sixty standing at the head of the steps with a slate under his arm and forty people on the yard behind him, and the man was not Ilya, and the man had the Fourth Sluice on his sleeve and the Crown Terrace intake on his coat, and he was reading a line off the roll with his thumb above it.

Nobody in the yard said anything when Marek came up the steps. That was how he understood what it was.

"Clerk Vale."

"Warden Dettmer."

"Her name is struck at the change of watch because she did not come off the platform at the end of the shift, which is the rule and has been the rule since my father's year." He took his thumb off the line. "It is the third time in nine years I have struck a name for a shift not ended. One of the other two is at the Ropewalk standpipe and he is sixty-four, and I struck him, and he came back at the third bell the next morning and asked me to enter it anyway, because the hours were hours. So I entered it."

"Where is she?"

"That is not in a roll. I do not know where she is. I know that a roll has a rule and I know the rule and I have applied it, and I will say to whoever asks me that I asked her twice at the fifth bell and the sixth, and that the low kitchens' count came up the hill with it and I have it in my hand here, so the ward has not lost a count. The ward has lost the woman."

Marek looked at the line. The line was a name in a Warden's hand with a stroke through it and the hour beside it and nothing else, no cause, no note, no place, because the roll has one column for a name and one for an hour and there is no third column and there is no form for a third column and nobody has ever asked for one.

"May I say something that is not in the roll," Marek said.

"You may say whatever you like, clerk, and it will be said to me, in a yard, in the rain." Dettmer wrote the twenty-fourth of the rains in the margin with the hour. "But if you want it in the roll, it cannot go in, and if you want it in a file it wants an instrument, and if you want it in a file I will have to fetch the officer, and then it is a different afternoon and I lose the shift."

"I want it in a yard," said Marek. "Marda Senn chalked two columns on a slate at the second junction for nine days and the difference between them was never less than a quarter of the left column. She said the same thing in writing to the Warden's office twice and it was ruled out of use on the seventh and on the thirteenth. She carried eleven days of it up to the account desk herself this morning. And her counts are going to go on being correct, and there is no longer any person in this city who is in a position to say the word *difference* out loud with a number on it."

Dettmer looked at him for a while with the rain coming into the collar of the coat.

"Her counts go up on the tray with the rest. Grale takes the tray at the change of watch, and the account desk reads the left column, because the account has always read the left column, because the left column is the meter and the right column is four yards full of men who are not paid by the water office." He put the slate under his arm. "So the ward's counts go up and the right-hand column is not in them, because there is no column for it and there never has been one. And now there is not a woman in this building who is the next time anybody asks."

He went down the steps to the Crown Terrace causeway, and Marek stood on the yard steps with forty people behind him and the rain easing, and understood that the two hundred hundreds of pints a night that had come off the ward's own meter and gone up a hill instead of down four yards had just become a sentence that a woman had said at a door, and would from that minute on have to be carried by somebody who was not a clerk.

---

Ilya Senn was on the platform of the Third Sluice at the seventh bell with a hand-wheel in both hands, doing nothing, which was by now the most conspicuous available way of doing nothing in Morrowfen.

He had a lamp and a folded paper in the inside of his coat and he had been on that platform since the second day of the rains, and when Marek told him he did not say a word for a long time. He did not ask what had happened to the valve and he did not ask whether the low kitchens had water, and he asked four questions in a row in the voice of a man filling in a form.

"What hour was the watch due to end."

"The sixth bell."

"Was the count of the low kitchens delivered."

"It was in the hand of the officer who struck the roll."

"Was the roll struck in writing, in his own hand, with the hour against it."

"Yes."

"And is there a form anywhere in this building on which a person is entered as not returned, and where it goes, and who may strike it."

"No," said Marek. "There is a roll with two columns."

Ilya Senn nodded at that for a long moment, with his hands on the wheel.

"Then that is all of it, and there is nothing to appeal, because there is nothing to have made a mistake in." He looked out at the low ground, which was under four feet of the Silt and drying, and where the storehouse at the Underbank mouth was not standing. "And here is what I have been doing with my hands for two hours, clerk, since my sister did not come up the hill, and you may as well have it, because you will not be able to leave it alone, and I have nobody else in this building to put it in front of. I have turned that wheel about nine hundred times since the second of the rains. There is a blister on the heel of my left hand the shape of a coin, and there is nobody on this platform to show it to, and that is all the work is. A man can be perfectly right about a wheel and completely alone with it, and the two facts sit side by side on the same iron for three weeks and neither of them helps anybody.

"And then there is the other thing, and I am putting it in your hands because you will go and get it anyway. The intake at the head of my channel has been drawing short since the second of the rains. Not empty. Short. By the eighth it was short by more than it has ever been short in my life, and it is not me, because the draw down your channel is eleven inches and the head on that bank is three feet. Something has been taking it above the yard line on your side of the hill, and it has been doing it longer than this flood. I have watched that head since the sixth and I cannot put a figure on it, because I am not a clerk, and I am not going to be the man in a room who says a number about a Crown Terrace account."

Marek did not say anything.

"So she has the take-off. She has had it since the sixth, and she was going to write it into a place where it could not be rubbed out with a wet sleeve. And now there is no name on the roll and no column on the slate and no woman in the building who is the next time anybody asks."

Marek stood in the wet on the platform of a valve that had been open for twenty-one days.

"Then I have to find it somewhere she did not look," he said, finally.
