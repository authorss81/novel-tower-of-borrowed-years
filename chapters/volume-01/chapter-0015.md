# Chapter 15 — The Second Document

The reason for a hand-copy is never that a person wants a copy. A person wants a copy because a person has no other way of getting a thing in front of somebody who will be asked to do something about it.

Marek had two unofficial renderings of page three — one in the back of his own book, made in a cold vault on the twelfth with a lamp in his teeth, and one in the levy office's hand made on the Chair step at four in the morning while a boy of twenty corrected two figures out of eleven without arguing. One was a record of his own guilt and the other was a page in a hearing bundle with a boy's name at the foot of it, and neither of them was a document, and he understood that on the fifteenth and had spent a day and a night not doing anything about it.

At the last of the light on the sixteenth he came up the Tally Stair with a sheet of the Tower's own copy paper off the spike, sat down on the bottom step, and wrote page three out a third time.

It took him two hours and ten minutes, because he was not writing it from memory any more. He had the hearing-bundle copy — Kelb's copy — in front of him, and the rule is that a copy of a copy is a copy, and that the two hands must agree or the second one is a second document, and so he wrote in the levy office's hand: the four column headings, the eleven households, the days and the wage, and then *surety*, and the eleven names, and then *witness*, and eleven empty cells, at the same slight pressure in the same places, in the same order, and he wrote the eleven names without hesitating on any of them, because a man who has written a page out three times has a page in him rather than a document.

Then he wrote the four words at the foot.

*Copied from the original.*

A hand-copy is a certification. That is the whole of what it is, and there is no other sense of the word in this building: it says *I have compared this with the original and it agrees*. It is worth exactly what the original is worth, and it is worth nothing at all if the original is a document the Tower may not certify, and there is a class of paper in this building that no clerk may certify, and everything in this copy is in that class, twice over.

The first time is simple. A suspended settlement is not a state that was ever made. The Tower does not certify the existence of a state it does not hold, and it will not make a copy of a held settlement, and that is not obstruction; it is a table in the posting desk's book where a person can look up the meaning of *held* and find it.

The second time is the one that matters, and Marek had known about it since the twelfth and had not let himself know it.

At the foot of the entry's fourth clause, and reproduced in the margin of this sheet because a copy carries the whole of what it copies, is the footnote. And the footnote says, in full: *Held against the standing reserve. The standing reserve is the first entry of this volume.*

And the first entry of that volume is the page on which a standing order of the Yearspire Trust says, in a flat bureaucratic register three hundred years old: *no clerk may erase this signature, and no clerk may copy this page.*

So the text of a page that no clerk may copy is in his sheet. Not paraphrased. Not summarised. The words, in the words.

He took it to the duty desk.

The woman at the posting desk's night chair had been in the copy room twenty-two years and she read the first four lines of the certification clause, and then she read the footnote, and then she put her hand flat on the sheet and left it there.

"How much of that did you know before you wrote it?"

"Since the twelfth."

"Then you wrote it on purpose, which is worse, and I'll say that to the officer and you can't stop me, and I would rather you hadn't made me say it." She lifted her hand. "Clerk, there is no charge for making this. There is no *procedure* for making this. A hand-copy is a certification and a certification is only good against a document the Tower may certify, and there is a class of page nobody may certify, and any paper carrying those words carries the bar with them. A man who makes one of those has not made a copy. He has made a second document. A second document has no standing in a room. Not because it's false. Because nobody in the room is permitted to produce it, including you, including the officer, including a Deputy Steward, and the reason nobody is permitted to produce it is that the order on that page is a standing order of the Trust and it is not one the Trust's own people are above."

"So it is worth nothing."

"It is worth nothing," she said, "and it is worth nothing *legally*, which is a different thing from not being true, and you may put both of those in a sentence in a room and the room will hear the second one and throw away the first. That is the trouble. If you had a lie, I would help you. I have helped people with lies; a lie is a document and a lie has a chain and a lie can be got at. You have brought me a true thing I am not permitted to hand anybody."

He stood at the duty desk and understood, with the particular clarity of a man who has spent six days converting uncertainty into a procedure and has just been shown the exchange rate, that a procedure is stronger than a conviction and always had been.

He could not file it. Filing it would make it an exhibit, and an exhibit in a levy hearing is a document the hearing must address, and a hearing that must address a document produced in breach of a standing order of the Trust does one of two things and neither of them is to look at it.

He could not produce it. He could not withdraw it. He could not even destroy it, because destroying it would be the one act in the whole business that was unambiguously a crime, and everything else he had done was arguable, and he wanted — and could not say how much he wanted — to go on being a man who was arguing.

So he wrote on the wrapper, in pencil, the date, and the fact that it existed, and the words *copy of page three, labour schedule, made from the copy in the hearing bundle*, and nothing else, and no name, because his name on a wrapper was a man's name on a second document.

Then his hand went.

It did not go the way it had gone on the peak. There was no iron taste and no hard edge round his vision. It simply stopped accepting instructions somewhere around the fourth bell of the night watch, and there was a wet pain in the inside of his right elbow, deep and specific and entirely unlike a headache and entirely unlike the nose, and he sat on the bottom step of the Tally Stair and put the sheet face down on his knee and pressed his thumb into the joint for a while and waited for it to be over, and it went off, and came back at the fifth bell for about a quarter of an hour.

Two hours and ten minutes for a page, and then the hand that wrote it stopped working, and there was no form anywhere in the tower that would pay for the hour he had just spent.

"You've been holding that page the wrong way."

Nim Rook was sitting on the ninth step with her knees up and her boots wet through, and had evidently been there for some time.

"I can see that you have," she said, "because I can see the sheet, and I have been in this room for two hours and eleven minutes and nobody has come up this stair, which is not a thing that happens, and I have watched eleven men come up this stair in my life and nine of them were carrying something they were frightened of. Hold it by the edge. The wet's in your elbow, not your wrist. If you have to keep touching it every nine lines then you have already lost the next hour and there is no point starting the next hour."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me, I'm waiting to be paid." She came down four steps. "You've been up here two hours with a lamp and a page and a boy asleep in the low beds, and nobody came up this stair to help you, and do you know why?"

"Because the case is held."

"Because nobody holds the fund that pays for the copy of a held settlement." She said it as if he were extremely slow. "There isn't one. There is no office in this building that is responsible for the copying of a settlement that does not exist, so a settlement that does not exist gets copied by whoever is nearest and gets paid for by nobody. I did the ward's whole levy up four floors in October of last year with a candle in a holder made out of a spoon, and I carried the fuel basin, the tanner's and the Gate yard across a landing for two hours, and there was no fund for that either, and nobody has ever written to me about it and nobody has ever paid me and I have never once asked anybody to, because asking is a thing you can do about money and there is no money in it."

"Who did the levy in October?"

"I did the levy in October," said Nim Rook. "The Tower's night copy of a ward's assessment is a printed run. The Tower's night copy of a *held* assessment has to be longhand, because a held one is not a state, and longhand has to be done by a person, and in this building a person is free."

She sat back down on the step above his.

"So that's my price," she said, "and I've been waiting two days to say it because you were busy. It isn't money. You'll pay me nothing. I'll carry anything, at any hour, for nothing, and I have been doing that for two years and I'll do it for a hundred, and I don't want a farthing and if you offer me one I'll throw it in the basin."

"Then what."

"Her name is Ilen." She said it into the stairwell, not to him. "That's the price. The moment you find out anything at all about a name of mine, you tell me. Not in a week. Not when it's convenient. Not when it's safe. The moment."

Marek sat on the bottom step of the Tally Stair with a wet page face down on his knee and looked at a girl of nineteen sitting three steps above him with her boots wet through.

"Ilen Rook," he said.

She did not move. That was how he knew. Two hours on a stair and she had not shifted once.

"Say it again," said Nim Rook.

"Ilen Rook."

"That," she said, "is the first time anybody has said her name out loud in a room in two years, and I did not ask you to do anything, and I am not going to tell you what it cost me, and I am going to go down four flights now and take that page to the Rainmarket lodging-house for a woman who cannot read it and who will be able to say that it is a copy, which is more than anybody in the tower would be able to say, and I will have it back before the copy room opens."

"You can read it."

"I can read it," said Nim Rook. "She cannot. There is a difference and there is going to be a difference for a long time."
