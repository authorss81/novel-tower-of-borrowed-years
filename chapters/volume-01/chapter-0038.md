# Chapter 38 — Lio's Name

Hesper Rann's room on the eleventh of next month, which was a Sunday, had four framed certificates on the wall and a window onto the water and nobody in it but the two of them, and he had sent for Marek, which he had never done in four years, and Marek had come at the seventh bell in a new coat with nothing in his hands.

"Sit down. Not the chair with the leg. I have not offered that chair to anybody since the twenty-second and I am not going to start with a man under suspension."

"There is nothing you can do for me that you have not written down."

"That is not the worst of it and you have not got to the worst of it yet." He had the Tallow file on the desk, closed, with his hand flat on it, and there was a form face down beside it. "I am going to say four things and then you are going to go home, and I am going to do them in this order, and the order is the order, and I have thought about the order for two days and it is the order that is cruellest and I have not found a kinder one that still works."

He turned the form round. It was the W-4 of the seventeenth of the rains, countersigned, with his name and no other, and *first dry day, the 24th* at the foot in his own square hand.

"It is in the drawer. It has been in the drawer for nineteen days. The twenty-fourth did not hold dry, and the schedule on it is still writeable, and the start date is a man's decision and not mine, and I have not destroyed it and I have not produced it and I have not taken it out of that drawer in nineteen days, which is the longest I have ever left anything in a drawer, and I have had a reason every day and I am not going to give you one."

"You had a reason on the seventeenth and you gave it to me."

"On the seventeenth I had a reason and I gave it to a clerk in a room with no second person in it, and I told you at the time that I would deny it in a room later, and I am now telling you that I will not, and I have never enjoyed that less and it is the only thing in this conversation I am going to be kind about." He put two fingers on the paper. "A form is worth what the man holding it says it is worth, on the day he holds it up. That is not a threat. That is what a form is. Your brother's schedule is eleven days a season at the ordinary wage, and the years are gone and the draw posted on the fourteenth and are working, and the schedule is the only thing left in that contract that anybody in this city can still give away. And it is in a drawer."

"Then produce it."

"I have not decided to and I am not going to today, and you are not going to make me, and if you had been clever enough to make me I should have had to do it and I should have resented you for the rest of my life. Sit down and let me get to the second thing."

Marek sat down.

"Your brother has been a line in a book since he was a boy." Hesper did not raise his voice and he did not hurry. "His grandmother is line seven of eleven on a roll and has been dead eight years and nobody has struck her. His father is somewhere in the water office's mind as a ratepayer. And Tomas has had a look at every arrangement that this building has ever offered him and has decided, each time, that the arrangement is a sentence with his name and a date in it and a wage in it, and that the difference between a man who is owed something and a man who is a *line* is the difference between a person and an entry, and that he would rather keep the vigor and pay it out of a pot than have a witness on him with a date a room can read out loud."

"That is his right."

"It is his right and he has had it since the sixteenth and he will have it on the sixteenth of next month and it is the correct position and I am not arguing with it." Hesper turned round in his chair. "Vale. He did not arrive at it by himself. He arrived at it over a fortnight, from a clerk in a corridor, about a sheet of paper, and the clerk had a mother in this building who said *I am signing anyway* and a brother who said *there is no error*, and both of those went into a room before him. You did not sign and you did not refuse. You asked for it in writing, which was correct, and you left it on my desk, which was not."

"I did not touch it."

"You have not touched it in thirty-five days and it has been in this room the whole time, and your brother has known about it since the eighteenth, and you have never asked him whether he knows."

Marek said nothing.

"So I am going to say the second thing and it is about a woman on a floor I have watched for nineteen years, and then I am going to say the third thing, and the third thing is the one I have been building for three days and I am not proud of it and I am going to do it anyway."

---

"My sister works on a copying floor," said Hesper Rann. "Fifth floor of the fund building, the one over the chandler's shop, the loose sheets and the fair-copy work, and she has been at it for twenty-two years, and she is forty-four, and she comes home at a quarter to six every day and has done since she was twenty-two.

"Eleven weeks ago a man on that floor cut his thumb and sat down on the boards and would not say what had happened to his hand, and the foreman sent him home, and my sister cleaned the board he had been working, because she always cleans the board, and the wax came away and there was a mark on the underneath of it, on the wood, under where his wrist would have been, and she looked at it for a while and then she washed the wax off and she did not tell the foreman and she did not tell me and she has not mentioned it to me once, and it is eleven weeks.

"I am telling you that because I have watched you carry a sleeve for a fortnight and go and push it back on the twenty-sixth because a man in a supervisor's room asked you to, and I have not asked you one question about it, and I have not asked her one question about it, and I am telling you the price of both of those things so that you know what I know." He put his hands flat on the desk. "A person who does not ask a question at home pays for it in the evenings, at a quarter to six, for the rest of her life, and there is no form for that either, and I have looked."

He looked at the file.

"Now. The third thing. I have read the district account of the twenty-eighth, which I have a right to read, and I have read the school register at the Tallow Gate, which I have a right to read, because the school is a public body and the Trust is the payee of the account the school comes off. Not the roll. The register. The roll is the levy. The register is the school."

He did not have a paper in his hand and he did not need one.

"Vale, Lio. Off from the twenty-fourth of the rains. Back on the first of this month, for the week the difference in the record bought. Off again on the sixth, and it is shut this morning because it is a Sunday, and it will be shut again on the fourteenth and the term is suspended. That is her year. There is no more of it than that and it is the whole of the year of a child of nine who has done nothing at all."

The rain came on the window.

"I am not going to be able to do anything about that," said Hesper Rann, "and I have found that I do not much enjoy having looked at it. I looked at it on the Friday and I have not slept properly since, and I want you to know that it cost me something, and I am telling you that so that you cannot tell anybody afterwards that I did not know what I was doing."

"You have read a child's name in a register."

"I have read four. There are eleven children on that register and the school has ninety-one and I have read the eleven and the four are the ones a levy is supposed to be able to move, and the reason it can move those four is that the school is the last line on an order in a frame, and the order gets cheaper as it goes down, and the ward's levy days have not been drawn yet, and when they are drawn that is a line above the school." He did not move. "So your daughter is the cheapest single thing in Tallow Ward. That is not an opinion and it is not a threat. It is the position of the last line on a page."

"Hesper—"

"And here is the offer, and it is the fourth thing, and I am going to make it in the same words I used on the seventeenth, because I am not a man who rewrites his own offers, and because I want you to be able to say afterwards that I did not come at you new." Hesper turned the W-4 round again and put a finger on it. "Take the schedule. Take it, and take it in writing, and tell your brother I have said so, and I will enter your household's assessment at the close of the quarter on a figure I choose, and I will do it as a supervisor of the settlement desk, which I have done four times in nineteen years, and Grale would know, and Grale will not be asked. Your household off the account for a year. Not the ward. Yours. Nine days for Lio, a term for your brother, and a woman on Sill Road gets eleven years back, and none of it is in the rules, and all of it is in a drawer."

The lamp came on along the north wall outside, which it did not do on a Sunday, and neither of them mentioned it.

"You have not said the fourth thing about tomorrow," said Marek.

"I have said the third thing four times and I have said tomorrow not at all, because you did not ask me and I am not going to volunteer it." Hesper picked up the form and put it back in the drawer and shut the drawer, which was the sound the whole conversation turned on. "You have a book with four things in it and a room where a woman has a sheet and a chair and a notice on the board with a date on it, and I have wanted you to get to tomorrow for a week, and I have not helped you with it once, and you did not ask, and I want it understood between us that we are both very careful people and that neither of us has done a single unkind thing on purpose in this room."

"You have told me my daughter's name is on a register in a room with no second person in it."

"I have," said Hesper Rann. "In a room with no second person in it, in a form with no copy, on a Sunday, and there is no instrument for any of it, and I have gone to the trouble of not writing one word down, and I have done that on purpose, and it is the first dishonest thing I have done in this office in nineteen years and I have got nothing out of it except that you will not sleep, and I can live with that."

He got up and put his coat on over his arm.

"Send for me if you want to talk about the schedule," he said. "I will not send for you. And I will not be unkind to you again this month unless you open a file, and if you do open a file, that will be in a room with a second person in it, and I have spent nineteen years learning which of those two things a man can survive."

And Marek Vale sat in a supervisor's room on the second floor of the Yearspire on a Sunday evening at the seventh bell with a drawer shut across the room from him and a form in it that he had wanted for thirty-five days, and understood the whole shape of what had just been done to him, and could not prove one word of it, and had a night in front of him in which nothing at all could be done about any of it, and the date on the notice at the bottom of the hill was the morning after tomorrow.
