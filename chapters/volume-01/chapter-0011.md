# Chapter 11 — What the Years Cost

The lane behind the Morrowfen clinic held eleven people who had been there since the small hours and did not go in the front, because the front was for people who were walking, and because a person who has been standing in the wet since three in the morning is a different sort of animal from one who came in on a stretcher an hour ago.

Marek came up it with the ward's levy still under his arm, because he had carried it out of the copy room and had not put it anywhere, and because putting it somewhere would have meant deciding what it was.

Sera Holt was in the surgery doorway with a lamp in one hand and a basin under her arm and no patience whatsoever left in her face.

"You've cut your arm," she said.

"Yesterday."

"Then it's dirty." She put the basin down and took his wrist and turned it to the light, and the skin along the inside of his forearm was a raised seam four inches long and slightly angry at both ends, and it had been opened, drained, and closed again by somebody competent. "Who did that."

"I did."

"You did it with what."

"I don't remember."

"That is not an answer to a surgeon's question."

"I was holding a page down for six hours," Marek said. "And the answer to the first question is no, and I am not going to ask it twice, because I can see it in your face and it is going to cost me the same the second time."

Sera Holt looked at him for a moment longer than a tired woman needs to, and then she made a sound that was almost a laugh and did not arrive.

"There is no form," she said.

That was the whole of it. She had understood the question before he finished asking it and she had answered it in three words, and there was not going to be any more of it, and the three words were going to sit in him for the rest of the year.

"No form," Marek said.

"You have been in this building four years." She let go of his arm. "You have got an entry for a suspended settlement, which is a thing that has no state, and a boy in my low bed whose hand is worth what it will be worth in eight years, and you have come up my lane to ask me whether the tower has a schedule for putting a boy's years back into a hand. There is no form. There has never been a form. There is a book in the vaults on the second floor where they keep every yearline mark anybody has ever asked about, and a man called Osk who keeps it, and Osk does not keep a fee, because the tower never decided that a person should be charged for being damaged. You may go and look at your own in it. You may not pay anybody's.

"Then somebody pays.

"Somebody pays," Sera said, "in money or in time or in nothing at all, and it is never the same somebody, and the reason you cannot find the form is that there is no office that owes this. There is no trust. There is no reserve. It is a *gap*, and the tower is very good at gaps, because a gap is a thing it can point a settlement at when it has finished with you." She picked the basin up. "Go and see him about your arm at noon and not now, and do not take your coat off in my corridor. I have nine people in there who came in last night with cuts they got carrying other people's furniture, and they are being treated free, and they do not need to watch a clerk be comfortable."

He went out and sat on the bench at the end of the lane with the levy on his knees.

Inside the waiting room, which held fifty people and was holding about thirty, a woman of forty-three was sitting in the fourth chair from the door with her hands in her lap and a folded sheet of paper in both of them, and she had been sitting in that chair since before the lamps were lit.

He knew who she was because a nurse had told him at four o'clock in the morning that the boy's mother was in the fourth chair and had asked for the fourth chair specifically, and would not change it, and had said that if anybody came to move her she would like to be told first.

That was the part that undid him. She had asked for the fourth chair.

He stood in the doorway for a while and then went in.

"Mrs Kelb."

"Mr Vale." She did not get up. "Sit down, the chairs are free, and don't stand there being a man in a doorway at me, I'm not going to faint." She turned the folded sheet over twice and put it on her knee and smoothed it. "You're the one that came down the lane with him last night."

"I am."

"Then I've got four things written down and I want them in this order, and I want them said out loud, because I have found out a great deal about this city in one night and one of the things I have found out is that it will only say what it likes if you let it, and I am not letting it."

Marek sat down.

"Go on," said Milena Kelb. "The paper first. Read me the paper."

He read it to her. He gave her the thing in his own voice and not in a clerk's, because she had asked him not to. He said: the ninth day of the rains, the ward's water settlement for Tallow Ward, a supervisor's signature, a countersignature at the counter-desk in pencil on the same afternoon by a junior clerk of the third week, the totals added, and the totals correct.

"Right," she said. "That's one. Now the second. My son's name and what he did with his hand."

"He is Ondra Kelb. He had been three weeks in the post on the ninth and is in his fourth week now. He stood at the counter-desk on the afternoon of the ninth and added up the column of totals on that entry with a pencil, because the pen had not come, and the column agreed with itself, and he signed it. He says he looked at the column and not at who wrote the column, and that he would rather have said that out loud in a hall than have somebody say it for him."

"He said that this morning?"

"He said it on the stair at the sixth bell last night and he was quite clear about it."

Milena Kelb nodded at her own knees.

"Third."

And Marek said it, in a flat voice, in a waiting room, to a laundress from the next street from Sill Row, and he said it in third position because a boy of twenty on a stair had asked him to: a remedy in the margin of the oldest volume in the tower, written in a hand four hundred years dead, an office it named that had been vacant for three hundred years, no authority, no witness, nobody asked, from the last of the light to the sixth bell, and the cost of it came out of a living hand at the sixth bell, and his name was on the notice that said he had taken the duty.

There was a man on the far side of the room with a sprained wrist who had been listening without meaning to, and he stopped listening halfway and started again. Nobody else moved.

Milena Kelb's hands did not move on the paper.

"Right," she said. "That's three, and you're the third, and now I know which one of you I am talking to, and that is the last one I needed." She turned the sheet over. "The fourth is that nobody has been to see him. Not the Trust, not your office, not the men who wrote it down. He has been in that bed since before I got here and there has been a man from the fund-office asking him questions at the foot of it, and that's the fund, that's ours, I know that, but nobody from the tower. And I'm not angry about that, Mr Vale, I'm not. Angry's a thing I'd have to keep up. I'm asking you to write it down that they didn't come, so that in a year when I'm telling somebody, it's a fact and not a feeling."

"I will write it down."

"Good."

Then she said the thing that came next, and she said it evenly, and she did not look at him while she said it.

"Now I'm going to tell you what the fund pays, so you don't have to work it out and be embarrassed. The fund pays the doctor's hours and the two fingers of the bone-medicine and the six weeks of the cot, and it pays the woman who comes in to wash him, and it will pay a hand-book when he's ready for one, and it will not pay him back for a year of being able to use his hand, because there is no box on the form for that. That's the whole of what I know and I know it because I've read the form, because I take in washing at the end of the row and the woman who does the fund accounts sleeps across from me and I have been in her kitchen for eleven years."

Marek said, "Mrs Kelb, I would—"

"I know what you would," she said. "And I know what you would cost. I've been up all night in this room doing arithmetic of my own and you're not in it, Mr Vale, you're the fourth thing on my sheet and the sheet is finished." She put the paper in her apron pocket. "My sister — his sister, he's got a sister, she's eleven, she's at the ward school on Sill Row and she's the one who's going to be at that door this afternoon. She'll have heard about it in the yard by the fourth bell. She'll come and she will not be able to look at his hand, and I could use a clerk to stand in a schoolroom on the nineteenth and be a wall."

"Nineteenth?"

"The nineteenth," said Milena Kelb. "That's a Saturday. The school closes its books on the twenty-third and I'll have to be in that room on the twenty-third anyway. So that's the nineteenth, and I have written it in the book, and you can put it wherever you put things."

Then she picked up the sheet she had brought for the folding, unfolded it, and began to fold it again, and Marek understood that the conversation was over and had been over for some minutes.

He got up and went out into the lane, and got about six steps.

His own arm was across his chest because he had been carrying the levy in the way that a man carries a thing he has been carrying for nine hours, and his sleeve had ridden, and in the grey light under the eaves he could see a mark on the inside of his forearm that he had not seen before.

It was a ruled line, about the width of his little finger, running parallel to the old one, an inch below it, and it had been crossed with four small notches at even intervals — the same four-notch spacing, the same depth, the same slight drag at the end of the third one, as the mark Lio had asked him about three days ago in a room above a lockkeeper's shop.

Sera Holt came out with a bucket and saw him looking at it.

"That came up in the night?" she said.

"Yesterday."

"Then it came up fast, which is not the ordinary way." She took his arm and turned it to the light without hurrying and put a thumb on the far side of it and looked at it the way a woman looks at a thing she is reading rather than a thing she is treating. "That's a notching. Somebody has ruled it and notched it. It is not a cut and it is not a burn and it is not what a man gets from hauling a case down four flights." She let go of it. "It is the kind of mark a yearline makes on a person when a delay has been put in the body and left there. You will not find a word for it in any manual in this building. There is one line about it in Osk's book and it is the shortest line in the book and it does not say what it is for."

"What does it say."

"It says *it is not the shape of anything that happens to a man by accident*," said Sera Holt, and went back in with the bucket.

He stood in the lane with the levy under his arm for a long time, and did not put the two things together, because there was no room in him for the second thing; it would have come out as a piece of arithmetic and he did not yet have the arithmetic, and because the number four was on his arm and it had been on his arm since he was a child and four was not a word he was prepared to hear a meaning attached to by anybody, including himself, ever again.

Then a man came round the corner of the lane at a fast walk with a Warden's coat over a work coat and boots full of somebody else's water, and he was carrying a yard lantern by the bail in one hand and a folded sheet in the other, and he got to the clinic door before Marek did and put the lantern down and said, without any greeting at all:

"Vale. Are you the clerk who did the hold."

"I am."

"Right." Aldis Kern did not soften it and did not sharpen it. "Then here's where the ward is, and it's not a message, because I don't send messages, I'm the captain of the water in Tallow and I'm telling you a fact."

He held up the folded sheet.

"There's four hundred of us at the bottom of Sill Road and we're going up the causeway this morning, all of us who can, on foot and in the carts, and we're going to be outside that building before the room's called, because I've been the captain of that water sixteen years and I have never once in sixteen years let a clerk say a number about my ward that I wasn't in the room for. So you can tell them I'm coming, and you can tell them one more thing, and I want you to say it exactly the way I'm saying it, because I've thought about it and I want it said."

"Go on."

"The hold bought us six bells," Kern said. "Six bells and no water. The water came out of a valve at the Third Sluice that a Warden has been holding open since the second of the rains against a sealed Crown Terrace order, and that water is why the Gate tank is full this morning and why the low kitchens are not empty, and there is a man over there who will be called a saboteur in a fortnight and he is the reason there is a kettle boiling in that yard. I've had that written down on the back of a levy sheet by one of my own yard clerks and I have read it out to my three captains and they have all three agreed to say it, and we are going to say it in a room with a door, and I am going to say what it bought *and* what it did not buy, and the second list is longer than the first, and I have written the second list out myself."

"Then say it before you go, so I know what I'm hearing."

Aldis Kern looked at the brick wall of the lane for a second.

"It did not buy us a school year," he said. "It did not buy Rezek his hands. It did not buy a mother her son's handwriting. It bought six bells of a man's night and it cost a boy his hand and it did not put one pint against this ward's bill, not one, and I have the account for that, and the account is worse for us now than it was on Friday, because the screen is holding and the meters are honest and we are taking two and three-quarter times our standing schedule where the paper used to say four." He took up his lantern. "That's the second list. I'll give you the first one in the yard and you can count the yards if you like counting."

At the mouth of the lane the crowd had got bigger. There were forty of them now, and a boy of about eleven standing in the middle of it with a shawl over her head and her hands inside it, holding herself the way a child holds herself when she has decided not to be a child.

And the crowd was not one crowd. Marek stood in the lane mouth and read it the way he read everything, at arm's length, in bad light, for the two places where two inks disagreed about the same year, and the two places were clear.

A third of them had come to thank somebody. They were the ones who had brought things — a pot, a wrapped parcel, a bundle of dry wood, a bucket they intended to leave on a step. They had made a night of this and the night had gone in their favour and they wanted somebody to stand in front of them at the eighth bell and be thanked.

The rest had come because two of the yard captains had been told by a man off the causeway that it was the junior clerk who had put a bad column on a water bill.

And they were not to blame for it, as far as Marek could see from where he was standing. The clerks had been saying it since the middle of the night because it was the only version anybody had, and a boy is easier to be angry at than a building, and there was not a single document in the tower that had ever told them otherwise.

He went up the lane past them with the ward's levy under his arm and a ruled line under his sleeve.
