# Chapter 26 — Two Stories of One Night

The river came up on the twenty-seventh without any rain at all, which was the thing nobody in Tallow Ward had expected and the thing that every man who had lived in the basin for forty years had expected.

By the second bell the Silt was a foot higher at the Tallow Gate than it had been at the same hour on the fourteenth, and it was still rising, and Bell Weir was at its limit and had been for three weeks, and the rain had stopped on the twenty-second and the water had not.

"The sluice," said Aldis Kern, in the Gate yard, at the second bell, to about sixty people. "Say it out loud, somebody. Say what happens with the sluice open and the river coming."

Nobody said it.

"That is what I have been asking people to say for nine days and it is the first time it has been the correct question." He put the sheet of oiled paper on the trestle table, with the forty items on it, and left his hand flat on it. "On the fourteenth the water came off a valve and it went into a cellar and it came up through a floor and it broke a boy's leg, and that is item one and item two and I read them to you in this yard three days ago and I will read them again tonight, and the Underbank comes first, because that is the order of this account and it is not going to change because the river has turned round.

"And tonight, with the sluice open and the river up two feet, the Underbank passage is the first place that fills, because it is the lowest door in this ward and the sluice is open, and if the Gate tank falls below the sluice head then the channel is going to run backwards into the passage, and everything I read to you on the twenty-fourth about a condemned storehouse is going to happen again to a storehouse that is still on the roll."

He turned round to the yard.

"So the ward has one decision to make before the lamps go and I am going to make it, and then I am going to go and stand at a hearing at the eighth bell tomorrow morning, and I want both of those things understood to be the same decision and not two."

---

He had called the yard for the fourth time in nine days and two hundred and sixty came, and he had a sheet of yard paper and not enough hands to go round with it, so he wrote the two sentences at the top of it in charcoal, large, and held it up at the tank.

*ONE. The ward's water came down a channel off the Crown Terrace intake by a valve a Warden held open against a sealed order, and the ward was not asked.*

*TWO. The ward's water was not lawfully delivered to the ward, and the ward will pay for every hundred pints of it at the ordinary wage.*

He held it up for a long time and then he put it down on the trestle table and let the yard have it.

"You cannot have both of those out of one mouth," said Kern, "and that is not a complaint about the Trust, that is a fact about mouths. There is one man going into that room and he is me, and I have to choose which of those two sentences the Trust hears first, and I have had since the sixth bell this morning and I have chosen, and I am going to tell you why before anybody stands up and tells me I have sold the man who fed us."

Nadja Ostrow got there first, and she is a cook of thirty-eight who has one of the eleven names on the roll and has been boiling pans on a ration since the fourth of the rains.

"Captain, I have been in the low kitchens every night since the second and I have got two hundred children in that room and I know exactly what the sentence is that I want said in that room, and it is not yours."

"Then say it here and I will carry it if it can be carried."

"Three hundred people in the low kitchens were alive on the fourteenth because a valve was open, and the whole city knows it, and the ward is going to be in a room tomorrow and it is going to be asked to say whether it stole its own water, and I am telling you that it did not steal it, it was given it, and a gift does not become a theft because the man who gave it had no authority." She had her arms folded and she was not shouting, which in that yard was worse. "And you are going to go in there and say the other thing. You are going to tell them we were given water unlawfully and we will pay for it, and I want you to know that there are people in those low kitchens who will hear about it in a week."

"Yes," said Kern.

"And you are going to do it anyway."

"Yes, and I am going to tell you the reason, and the reason is not courage and it is not a bargain." He put his hand on the two sentences. "Read the second one again. *Not lawfully delivered, and the ward will pay.* That sentence keeps the ward out of the mud. If the ward says the water was not lawfully its own, then the ward is a beneficiary, and a beneficiary of somebody else's breach pays the whole of what the beneficiary took, and it does not matter that it was a hundred and forty people carrying pails by hand with no authority either. If we say the water was a gift, then the Trust does not get a gift, it gets a ward with an open sluice above it and a Crown Terrace intake that ran dry for twelve days, and the Trust will want that looked at, and looking at it will produce a paper with a cause at the top of it, and that cause will be a name, and the name will be whichever man is nearest the paper. I have seen which man that is. He is forty-one and he is standing on a platform two hundred yards from here and I have no intention of handing that room his name and letting it do to him what it has already done to a boy of twenty."

Milan Vray said, from the back, in a rope-maker's flat voice:

"And the hundred and forty who carried it had no authority either, and my tar is in the cellar, and if the ward pays for the water at the ordinary wage then the ward will pay for my tar as well and I am the one who has to say thank you for that."

"Your tar is the first item on the sheet and the thirty-ninth is the slate on the building it was in, and it will be paid out of the yard purse in the first quarter and not out of the levy," said Kern. "You have my word on it in front of two hundred and sixty people and it is in my own book at home. It is not in dispute and it is not the subject of tomorrow."

"Then let me say the other thing," said Vray, "since nobody else will. I was one of the hundred and forty. I carried pails on the ninth and the tenth and the eleventh and the twelfth and I did not know where the water came from and I did not ask, and I would not have asked, and if you had asked me I would have carried them again the same. And I am not going to stand in a yard tonight and be told that the water was a gift, because I have been in that cellar and my boy's boots are in it, and there is a boy in this yard at the back with a shin in two pieces who is thirteen, his name is Vasil Ferun, and he eats at my table because his own people are at the Underbank and there is no room left at the Underbank, and this yard has had his name read out in front of it twice this week and never once put the two together, and no gift in this city is going to be a good enough name for that."

The yard was quiet for a while.

"Then we agree," said Kern, "and I will say it the way I have written it, and the two of you are both in it, and that is why it is the only sentence I can carry into that room. I am not going to say that a man saved us. I am going to say that the ward drew water from a channel above the ward meter without a right to it, that the ward did not ask for it and did not know where it came from, that a hundred and forty unnamed people moved it by hand for twelve days, and that the ward will pay for every hundred pints of it at the ordinary wage and has told the Trust so in advance. The Underbank is named first. The sluice is named second. And the sluice is named second and not first, permanently, and I do not care who is in that room."

He held up his hand before the yard could start.

"Two more things and then I go and get wet. First: this yard has forty items on a sheet and the sheet cannot go into that room, and not because the Trust is wicked. Because there is no box for it. A ward cannot file. A ward cannot petition, cannot appeal, cannot be sworn, and cannot be cross-examined, and we found that out at the door of the Threshold five days ago with three hundred and eleven signatures on a piece of oiled paper, and the answer we were given was that it is a piece of paper signed by three hundred and eleven people and addressed to nobody. So this sheet stays on this board. It is the ward's memory and it is not going to be anybody's evidence, and I have made my peace with that this afternoon and I have not made my peace with it, and I will probably never make my peace with it, and it is still the correct thing to do.

"Second: the ward has just decided that it will pay for water it had no right to, at the ordinary wage, out of four hundred people, and I want the four hundred people to hear the size of what they have just agreed to, because I have done the sum and I have got it wrong twice. It is not a hundred and forty pints. It is not two hundred. Somebody in this yard has heard two hundred and I want that number out of everybody's mouth before tomorrow morning, because that is two hundred hundreds of pints a night standing in a walled garden on a hill for eleven years, and not one drop of it came up this hill on the fourteenth. Two hundred hundreds of pints a night is what the Crown Terrace account has been taking off the top of our meter since the second of the rains, and it has nothing to do with the valve, and the valve is the reason the garden wants it. The valve cost the ward two hundred a night for twelve days and the ward never saw one drop of it, and if I go into that room and say *we will pay for every pint of the water that came down the channel*, then a clerk will take that to mean four hundred and forty hundreds of pints a night, because the only number in any book in that building is four hundred and forty, and the two hundred is in a wall book with a take-off on it that a woman named on no roll found on the sixth day of the rains."

"So say less," said Neris Kade from the back, where she had been standing in a coat that did not belong to her for two days. "Say what you can prove. Say that the ward will pay for the water it took from the channel above its meter, and put a number on it, and if the number is wrong they will correct you in the room and you will have to say thank you, and that is a far better afternoon than going in there with a number you cannot hold."

Kern looked at her.

"Two hundred," he said. "And if the room says two hundred is not the number, then the room is telling me where the water went, and I have been trying to find that out for three weeks and I would take it off a clerk like a gift."

---

The high water came in about the fifth of the night and it was not the fourteenth and it was not close to the fourteenth, and it made a mess of the low ground and it did not put anybody in the Silt.

The Underbank passage filled to the second step. The Gate yard was under two inches. A yard man called Petross had to be taken off the roof of the rope shed at the Tanner's with a line, and nobody was hurt, and his own opinion, afterwards, was that the shed roof had been asking for it since the spring.

And at about the second of the night, in the rain, forty or fifty yards from the Gate, a man of forty-one in a Warden's coat came down the channel bank with a hand-wheel under his arm and six men behind him and put a board in the mouth of the Underbank passage, and stood in the water above the knee holding it, and did not move for two hours, and the yard watched him from the tank.

Then the water went down, and it went down fast, because it always does, and by the fourth bell the Gate yard was draining and Aldis Kern was in the low kitchens with a lamp, and Marek Vale was on the steps of the Gate with a wet sleeve and a piece of oiled paper he had not been able to put into a room, watching a Warden walk back up to a platform he had not left for twenty-five days.

Kern found him at the fifth bell and did not say anything about the water.

"Twenty past seven tomorrow," he said. "Be there early. He will want the man who kept the valve to say why he kept it, and he will get it, and then he will turn round and find me, because I am the person the supply went to. And I am going to tell him what I told that yard and I am not going to soften it and I am not going to enjoy it, and I would like it understood in that room that I am not there to put a man in a box. I am there because a ward is not a person and somebody has to be standing at the end of the table when they ask the question, and I have decided that the somebody is going to be somebody who will answer it with the first item in his mouth and the Underbank at the top of it, and if that is the wrong man to have in the room then the ward picked him and I am not taking it back at breakfast."

"What will he ask you."

"He will ask me whether the ward would have paid for the water if it had been lawfully offered," said Kern. "And I do not know the answer, and neither do you, and neither does he, and that is going to be the worst half hour of my life and I would rather be the one in it than the twenty-year-old at the counter-desk who took the whole of it off a column he never saw."
