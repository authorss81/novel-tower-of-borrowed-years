# Chapter 2 — Nothing Wrong

He had told himself he was checking the totals.

That was almost true. The Vale file was in the up-tray because a vigor loan's draw could not be countersigned by anybody below the third grade, and the assignment sheet for the next four days had Sill Row and the Tallow yards on it, and Tomas Vale held a vigor loan out of the Tallow Gate yards, so on the morning of the tenth the case would come up to Marek's bench whether he wanted it or not. He would have countersigned the receipt on the fourteenth or he would have written a finding that stopped the draw. There was no third thing. Procedure was very clear about that, and Marek loved procedure for exactly the reason it was clear.

He carried the case down off Bell Stair anyway instead of leaving it for the morning, and by the time he was at the top of the Sill Road he had already read the first page twice.

The Tower's forms were plain. That was the first thing a clerk learned and the thing a borrower never believed. There was a great deal of marble in the halls, and a great deal of language in the hall's own voice, and the loan forms were written in short words that a person could read aloud across a kitchen table and be entirely true when they finished. The vigor form said:

**WHAT IS NEEDED.** A body that can work the twelfth hour and the thirteenth for eleven days. Stated as hours, because hours can be counted and a person can be told whether they have been got.

**WHAT IS PLEDGED.** Twenty-six months of the borrower's own recovery, drawn from his own body only. The form said *his own* four times, in four different places, and the fourth time it said *and no other person*, and there was a reason for that, which was that somebody in the first century of the Reckoning had pledged his wife's next pregnancy and had got away with it.

**HOW FAR IT REACHES.** The north-road span at Tallow, and its approaches, and nothing else. Not a second span. Not the workshop. Not the household.

**WHO WITNESSED IT.** Two. Witness one, Tamsin Vale, seamstress, of Sill Row: *I have read the need and I have read the pledge. I am the borrower's mother and I am signing anyway, because if the span comes down it comes down on my daughter's head.* Witness two, a name Marek did not know, a stonecutter on the north road, with his own statement: *I do not understand what a month of recovery is. I understand that he has given it and that his hands will be worse and that he wants the span up.*

**WHEN IT COMES DUE.** On the fourteenth day of the rains, the remaining draw, four months of the pledge. After that, service: supervised road work on the same span, eleven days a season for as many seasons as the schedule named, and then the residual, which was not money but a number of days in the ward yards, and which the form helpfully priced in the ordinary wage of the district.

That was the file. Nine pages of it. Two witnesses, complete terms, a scope with no gap in it, a settlement with a date and an amount and a method. The whole of it in the plainest language the tower had ever produced, and every part of it present.

Marek read the eight other pages in the room over the lockkeeper's shop with his coat still on, sitting on the edge of his own bed with the case on his knees, and he found — he actually sat and hunted, page by page, the way he would hunt an altered ink — and he found nothing at all.

No date overwritten. No witness phrasing repeated from another file, which he checked by memory against three he knew. No clause in a different ink. The pledge was bounded and the scope was narrow and the settlement named a day and an amount and a place to stand and be counted.

He sat with it for a long time.

The thing he kept coming back to, turning it over the way you turn a stone looking for the underside, was that his mother had signed it in the plainest possible words. She had not been talked into it. Nobody had explained anything to her that she had not understood, and there was no version of this document in which Tamsin Vale did not know exactly what she was agreeing to, and the form had let her say it out loud in her own sentence, in the witness box, where it sat in the record for the rest of time: *I am signing anyway.*

She was a seamstress. She could not read the marble language, and she could read that page, and she had read it, and she had signed, and she had been fifty-one and had buried a husband in the water of the seventh winter and had two sons at that table, and one of them could not lift after the eleventh hour.

The file was clean. The file was, by every standard the Chamber of Accounts had ever written down, a good file. It was a file that would survive a hearing. It was a file that Hesper Rann would have looked at, put down, and said nothing about, because there was nothing in it to say.

And Tomas's hands were already going.

Marek had known that for a month. He had watched it happen across a kitchen table and on the north road and in the doorway of the shop, and he had said nothing, because the man whose hands were going was a man who had gone to the clinic and been told the truth by a physician and had chosen, with full knowledge, to keep working, and it was not Marek's business to tell a man what his own body was doing while that man was still using it.

Tomas was at the back of the shop with a lamp and a grindstone, working a tenon on a gate-hang that did not need grinding. He had the piece braced between his knees and his shoulders up around his ears, and the rasp went along the wood in three short strokes and stopped, and went again, and stopped.

"How's the span," Marek said.

"Mm."

"You didn't go up today."

"It's raining."

"Rezek's foreman came by at noon looking for pitch." Marek put the kettle on. "He said the fuel basin was dry again. He said the yard's down to carrying from the Ropewalk standpipe."

"Mm."

"Tomas."

"I heard you." The rasp stopped. Tomas looked at his hands, both of them, spread out on his thighs, the way a man looks at a tool that has started to lie to him. Then he said, in a completely different voice, pleasant and empty: "It's a bad spring."

"It's the ninth day of the rains."

"I know what day it is."

Marek made the tea and put the cup down and did not say the next thing, and the not-saying took a visible amount of effort that he was aware of, and he turned around and washed the cup he had not used so that his hands had something to do.

Lio came in from the alley with her coat streaming and a fistful of something in paper.

"Two of the four tanks are dry," she said. "The Ropewalk one's dry. The Tanner's is low. The cook at the low kitchens says they are boiling their pans on the ration because the Gate tank went, and that the Gate tank is fed off the Ropewalk standpipe, and that the Ropewalk standpipe is on the fourth branch of a main that does not come from anywhere she has ever heard of. I told her it was the main and she said the main's in Crown Terrace so of course it's full."

Lio delivered this the way she delivered everything, as a report, with the pleasure of a person who has noticed that the adults are wrong and cannot be got to admit it. She was nine and she liked maps and she had drawn one of the ward in the last month that showed the pipes, and the pipes on it did not match the pipes in the ground, and she had not been corrected because there was no time to correct her.

"Lio, go to bed."

"It's the ninth day, I'm allowed up until the bell."

"Then go to bed at the bell."

She went, with the paper, and shut the door behind her with enormous care, which was how she shut a door when she was going to listen.

Marek sat back down on the edge of the bed with the case.

"You came up early," Tamsin said. She was in the front room with her feet under her and a seam ripper and a mending basket, doing the thing she did with her hands while she decided how much of a conversation she was going to have. "The case should be on your bench till morning."

"It was on my bench till morning. It's a drawer, not a snake pit."

"You read it."

"I audit it tomorrow."

"You read it tonight." She drew the ripper through a seam, and the seam came apart into two pieces, and she did not look up. "Well?"

"It's clean, Ma."

"I know it's clean. I signed it. You think I don't know what I signed?" She said it without heat. That was the terrible thing about Tamsin Vale: she was never angry at him. She was simply, patiently, a woman who had made a decision in a kitchen with two other people and had lived in it since. "Then what's the matter with your face?"

"Nothing's the matter with my face."

"You've been chewing the inside of your cheek since you sat down. That's a thing you do when you want to be asked and don't want to be asked." She set the ripper down. "Marek. What are you doing with that case?"

"Looking for the error."

"There's no error."

"Ma—"

"There is no error," Tamsin said, "because I read it, and because the second witness read it, and because I put my hand on it and said a true sentence in the witness box, which is a thing I would not have done for a gate, let alone a gate that is already up. If you want to be angry, be angry at the spring. Be angry at the fourth span. Be angry at whoever is supposed to keep that water up. I have done the one thing I can still do, and it was enough, and it will keep Lio's head out of the water for another season."

Marek looked at her.

He had a great deal of machinery in him and none of it worked. He wanted to say: you are the surety on a twenty-six-month pledge of your own recovery and I have a form that says a witness is somebody who is not being paid by the borrower, and you are the borrower's mother, and the form has a box for that and you ticked the box that says *I am aware that I am not independent*, and if I read that box out loud in a hearing tomorrow you would understand what you did and Tomas would understand and Lio would not understand for four years.

He wanted to say: I am going to find something, and when I do it, I am going to use it, and it is going to be you on the witness stand.

He said: "You should have told me before you signed."

"You were in Rainmarket with the seed copies for four days. There was no before. There was the ninth of the rains and there was the bell and there was a man who could hold a span up six hours for the cost of a man already being ruined, and you would have said what you always say, and I would have listened, and the span would have gone in the river at two in the morning." She picked the ripper back up. "You would have been right. That's the whole trouble with you. You are nearly always right and it has never once been the answer to the question in front of me."

That was the end of it. She went back to the basket. He went back to the case, and he read the ninth page, which is the page the borrower signs, and Tomas's mark on it was a builder's mark, a short hooked stroke with the chisel, because Tomas could not do fine writing and had never pretended to, and the form allowed a mark where a signature was called for, provided a clerk witnessed it, which Marek had witnessed, in the spring, with his own pen.

There was a witness space under the mark and Marek's own name in it, in his own hand, in March.

He shut the case.

Then he did not shut it, and instead took out a fresh sheet of his own paper — not Tower paper, not the printed numbered sheet, his own, ruled in his own hand with a column for date and a column for months — and he did the arithmetic.

Four months draw on the fourteenth. Twenty-six pledged, four drawn, twenty-two left. The road work, eleven days a season, and the schedule named four seasons, so forty-four days, and the residual priced at the ordinary wage of the district, which was eleven a day for a foreman and four and six for a hand, so what the family owed after the road work came to a number he did not like, and that number was payable in days in the ward yards, and days in the ward yards in the ninth day of the rains were the one currency the ward had none of.

He wrote it all down. He wrote the totals, and the dates, and the wage, and then he wrote the four seasons, and then he crossed the whole page out and wrote it again more slowly, because he had made an arithmetic slip and he could not let an arithmetic slip stand in a document, even a document with no number on it but his own.

Then he folded it in four, and put it inside his coat, against the chest pocket where he kept the little book of things he had promised his mother.

In the front room she said, "You're going to the ward in the morning."

He had not said it.

"You'll go to the ward," Tamsin said, "because you'll look at the petitions until you understand them, and you will not understand them from a bench. And you will be dull and slow and entirely correct, which is what you are." She bit the thread. "Bring back the water. Don't bring back a scandal. The scandal won't be any use to anybody at the bottom of it."

Marek put his coat on over the folded sheet.

He took the Tower's petition book off the shelf where he had left it, and he turned to the page for the ninth of the rains, and he found the entry he had made at the fifth bell, and he read it again: forty-one petitions, the ward's water, Kern's name first among the captains on the first of them, and a hundred and six lines under the third, and three hundred more in nine hands — four hundred and six in all, which is the ward, asking for one thing: that the water be not diminished in a time of flood.

Forty-one. The whole ward, writing to him, one letter at a time, in the rain.

He looked at the number for a while, and then he closed the book, and went down the stairs to check the lock, because he was the kind of man who checked the lock on a building he did not own.
