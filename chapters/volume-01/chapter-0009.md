# Chapter 9 — The Peak

After the fifth bell Marek Vale got up off the bench, and the getting up took two attempts and a hand on the desk, and he stood in the unlit copy room with blood dried on his upper lip and a tunnel round the edge of his head and a case open in front of him that he had suspended six hours earlier on the authority of a dead man's handwriting.

He left the case open. He had a rule about that too, and it was the only rule he had ever invented for himself, and it was that a suspended case stays suspended and does not get quietly closed, because a closed case is a case that has been dealt with.

Then he went down four floors and out into the weather, because he could not sit in the room with a hole in it, and because a man who has just held up a settlement in an empty building ought at least to stand in the thing he has held up.

The Sill Road was under six inches of moving water and the Standing Market had put out its night boards and the whole of the run down to the ward was a black shine with lamps in it. He got down to the Tallow Gate a little after the fifth bell and stood on the levee and looked at the ward.

The Gate itself was doing its job. That was the first thing, and it was a strange thing to feel at the fourth day of a flood. The floodgate was down, it was braced, the two new props Marek had watched a crew set in the spring were holding, and the water on the upstream face stood eleven feet above the low kitchens and was not moving at all.

The yards were running dry anyway.

Not empty. Dry, which was a specific and worse thing. The Gate tank had a skin of sludge in it and a bucket-scraper standing upright in the middle, abandoned, and the Tanner's was a third down, and the Ropewalk standpipe was making a sound like a small animal and about nine gallons an hour was coming out of it. The mains ran. He could hear them, the same low knock in the iron that he had put his palm on on the ninth day at dusk in a dry tank, and the arithmetic of that was the whole of the emergency: the water was in the pipes and it was not in the ward, and no document in Bell Stair had ever recorded that those two things could be true at once.

"Clerk."

Aldis Kern was standing in the road in a coat over his night clothes with a lamp in one hand, and he had been standing there long enough for the water to have come up over the top of his boots.

"Don't tell me," he said. "I know. The pipe's got the water. I've been standing in front of the pipe for two hours telling my own yard that the pipe's got the water." He put the lamp down on a stone. "Ninety-one people came out of the low kitchens tonight, and the school house at the top of Sill Row has four hundred and thirty-one in it now, because the school house is dry and it has a roof and there was nowhere else. Kade got them moved. I did not. I was on the tank until an hour ago and then I came down here to shout at a pipe."

"Where is she?"

"In the school house, counting." Kern made a sound that was not quite a laugh. "Counting them, clerk. By name, out of her own book, in the dark, on her own, because she says a list of people in a room is a thing that can be used later and she wants the room to have a list before anything happens in it. I have not had a thought in nine days that was as good as that one and she did it in a dark schoolroom with a pen tied to her finger with string."

He turned the lamp so it lit the road.

"Petross's screen is holding. The Gate tank is filling at half a rate and it is filling. If the peak stops in an hour we have water in this ward until the fifteenth and I will feed four hundred people until the seventeenth on what is in these pipes." He looked up. "And it has not stopped in an hour, and it does not look like it is going to stop, and I have been doing this for eleven years and I can tell you the difference between a river that has peaked and a river that has stopped being polite."

Below them on the road the ward was moving. Four men with a hand-cart and a roller going down toward the Underbank mouth with a lamp on the cart, and a woman with a blanket over her head shouting something that the water took away. And a knot of people at the end of the Basin road, and somebody in the knot was sitting down and then not getting up.

"That is Ferun," Kern said. "Ferun's boy. He went into the Underbank at the fourth bell to get a neighbour's goods out of a cellar and the water came up through the floor and put him off his feet and he came out with the other one." He said it flatly, the way a captain says a thing he has written down. "His leg is the wrong way. He is thirteen. He is on the school house step with a broken leg and he went into the Underbank to carry somebody else's bedding and I have got both of those facts and I have got them in that order and not in the other order, and I have got to be able to say them in that order for the rest of my life, and I would like somebody in this city to hear me say them in that order, and you are the only person on this road tonight who writes things down for a living."

Marek wrote them down in that order.

He got about nine words out before the tide of sound came up the road from the south, and it was not a shout, it was worse than a shout, it was a lot of people talking at once and getting louder, and it came round the corner of the Basin road and into the Gate road and it was a crowd of a hundred moving up the levee in the rain, and at the front of it, on the leading edge, walking with a lamp in his teeth and four men in a wedge behind him, was Ilya Senn.

The crowd came up to about ten feet of Marek and stopped, and did not stop well.

"Where is he?" somebody shouted.

"He's at the sluice—"

"He is *at the sluice*, he's been at the sluice for six days—"

"Open order says closed, order's the seventh, that's sealed, that's got a Crown Terrace seal on it—"

"He is breaking a sealed order with our water in it, that's what he's doing—"

"You'll want to hear this from a Warden and not from a Tallow—"

"You'll want to hear it from me," Ilya said, over all of it, in a voice made for a platform by forty years of shouting across water, and the crowd went down by a third, "and here it is, and it is one sentence, and I am not asking anybody to like it. The order is the seventh. The sluice has been open since the second. I hold it open. I have broken the order every hour since the second day of this flood and I will go on breaking it at the sixth bell tonight if the low kitchens are still dry. I have four letters in my coat in case anybody wants to read them, and my own name on my own work order, and I have never once pretended to be anybody else.

"What I want said back to me, and I am asking and not telling, is this. Close that sluice at the sixth bell. Go up to the school house and count how many people are in there. Then say to me, in front of these hundred people, whether you would rather I had obeyed the order." He took the lamp out of his teeth. "That is the whole of the case and I will not make it longer. If the ward says close it, I close it, and I will not say a word about it afterwards, and the low kitchens go without at the peak and that is the cost of saying it."

The crowd was quiet for about four seconds, which in a hundred people in the rain is a long time.

Then a voice from the back said: "Marda's on that sluice. Marda Senn's been down there two nights running with a bucket. Ask her."

"Nobody's asked her anything," somebody said. "Ask her now, go on."

And the crowd broke a little, the way a crowd does when it discovers it is about to be told what it already thinks, and two of Ilya's men put their shoulders in and made a gap, and through the gap came a woman of fifty in a Warden's coat with slate dust on one forearm and a lamp, and the rain coming down her face, and she was carrying a bucket in her right hand, and she put the bucket down on the road at Ilya's feet.

"You fed us," Marda Senn said.

"I did."

"You fed us since the second day, and the gate would have shut on us otherwise, and the fuel basin would have been under water on the sixth, and Rezek's eleven men would have drowned in the tar channel." She was not shouting. She had a Warden's habit of being audible in a yard. "And you broke a Crown Terrace order to do it, and a Crown Terrace water-clerk wrote you four letters about it, and the order was sealed, and I have read the four letters because he keeps them in the coat where anyone can get at them. That man is a fool if he thinks those letters are about a valve."

"He knows what they're about," Ilya said.

"Then let him say it out loud in a room." She turned round to the crowd, and she did not raise her voice, and the crowd listened anyway. "He has broken the order. That is true, and a Deputy Steward is going to write it in a line and it will be true. I have been down that sluice two nights with a bucket and I know what the water on the Crown Terrace side is doing, and if he shuts it at the sixth bell the intake at the head of that channel is going to have a great deal more water in it, and the intake is Crown Terrace, and Crown Terrace is where the order comes from."

"So the water goes to them."

"The water goes to them *tonight*." She looked at the crowd for a long moment. "Somebody in this crowd wants to say that out loud. Go on. Say it. Say the true thing, because we will all be standing in a room in ten days and I would like it said now rather than then."

Nobody said it. A hundred people in the rain looked at the ground and at each other and did not say the true thing, and Marek stood on the levee above them and understood, with a clarity that was almost restful, that the ward had already decided and had not decided and would go into the hearing room holding both, and that both would be true, and that there was no way to make them not both be true.

Ilya Senn picked the bucket up off the road.

"Keep your two," he said to her. "And I'll shut it at the sixth, and the ward will not be dry, and the letter of the seventh will be in a file somewhere with my name on it, and I will not have said a word about it afterwards." He looked up the leveve at the man standing above them with a lamp and blood on his face. "Clerk. You are the reason that order has not been obeyed yet and you should know that four hundred people know it. Write that down too, if you are writing."


Tamsin came up the Sill Road at half past the fifth with a covered pot in one hand and Lio by the other, and she did not come up to the Gate; she came as far as the bus shelter at the head of the road, which was the highest dry ground between the ward and the span.

Lio had been left at the shelter with her hood up and the pot in her lap.

Marek got down off the leveve and walked up to them and his mother looked at his face for about two seconds.

"You've cut your arm," she said.

"I have."

"There's a clean cloth in the front room." She held the pot out. "This is for your brother."

"I know."

"He can't eat it."

"Tamsin—"

"He can't hold the bowl." She said it in a completely level voice, standing in six inches of moving water at the top of the Sill Road at half past the fifth bell on the night of a flood. "He can't hold it and he won't be helped, and I have watched him try, and I have taken it away again, and I have decided that I am not going to stand in a road in this rain a third time to be refused by my own son. So you take it. You'll be up there. He is up there because he is up there every night of this flood and he has been up there every night since the freshet, and I have not once said one word to you about it, and I am not going to now, and I want you to notice that I have not."

"Mother—"

"Notice it," Tamsin said, "and then go up that road, because I have a girl to get home and I have a door to be in." And she got Lio up onto the shelter bench and put her inside the shelter with her feet off the wet, and gave her the pot to hold because a child will hold anything she is asked to hold, and then the two of them went down the Sill Road in the rain, slowly, with Tamsin's arm round the girl's shoulders, and neither of them looked back.

Marek stood at the top of the Sill Road with a pot of soup and a cloth that was not in his coat.

Then he went up.


The Tally Stair is four storeys of turning stone with a landing every nine steps, and a lamp on every landing, and at the top of it there is a wide step that everybody in the building calls the Chair because the first clerk of the Reckoning sat on it once in the founding year and refused to move, and there is nothing on that step at all except a floor and a lamp and the room above it.

Kelb was on the Chair with his coat off and his sleeves rolled and a board across his knees and a sheet of the Tower's own copy paper nailed to the board, and the lamp turned down to a bead, and he was writing with his whole arm.

He was writing beautifully. That was the thing Marek noticed first, from the bottom of the stair, standing on a step with soup under his arm: the boy was writing beautifully, in the small upright clerk's hand, and he was going faster than Marek had believed a hand could go, and the head of the first page was already finished, and the head of a page is the part nobody ever looks at.

"Mr Vale." Kelb did not stop writing. "She said you'd be on the hearing and I've done the roll and the entry first and the schedule I can't do."

"Why can't you do the schedule?"

"Because it's not in anything I've got." Kelb's pen stopped for the first time. He held up the wedge of paper he had been working from — the ward's copy, four pages, the roll, and on top of it the ward's notice of the assessment. "There's the roll, and there's the entry as they gave it to us on the Wednesday, and there's one, two, four, five, and there's no three. It's not in the ward's copy and it's not in anything the Tower'll do, because —"

"Because it's suspended," Marek said.

"Because it's suspended, and she said the Tower won't copy a held one, and I said I'd do it by hand, and then I looked, and there's nothing to do it *from*." He said it without any self-pity at all, with the particular resentment of a competent man who has been handed a job that has no input. "The Tower copy's in the case in the copy room and the case is held and I wasn't to open it. So I need page three off somebody, sir, and I don't know who."

Marek put the pot of soup on the step.

He had it in his coat. That was the whole difficulty and it took him about two seconds. He had it in his coat, folded in four, on the back page of his own book, in his own hand, and he had made it in a vault on the twelfth night of the rains in violation of a standing order of the Yearspire Trust that he had read on the same page as the signature nobody was permitted to erase, and if he produced it now he would be producing, in a public corridor, on the Chair step, an unofficial copy of a page that the Trust's own founding instruction forbids any clerk to copy.

And if he did not produce it, then the ward's levy would go to the hearing in the morning with the schedule missing, and the missing page was the page with eleven sureties in it and no witnesses anywhere, and the whole of the ward's case would be a case about water.

He took the book out of his coat, opened it on the step, turned to the back page, and read the eleven lines through once, quietly, to be sure of them. Then he shut it again and put it back inside, against the little book of promises, and it did not go any further than that, and nobody in this tower was ever going to be shown that page.

Then he took a sheet of the Tower's own copy paper off the spike where it had been standing for a hundred years waiting for a worse night than this one, and he sat down on the bottom step with his back against the stone, and he wrote the page out again from memory.

It took him twenty-two minutes. It was the second time in five days. The first had been on the tenth, in twenty minutes, with the original in his hand and a wax stitch to check every name against, which is how a clerk learns a document. Now there was no original and no stitch and no wax, and there was a lamp turned down to a bead and a boy reading over his shoulder and a nose that had been bleeding for five hours.

He wrote the four column headings. He wrote the eleven households. He wrote the days and the wage, and then he came to the fourth column and wrote *surety* and wrote the eleven names, and then he wrote *witness*, and then he wrote eleven empty cells, and he did it in the same hand and in the same order with the same slight pressure in the same places, because that is what a copy is and nothing else is.

Kelb read it over his shoulder and did not interrupt, which was the most professional thing Marek had seen anybody do all week.

"This isn't Tower hand either," Kelb said at last. "This one's yours."

"No," Marek said. "It's the levy office's, and I am writing it as the levy office writes, because a copy has to be the same hand as the original or it is not a copy, it is a second document, and a second document in a hearing is a document nobody in the room has standing to produce." He blotted it and did not blow on it, because you do not blow on a copy. "This sheet goes into the hearing bundle, as page three, and into nothing else — not the ward's, not the file's, and not yours. Your name goes on it as the copyist. Not mine. And if anybody asks you in a week where page three came from, you will say that a page was produced to you by a clerk in the Chamber on the Chair step at the sixth bell of the fourteenth of the rains, and that is all you will ever say, because that is the truth and it is the whole of the truth."

Kelb took the sheet.

Then he put his pen down and said, quite quietly, in the voice of a young man reading a column for the second time and finding it exactly as it was:

"There's no witness on any of them."

"No."

"There's no witness on any of them and there's a surety on every one." Kelb looked at the page. "Mr Vale, is the surety the same as the witness? Because if the surety is the person standing behind it, then there is a person standing behind all eleven, and I counted on the Wednesday and I thought the assessment was a water bill."

"It's not a water bill," Marek said.

"No, sir," said Ondra Kelb, twenty years old, three weeks in the post, on the Chair step of the Tally Stair at the sixth bell of the fourteenth of the rains, with a page in front of him that had been given to him by a man who was bleeding from the nose and would not say why, and he did not ask him why.

Marek picked the pot of soup up and sat down on the bottom step with his back against the stone and his head in his hands, and somebody came down the stair.

She was small and thin and about nineteen and dressed for a roof and not for a stair, and she went up past him on the outside edge without slowing, and then stopped, and came back down two steps, and looked at him.

"You're bleeding," she said.

"The Tower takes a fee," Marek said, "for looking at a live record, and it is not money, and I have looked at one too many this week. That is all it is. There is nothing else happening on this stair."

"That is a technically accurate answer," the girl said, "and it is not the answer to the question, and I've been sold that one before." She went up.

From the Chair, Kelb said, "That's the runner. Nim Rook. She's all right, she's not a—" He stopped. "She was in this room for a fortnight in the spring. Before me."

Marek sat with his head in his hands on the bottom step of the Tally Stair and listened to a boy copy a page he was not allowed to have, and he could not keep still for long, and after a while he got up and went and stood on the Chair beside him and read over his shoulder and corrected two figures out of eleven, out loud, in a voice like a man reading out a total, and Kelb wrote the corrections without arguing, because they were right.

Some way through the fourth page a voice came up the stair from the copy room, and it was the posting desk's night woman, and she was calling for the holding notice, because the sixth bell was nearly on and a held settlement that nobody puts on the board at the end of its bells is a held settlement that never ended.

"Mr Vale. It goes on the board. Now. The form says the day is moved to the end of the year, and the year ends at the sixth bell tonight, because the clerk who took the duty has no office to put the rest of it in — that is in the second clause, you may read it, it is his own clause. So I have written the hour and not the year. And there is no clerk of record, and the form has no box for a man, so I have written *no office of record* on the line, and the Trust's answer to that on the third floor is going to be a letter, and I would like the letter to be about a form and not about a person, so I am asking you for one thing."

"What."

"Your hand on the notice. Under the line, as the applicant, with the hour. The notice says who took the duty." She held the spike out. "It is not a signature on the entry and it is not the chain and it will not be read as either. It is the only place in this building where a man can put his name on the fact that he did a thing. Everything else tonight will be done by somebody else."

The lamp on the Chair landing was turned down to a bead. The tower was about to ring.

Marek took the notice and put his name on it, in the small upright clerk's hand, without flourishes, and wrote the hour, and gave it back, and did not say anything at all, and the tower rang the sixth bell of the night of the fourteenth of the rains, and the delay came out of him all at once, and it did not hurt any more.

It simply stopped.

The hand that had been on the page for six bells lifted off the bench and hung in the air, and the entry was a page, and the copy room was a room, and the case was open on a bench with a fair fast hand at the top of it and a boy's pencil at the bottom, and the settlement was due, and it was due that minute, and nobody in the building had done one thing about it.

Down on the road at the top of the Sill Road a Warden shut a valve he had been told to shut, out loud, in front of a hundred people, and the water in the Crown Terrace channel came up a foot and a half, and four hundred and thirty-one people were in a school house on Sill Row with a list of their names in a book tied to an examiner's finger with string, and a man who was going to be the cause of something walked up the north road in the rain with a lamp in his teeth.

In the Tally Halls, a settlement became due.
