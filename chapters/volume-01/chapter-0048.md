# Chapter 48 — Eleven Days a Season

The form came out of a drawer in Crown March on the twenty-second of next month, in a bundle of a supervisor's personal papers that a Trust officer carried forty miles and put on a trestle table in the Gate yard without a seal and without being asked, and Renata Vole did it in about nine seconds and then sat down and let the room work out what she had done.

"Form W-4," she said. "Supervised work, settlement schedule. It is a supervisor's instrument and the supervisor is in a cell, and his papers went into the cell with him because that is what happens to a man's coat. The Deputy's office has produced it. The board has not asked for it, could not ask for it, and has no power to make me. I have produced it because it is my office's form and I am the officer above the man who filled it in, and because a form that exists in a drawer in a cell forty miles away is a form that has been taken out of a man's hands for three days, and I do not think this office should be the kind of office that does that."

Nobody thanked her and she did not appear to want to be thanked and about two hundred people in the yard had not been in the room on the sixteenth and had no idea what any of it meant.

The form was one side of a sheet. It named a span, a number of days a season, a number of seasons, a start, and a witness, and it was signed with a supervisor's name and no other, which was how Hesper Rann had done everything for nineteen years.

*W-4. Supervised work, settlement schedule. Vale, T. — north-road span. Schedule written out, named, scheduled; independent witness, third party. First dry day, the 24th.*

The first dry day was the twenty-fourth of the rains and it was three weeks gone.

---

Tomas Vale came down to the Gate tank at the third bell on the twenty-second because his brother had sent word and had not sent a reason, which was correct, and he came in the middle of about two hundred people and stood there in a road-crew's coat with his hands behind his back, and about nine feet of that coat was there so that nobody would have to look at the rest of him.

He listened to the whole of it standing up, which took about two hours, and he did not interrupt once, and when Harrow had finished he said the first thing.

"No."

Harrow waited.

"You have all been very careful with me," said Tomas Vale. "I have been in four rooms in a month and every one of them has been careful with me, and I want to say that I know what that costs, because I have watched it cost my brother, who is standing at the end of this yard in a coat with a certificate he gave away on Thursday so that he could be permitted to stand near me.

"So I am going to say the four things I came to say and then I am going to go and work.

"First. Nobody is giving me a schedule. The schedule was in the loan. I read it in March of last year at this table and my mother read it and signed the box that says she signed anyway, and there are two witnesses on it and one of them was a stonecutter I have never been able to buy a drink for. Eleven days a season of supervised road work on the same span, and then a residual of days in the ward yards at the ordinary wage, and the form says all of it in the plainest words this city produces. I signed it. My hands were already going and I signed it anyway, and I have never once said that was anybody's fault, and it was not, and I am not going to start now that there are two hundred people standing in a yard watching a brother be brave about it.

"Second. The draw posted on the fourteenth as scheduled. Four months of twenty-six, in my own body, and the vigor has been working for five weeks and the span is up and the load path holds and there is a crew of six where there were three. Twenty-six months is twenty-six months. There is nothing anybody in Bell Stair can do about that and there is nothing anybody in Crown March can do about it and there is nothing in a hundred and nine yards of Tallow Ward that can do about it, and the last person who is going to come up this hill and tell me my years are coming back is going to have a very short walk back down it.

"Third. A schedule is a schedule. I will work it. Not as a favour and not because a man in a good coat turned up with a piece of paper, and not because my brother handed in the only clean thing he had to make himself able to stand in a room. I will work it because I signed for it in March of last year, in front of two witnesses, and because it is a schedule, and because a man who takes the help for the schedule and then lets anybody call it a rescue has misunderstood what a schedule is."

And then he said the thing that made the yard go quiet, and he said it to the yard and not to his brother.

"Fourth. My mother has a mark on my brother's arm with four notches in it and she has never told either of us where it came from, and I have known about it for a month and I have said nothing, and I am saying now that I have said nothing, in this yard, so that in about four years when somebody asks why a Vale wrote a schedule on the twenty-second of this month and worked it, there is one sentence in the mouth of somebody who was there, and it is not that somebody was helped. It is that a man asked for his own file."

He picked up the form, read it, and gave it back.

"There is one thing on it that is not right," he said. "The witness. You have written *independent witness, third party* and that box has been empty in this ward for six weeks and everybody in this yard knows why. There is no form on which a yard asks the city for a person."

---

"There is," said Sera Holt.

She was at the back by the tank steps in a Warden's coat over her own, and she had been there for two hours, and about a hundred and forty people had not noticed her, which is the only reliable description of a physician in a yard.

"I signed one of the two long sheets at that tank on the seventh and not the other, and I said once, and only once, and it was not because anybody asked, because a yard cannot ask and every one of you knows it. I said the rule is that a witness is somebody the ward cannot reach and cannot pay. That is still true and it has not stopped being true because a board in Bell Stair has now written a box on a form.

"So I am not signing because the yard asked me to. I am signing because there is no form on which a yard asks the city for a person, and a form with an empty box in it is a rule that has failed twice, and it has failed twice because the only people who can fill it are in a building on the hill, and one of the people in the building on the hill is a man standing at the end of this yard with a certificate he surrendered on Thursday, and he is the last person in Morrowfen who may fill that box and I would like everybody in this yard to understand exactly why."

"Which is?" said Harrow.

"Which is that he is the ward's own clerk of the day," said Sera Holt, "and the ward's clerk of the day signed the page under the totals, and his brother is the man on the form. I have been a physician twenty-six years and I have watched two hundred people destroy a claim in about nine minutes by being grateful to the wrong man, and I am not going to stand in a yard and let it happen for the third time in one season."

Marek Vale, at the end of the yard, in a coat, with nothing in his hands, found that he had been ruled out of the only job in a month that he had actually been wanted for, by a physician, on the grounds that he was the wrong man, and that it was correct, and that he was not going to be able to think about anything else for a day.

"I will sign it," said Sera Holt, "on one schedule and not the other, as I signed one sheet and not the other, and Mr Vale will countersign the other side as the Chamber's clerk of the day for the purposes of a form that has nothing to do with him, and the reason I will do that is that a witness who is only ever the independent one is a witness nobody can rely on, and I have been a witness in four hundred rooms and I have never once been the only one."

---

Vole read the settlement out of the file, standing at the trestle table, in the first person, in a yard, in about the flat voice of a woman reading a line off a page, and about two hundred people heard the whole of their brother's contract for the first time.

"Need: a body that can work the twelfth hour and the thirteenth for eleven days. Pledge: twenty-six months of the borrower's own recovery, drawn from his own body only, and the form says *his own* four times. Scope: the north-road span at Tallow, and its approaches, and nothing else. Witnesses: two. Due: the fourteenth day of the rains, the remaining draw, four months of the pledge, which posted. Service: supervised road work on the same span, eleven days a season, for three seasons, with an independent witness and a schedule written out and named. Residual: a number of days in the ward yards, priced at the ordinary wage of the district.

"That is the file. It is nine pages. Every part of it is present and none of it has been amended and I am not going to pretend that anything in it is a problem, because it is not, and because the man who signed it knew what he was signing and told a clerk on the nineteenth of the rains that he did not understand a month of recovery and understood that he had given it.

"The twenty-six months is gone. All of it. Four months of it posted on the fourteenth as the form required and nobody in this yard prevented that and nobody in this yard could have. This office converted a settlement schedule, not a loan. What is on this form is what the loan already said would happen, in the same words, on the same span, and the only things that are different are that the schedule is written out, and that it is named, and that it is hung on a board where four hundred people can read it.

"It is not a refund," said Renata Vole. "I am going to say that out loud in a yard because the Deputy's office has certified settlements in this city for nine years and has never once been in a yard when one of them was converted, and because I would like it entered that the difference between a conversion and a refund is that a refund gives something back, and this gives nothing back, and there is nobody in Bell Stair who is able to give anything back to anybody, and I have looked."

Then she read out the crew.

The schedule named six. Tomas Vale, at the head of it, because the scope named the span and the span is his. Bohum Ostrek, six years on the road, who took the crew's draw on the sixteenth of the rains on the same vigor form, twenty-six months, two witnesses, settlement eleven days a season at the ordinary wage, and who signed at a counter-desk and lost two weeks of the hand out of the middle two days later, lawfully, with a clause nobody has ever questioned and nobody is going to be asked to question today.

And four of the fuel-yard hands off Rezek's bench, named, of whom one was a household at the back of the Tanner's yard that had said on the fifth of this month that it would take the work in its own yard or not at all, and had been heard, and was in it.

The residual days went onto the same board as the ward's own levy, in a fourth column, under the yard roll and the oiled sheet and the two sheets of twenty-nine answers, and the nineteen working sheets went on the nail beside it with their strings on, because a record a person can lift off a nail is a record.

And then a thing happened that was not procedure and that about two hundred people came down the hill in the wet to look at, which is that the six men named on the schedule went up it.

They went up at the fifth bell and they stood on the span, all six, in a line, in the rain, and Aldis Kern asked them to stay where they were for about a minute so that a man on a trestle table in a yard could see how many of them there were, and there were six, and one of them was fifty-one and could not close his right hand and one of them was thirty-three and had two weeks of the hand gone out of the middle of him lawfully, and a boy of nineteen from the Tanner's yard who had carried a storehouse door up a cellar stair on the fourteenth stood at the end of the line with his hand on the rail, and the load path went under all of them and the rail held, and Milan Vray said so from the bottom of the steps, out loud, in the ordinary voice of a man looking at a thing he is in charge of.

"That is a span," he said. "That is all it is. It is a span and six men on it and it is up, and it is going to be up in November whether anybody writes a schedule or not, and I want that written on the board as well, because in about nine months somebody in a building is going to tell this yard that the north road cost the ward something, and it did not, and the only thing on that bridge that came out of a room in Bell Stair is a piece of paper nailed to a wall at the bottom of a hill."

Tomas Vale looked at the board for a while, and then at his own hands, and then said, "Well. That is a schedule."

"Yes," said Bel Harrow. "It is a schedule. It is in writing, it is on a board, and the officer of the Crown March who has to countersign it is on a road between here and the coast and will be here on Friday, and until he signs it I have to tell you that I do not know that it is lawful, and neither does the Deputy, and neither does the man in the good coat who wrote it in a room with a leg on it, and I have said all three of those things out loud in a yard and I am not going to say them quietly in a finding."

Tomas went up the hill at the fifth bell with five other men, and about two hundred people in the Gate yard watched the six of them go up it, and nobody in that yard said one single thing about what had happened to a clerk's years, because there had been no news about a clerk's years. There had been a schedule. There would be a schedule in March, and one in the season after, and then the residual, and there was not going to be a fourth thing, and every person standing in that yard had worked out the same sum and had got the same answer and about nine of them had a daughter of a school age.
