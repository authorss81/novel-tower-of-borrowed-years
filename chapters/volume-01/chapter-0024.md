# Chapter 24 — Two Books

The water office at Crown Terrace was a low building with a slate roof and a good door, and inside the door there was a bench for people who were waiting and no clerk at it, because the water office did not have a public counter; it had a counter for the district and a second counter for the public, and the public one was a hatch at the back that was shut on a Friday morning in the twenty-fifth of the rains.

Marek had a paper. That was the whole of his reason for being there, and it was a piece of Trust paper with a seal on it which said that the clerk of the day for Tallow Ward had applied, under the fourth term of the notice of the twenty-second, for the production of the maintenance record of the junction screen and bypass apparatus, and that the Deputy Steward had directed the production of it, and had directed, under the same fourth term, such other account books as the clerk should name.

He had named two. He had named them on the twenty-fourth, sitting at a table in the copy room, and it had taken him the whole of one afternoon to be sure of the words, and the words were *the maintenance record* and *the reading record of the same apparatus for the nine days of the rains*, and the reason the second was in there was that a man had once told him in a room that a counter-signature certifies two things, and he had spent the last two days of the twentieth of the rains discovering that a Trust can certify a wrong number for months without anybody noticing, because the wrong number is in a file and not in a body.

Neris Kade was on the bench and had been on the bench for an hour, because the Rainmarket season works take their water from the same office and she had come to ask about a schedule that was not this one.

"You have asked for the maintenance record," she said.

"I have asked for the maintenance record and the reading record."

"You have asked for two books that live in two rooms and are kept by two offices, and the man in charge of the first one is Hask and Rundle and the man in charge of the second one is a clerk called Vey, and between them they have never in nine years been in the same room." She stood up. "Also you have asked for them in a sealed investigation, which means that when they come out of those rooms they will come out with the Deputy Steward's seal on them, which means they are evidence, and you have not made an accusation, and I want that said aloud somewhere before we go in, because in about an hour one of us is going to be tempted and I would like there to be a sentence available."

"Where would you like it said."

"Right now. Out loud. To me."

"You are not accusing anybody of anything," said Marek. "I am asking for two books."

Neris Kade looked at him for a moment and then said, "Good," and went in.

---

Sander Vey was thirty-four and had a table and a window and a cup of something cold on the corner of it, and when he had read Marek's paper twice he said, "This is a great deal of trouble for a fortnight's water."

"Read the fourth term again," said Marek.

"Application for production, in an investigation, of the maintenance record and the reading record. Yes. I have never had one of those for this office and I have been in it eleven years." He looked at the two of them. "The maintenance book is not mine. Hask and Rundle keep their own. I can send for it and it will be here by the second bell and I will enter it in the record, because the term says I will."

"And the reading book."

"The reading book is mine. It is a wall book and it is not kept on this floor, it is kept in the counting room, and a reading record is not a document, it is a column."

Marek stood still.

"Say that again."

"It is a column." Vey turned a drawer round. "A reading is not a document. A document is something a person signs. A reading is a figure somebody copies off a dial at a certain hour and puts in a column, and then somebody signs a line under the column saying the column is the account, and that line is the whole of the certification. There is no other paper in this building about any of this. The maintenance book is about pipes and the reading book is about numbers. They do not live together and nobody here has ever had to put them on the same desk."

"That is the sentence," said Neris Kade.

"That is the sentence," said Vey, without understanding why it mattered. "Which one?"

"You said a reading is a figure somebody copies. And a maintenance is a thing somebody did." She put her hands on the back of a chair. "You cannot explain one with the other. I have watched three districts in nine years try, and every one of them ended with a document in a room that nobody could read aloud. A reading failure is a wrong figure. A maintenance failure is a stopped screen. They are not the same failure and they are not the same book and one of them does not cause the other, and if somebody tells you it does, then what they have given you is a reason, not a cause, and a reason is worth exactly as much as the person giving it."

Vey thought about that for a moment, and then he said, "You are the field examiner."

"I am."

"Right." He went to the door. "I will send the book down. I am going to send for it myself, because the last time anybody sent for it there was a business about a work ticket and I would rather the Deputy Steward's seal be on a book that I have put my hand on."

---

It was in the counting room at the second bell, and it was a wall book, and it was enormous, and it had been kept on a wall by a window for eleven years, and it was a quarter full.

Marek read a line, and then read the previous line, and then read the nine days of the rains across, and then went back and read them again, because the first time he had been looking for a lie and the second time he was looking for a mechanism, and there is no lie in it at all.

*Reading, the ninth of the rains, the second junction: six hundred and forty hundreds of pints.*

*Certified: S. Vey.*

And the eighth, six hundred and thirty-eight. And the seventh, six hundred and thirty-six. And the sixth, six hundred and thirty-eight. And the fifth, six hundred and thirty-four, which is a flood figure and is nobody's fault. And going back through the month before, four hundred and ten, four hundred and eighteen, four hundred and six, four hundred and twelve, four hundred and twenty, and the two hundred was not there, and then on the second of the rains it was.

Two hundred and some hundreds of pints a night, entered in a column, once a week, by a man who copies figures off dials, and certified under a line with his name on it, and in the ward nobody had ever been shown it and no one had ever asked to see it, because the yard counters are four private books kept by men the water office does not pay, and a private book is not evidence, and the Trust said so in a room on the fifteenth with a hearing officer's voice and no cruelty in it at all.

Neris Kade had the maintenance book open in front of her, and she had it open at the wrong end.

"You do not need it," Marek said.

"I need to see the day." She put her finger on it. "Here. The screen. Fouled, cleared, fouled again, cleared again — they come to this chamber four times a season and it has been on their list since the long summer and it is on their list now, and on the thirteenth there is a ticket and it is paid and the man who did the work is a Warden's crew and the payer's name on the ticket is *water office, at the expense of the district account.*" She read the line twice. "So the maintenance is real. The screen was foul and the ward was short and the ward bore it, and the ward did not know the ward was short because the account said the opposite."

"The fence," said Marek. "The cause line on the fifteenth. It said the excess arose from a failure of routine maintenance upon the screen and the bypass, those works being the maintenance responsibility of the district's irrigation contractor upon Crown Terrace account 4-Crown."

"Yes."

"And every word of it is on this table. There is a contractor. There is a maintenance responsibility. There is a date and a ticket and an account, and the excess is four hundred and eighty, and the maintenance failure is a fouled screen in a chamber that was on a list for four months, and the ward's short water was a hundred and forty people's pails." He put two fingers on the wall book. "And here is a different book, kept by a different office, that says the account's figure was wrong by two hundred hundreds of pints a night for nine days, and it was wrong because of a take-off that has no meter on it."

"You have your fence down."

"I have two books on a table in a counting room and a figure that closes, and I have not said one word about any person, and that is the part I keep waiting to fail."

---

They went down into the second junction chamber at the third bell with a lamp, because Neris Kade did not take anybody's word for a pipe.

The chamber was at the bottom of a stair of eleven steps and it was half full of silt that smelled of the Silt, and the ward meter was on the wall with its dial and its index plate, and above it, at the height of a man's shoulder, there was a boss of iron with a pipe going out of it and up the hill, and no meter, and a blank plate where a meter would have been, with the four screw holes still in it and painted over.

"Four holes," said Neris Kade. "Somebody took one off and screwed the plate back over the holes."

"Can you tell when."

"I can tell it was off within a year. The paint is newer than the plate by about that much." She put her thumb in one of the holes. "And the pipe is the same size as the garden main on the Crown Terrace side, because it is the garden main. It was cut when they laid the north gardens, and it was cut above this meter, and the water office metered the garden at the far end of it, where the garden is, and did not meter it here, where the water comes from."

"So the ward's meter reads the ward and the garden."

"The ward's meter reads everything that comes down the hill, and about two hundred hundreds of pints of it a night has been going up into a walled garden for eleven years, and about two hundred hundreds of pints of it a night has been going up into that walled garden since the second of this flood, and the only difference since the second is that somebody wanted it a great deal more than usual." She put the lamp down. "The garden knows nothing about this. I promise you the gardener knows nothing. He is handed a hose and a shift at the top of a hill and the water comes up out of the ground."

---

They got the reading book to Stillstep Island at the fifth bell, and Marek made the application in the vestibule of the Threshold in front of nine people including a stallholder and a queue for the public hatch, and read out the figures from the ninth of the rains, and read out the four yard counters for the same night, and read out the lawful standing schedule of one hundred and sixty hundreds of pints, and then he did the sum out loud in a public hall, which he had never done before and which was the single most frightening thing he had done in three weeks.

"Six hundred and forty. Four hundred and forty. One hundred and sixty. The ward drew two hundred hundreds of pints of water that went into a garden on Crown Terrace, and the yard counters have said so since the ninth and the account has not."

The vestibule clerk entered it. That was all he was asked to do and he did it in the ordinary way, and it went into the record of the investigation as a figure read aloud by a clerk, with no accusation in it whatever.

Then Vole came out of the third floor and stood at the foot of the stair with her hands behind her back and looked at the two books the clerk had brought up, and she had the reading book open before she had finished asking for it, because she had been reading a certified delivery for nine years and she knew what a wall book looked like.

"This is a wall book," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Not a copy."

"No, ma'am."

Vole read the ninth, and the eighth, and went back and read the second of the rains, and read the month before it, and closed it.

"Two hundred and something hundreds of pints a night," she said, "on an unmetered take-off above a ward meter, for the whole of the nine days of the flood, certified weekly." She looked up. "Mr Vale, the cause of this excess has been in front of this office since the fifteenth as a maintenance failure of the junction screen and bypass upon the account of the district's irrigation contractor, and it is not a maintenance failure of a screen, and the screen was foul, and the fouling is not the cause of the excess, and the excess is four hundred and eighty hundreds of pints of which two hundred was never in Tallow Ward."

"The contractor's maintenance is real, ma'am. It is a real failure and the ward paid for it in pails."

"Then the contractor's maintenance is one thing and the account is another, and this office certified the account, and the contractor will not be pleased and the contractor does not matter this morning." She handed the wall book back. "Corrected, the delivery for the peak night is four hundred and forty. Against a lawful hundred and sixty, that is two hundred and eighty. The ward's own roll, of which I have had a copy since the sixteenth, says two hundred and seventy-nine hundreds and forty pints." She looked up. "Twenty pints. In nine days. Clerk, the ward has had the right number on its own roll since the ninth and this office has had the wrong one on a wall for nine days, and neither of those facts has anything to do with anybody's honesty, and both of them have everything to do with where the book is kept."

Then she added a fifth term to her own notice, in the vestibule, in front of the same nine people: the take-off above the second junction meter, its date of construction, and the account upon which its draw is carried.

And that account was 4-Crown, and it had nine gardeners on it with wages against it, and it carried two hundred hundreds of pints a night of water that came out of Tallow Ward, and the money it carried was the district account, and the district account was two hundred and forty per cent over, and the ward was on that account too — not because of a levy, not yet, but because a district account is closed by charging the water it has delivered to the people it delivered it to, and for eleven years the ward had been the only body in the basin on which that closing figure could be made to balance.

Marek wrote the number down on the back of his hand in the vestibule of the Threshold at the fifth bell of the twenty-fifth of the rains, in front of a stallholder and a queue, and it was two hundred, and it was the first figure anybody had put in that hall for thirteen days that made the ward's bill smaller, and it also made somebody else's bill larger, and he could not at that moment tell which.
