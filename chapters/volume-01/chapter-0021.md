# Chapter 21 — A Ward Is Not a Person

The notice went onto the posting board at the first bell of the twenty-second, over the top of the hold notice, which had been up since the fourteenth with *no office of record* on the line and Marek Vale's name written under it as applicant, so that for the rest of the morning anybody who wanted to read about a delay in the settlement had to lift a notice about a settlement to see it.

The notice was Trust paper with a seal on it. It said that a Trust investigation had been opened into the water account of Tallow Ward for the ninth to the fifteenth day of the rains; that it would sit in the third floor at a date to be appointed; that the parties might attend; and then it ran four lines where the terms were.

*1. The junction account and its certified delivery, and the works carried out on the thirteenth.*

*2. The maintenance record of the junction screen and bypass apparatus.*

*3. The drawing at the four yard counters.*

*4. Such other account books as the Deputy Steward directs.*

Marek read it twice standing in the rain with a queue behind him. It was a properly drawn notice and nobody could fault the procedure behind it, and it covered, in four lines, everything that was arithmetic, and not one thing that was a question of anybody's conduct. Somebody in the Deputy Steward's office had taken a difficulty that had a person in it and written a notice with no person in it, and the notice was already sealed.

He took his hand off the board and made himself walk away from it, and the queue went on behind him.

---

Renata Vole's office was on the third floor with a window onto the water, a clock on the table, and a file that was thicker than the one she had had on the fifteenth. She did not offer him a chair.

"I am going to make you an offer, and you are going to do arithmetic in your head, and I am going to let you get on with it, because I have got a Crown Terrace irrigation account in front of me at eleven o'clock." She pushed a form two inches across the table, which in Renata Vole's office was an act of considerable warmth. "Fourth clause of the settlement desk's standing practice, second line. Read it."

It was the vacation of a posting where the assigned clerk of the day denies the assignment.

"Where was the file assigned," said Vole. "The desk's book, the ninth."

He went and looked. It was in the ninth's column in Hesper Rann's square hand, on a line he had read a hundred times in eleven days without seeing, and it was one letter.

*V.*

Not a name. A letter, in a supervisor's hand, in a column headed *assigned*, beside four other wards whose clerks had their surnames there.

"That is mine."

"That is yours. It is a letter in his hand. There are four people in this building who will say that on the ninth, at the sixth bell, Hesper Rann took an unassigned Tallow file off a bench and gave it to the clerk at the third desk. I am one of them, and I will answer it truthfully, and I have spent nine years building a reputation for answering things truthfully."

The other side of the form said: *Statement of the clerk of the day: that the file described above was not assigned to me as clerk of the day for the ward named, on the ninth day of the rains or at any time in the rains.*

Twenty-nine words, twenty-eight of them about a thing that had happened in a hall in ninety seconds, and the last one the word *assigned*.

"Sign it and I strike your certificate of posting," said Vole. "Not the settlement — the certificate. The one page that says the entry was lawfully posted on the eleventh. It comes out of the file and it comes out of your record, and what is left against your name is a file you were passed and a ward you did not certify. You hold your post. There is a raised mark against your name in nineteen years and there will not be another one."

"And the settlement."

"Certified on the fifteenth, by me, under my seal, at the standing schedule. My certification does not depend on your certificate of posting. It never did. That page makes a settlement due; it does not make it lawful. They are two different offices in this building and I have signed in one of them for nine years." She looked at him for the first time in ten minutes. "A settlement whose certificate of posting is void is not due and is not overdue. It is not payable. There is no date on it. It is a matter in progress, and a matter in progress is a matter I can hold for two years without anybody in that ward being asked for a pint of water by me or by anybody else."

The arithmetic started without his permission, and by the eighth line it was finished, and it took about forty seconds and was the single most useful thing he did all morning.

What the offer bought the ward: four households do not pay this season. The Rezek fuel-yard household is one of the four the posting has named since the ninth. Somebody in the low kitchens eats in the fourteenth month and somebody in the Fuel basin sleeps in a bed, and a woman in that ward has been telling her husband for thirteen days that the bill is coming whether they can pay it or not.

What it bought him: the only piece of paper in the world that was his, with *Yes* written on the party query in his own hand because he had been asked to and had not read what the query asked. He had built four years of his working life on the fact that he had not written the lie, and the certificate of posting was the reason. And his name, in his own hand, in a form in this room, denying a thing four people had watched happen.

He got to the tenth line and stopped, and Vole watched him get to the tenth line and stop and said nothing at all.

Then Marek Vale did the thing he had been doing since the ninth, which was to ask for it in writing, because a verbal offer by a superior in a room with nobody else in it is worth nothing.

"Give me the form, and give me the terms in the same hand, with a form number and a date on them."

Vole wrote on a slip in her own square hand — blunter and heavier than Hesper's — and pushed it across as well. Two pieces of paper made by the Trust, and one of them the first honest document put in front of him since the ninth.

"Sixth bell of the twenty-fourth. And I will tell you what it is worth, because you would work it out and I would rather you spent the forty seconds on the ward's water. It is worth nothing. You are the only man who can be asked, and two other names on that column in the last two years were asked, and one of them signed in eleven days, and he is at the intake floor now and he is thirty-one and he will end his career a good clerk. That is what this is: an offer made to clerks, not to you."

"You want me to sign it."

"I want the matter closed by somebody who can close it." She put the pen down. "Take both papers away with you. If you leave them on this table they are an instruction, and if you take them they are a question, and I would much rather you went out of here with a question, Mr Vale, because a question is the only thing I have ever been able to rely on in this hall."

He took both and stopped in the doorway with his hand on the frame.

"The hold notice on the board. Is it in the terms of the investigation?"

"No," said Vole, without looking up, "it is not, and it was not in my four lines, and if it is in the file it came in by nobody's hand, which is how anything gets into a file in this building, and you know that better than anybody alive."

He was three steps into the corridor before he understood what she had done, and he stood against the wall of the third floor for a while and let it come. A document that had been on a public board for eight days was not part of the inquiry she had opened, and she had said so in a room with a door in it, in a way that could not be repeated to anybody.

---

The ward came up the causeway again on the twenty-second and the Threshold turned them away at the door for the third time in thirteen days, and this time it was a hearing officer with a list.

They had a petition: three hundred and eleven signatures on the back of a fuel-yard tally, because the ward does not have paper. It asked for four things — that the ward be a party to the investigation, that the account's reading book be produced, that the four first households be told what they were paying for, and that the ward's water be accounted for at the yards and not at the junction.

The officer read all three hundred and eleven names without hurrying, in the doorway, with the rain coming in under the lintel.

"Captain."

"Aldis Kern. Tallow Ward water."

"I will read it twice more, because it is well written. And then I will tell you what it is, and it is not a decision of the Deputy Steward's office; it is the standing orders, and I have to apply them whether I like them or not." He held the paper up by one corner. "This is signed by three hundred and eleven people and addressed to nobody. A ward is not a person."

Nobody in the corridor said anything.

"A ward cannot petition. A ward cannot appeal. A ward cannot be cross-examined, because there is nobody there to be cross-examined, and a ward cannot be sworn, because a ward cannot speak." He turned the paper over. "Every form in this hall has a box for a person in it, and there is no box for Tallow Ward, and there has never been one. If I could put a ward in a box I would not know what I was swearing it to."

"Then who speaks for us," said Kern.

"Two people, and only two, and you will not like either answer. The clerk of the day, who is a person and a party and is named in the record." He nodded at Marek without looking at him. "Or you, sir, in your own name. You may attend, be heard, put questions, and be cross-examined. I want you to understand the last of those four words. When you stand at the end of that table you will be standing there alone, and none of the questions will be answered by anybody in this corridor."

"You cannot cross-examine four hundred people."

"I cannot cross-examine a ward, sir," said the officer, "because a ward cannot be cross-examined, and I am afraid that is the only reason it is convenient to me and not a mercy to you, and if you sat down there alone I would think less of you than I do at this moment."

Somebody at the back said, "Then he can ask us things in the yard." And the officer said, without turning round, "Yes. And whatever you tell him will be what one man says that four hundred people said, which is a worse sentence than the one you have written, and I would not tell you that at a door if I did not think it was true."

Kern stood a while with three hundred and eleven wet signatures in his hand.

"Captain," said the officer, "if you want to be a party I need your name in a box. One page. I will fill it in for you, because I have filled in four hundred and nobody has ever wanted to read it."

Kern looked at the form and did not take it.

"Not yet," he said. "Not until I know what a man in that room can say without the four hundred in it."

The officer put the form down on the ledge anyway, and said the petition would be entered as a document received and not acted upon, and that the *not acted upon* was the Deputy Steward's phrasing and not his, and that the words a clerk does not write on a document are the ones read out in a room six months later.

---

The last of the light found him in the copy room with the lamps going along the north wall, and the examiner was already there with a slate under her arm and four sheets weighted with a cup.

Neris Kade had a ward sheet of her own, and he looked at it for a long time before he said anything, because it was not a form and it did not come out of the water office or the levy office or the Fund. Four columns on four sheets, the headings in her hand, and the headings were the four the yard had given: the two sentences that kept coming back, and the two worse ones, which between them made the four. The top of the third column was blank, and she had left it blank and put the day on it.

"Twenty-second. I have been at the Gate yard every day since the nineteenth. Ninety-one people on four sheets, a date on each, and nine stops, and I have written the nine stops down as stops. And I have stopped keeping them in my own hand, which I have thought about for two days, and have not done, because I am fond of my own handwriting and I have a bad feeling about what I am starting."

"Read me the part I will want to argue with."

"I took it into the Threshold vestibule at the noon bell and laid it on the clerk's table and asked to have it entered in the record of the investigation, and he asked what instrument it was, and I said it was a record of examination made under a method published in this ward on the twentieth, and he said a method published in a yard is not an instrument, and I said it is the only thing in this city that has ever been made by a body first and a document second." She squared the sheets. "He entered it. He entered it because it is useful, and do not go looking for a better reason. It is the first time in my working life that a body has gone into a Trust file before the document did." She picked the sheets up as he reached for them. "You may read it here and you may not take it out of this room."

He read it standing. Ninety-one names in four columns, a blank over the third, and among the twenty entries he did not recognise a line in the levy office's hand that was not his name, because he had entered his own scar in the third pile on the twentieth.

*Fourteen. Left forearm. No instrument.*

He put it down.

"The investigation's four lines," he said. "You have read them."

"Arithmetic. The account, the maintenance record, the four yard counters, and such other books as she directs. Not one line of them is about whether a document is true, and I have been in this city nine years and I have never seen an investigation that was not a way of counting."

"No. It is a way of counting."

"Then somebody has to make sure it is the right count, and I have no standing to do that and you have none either while that form is in your pocket."

Marek did not touch his pocket. He said out loud that the Tallow file had been assigned to him on the ninth by a letter in a supervisor's hand, and that the Deputy Steward had offered him twenty-nine words, and that the date on the slip was the sixth bell of the twenty-fourth.

Neris Kade listened without moving.

"You know what you have just told me you are going to do."

"I know what I have told you I have not done yet."

"You have told me that there is a paper in your coat that says a thing that is not true, and a paper that says a thing that is true, and a date, and that you will decide on a Tuesday." She picked up her four sheets. "Whatever you decide on the Tuesday, decide it because you can live in a city with the decision. Not because you would rather be the man who said no. The second one is a costume, and I have seen it on better faces than yours."

At the door she stopped and came back, which she did not often do.

"And Marek. When you have decided you will want to tell somebody. You always want to tell somebody, it is the only thing about you that has never once got anybody hurt, and this week it is going to get somebody hurt. There is no form in this city for a man to say *I have a paper I cannot show you* to a person who is not a clerk. So I will say the sentence that is available instead: somebody in this ward has a thing he cannot produce, and there is no way to help him, and the reason is that he is right about the reason."

She went out. The lamps went about their business along the north wall.

Marek sat down and took out his own book and turned past the back page — the footnote and the margin and the three renderings of page three and the third copy of a nine-word cause line under a wrapper — and went to the last free half-page and wrote what the twenty-second had cost and bought, in two columns, because he could not stop.

*If I sign: four households do not pay this season. My certificate comes out of the file. I have two documents in a coat and one of them is in my own hand and says a thing that is not true.*

*If I do not sign: four households pay. I keep a paper saying I certified a lie was lawful. I have one document in a coat and it says something true and nobody will ever ask me to show it.*

*What I buy either way: the ward still pays a bill for water it did not use. I do not know how much of it it did not use. That is the whole of the work.*

The elbow came up in the inside of his right arm halfway through the second column and stayed, and the hand was not right for the rest of the evening. He wrote the date at the top and shut the book and added nothing, because the only thing worth writing twice in thirteen days is that there is no record of what a person should have done, only of what they did.

Then he went down to the Gate yard in the dark to find out whether a ward that cannot petition can still hold a meeting, and found that it could, and that there were sixty people in it, and that they were arguing about the Underbank.
