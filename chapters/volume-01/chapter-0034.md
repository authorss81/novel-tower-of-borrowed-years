# Chapter 34 — The Number Is Not an Accident

The ward re-listened the finding at the Gate on the fourth at the ninth bell, standing round a tank in weather that had given up trying, and Marek was not asked, and nobody came up the hill to say so, and he noticed the not-asking at about the fourth bell and then stopped noticing it because he had begun to be used to it in three days and that was the part that frightened him.

The family book was on the table when he got in at the seventh with the lamp, under a weight, because a thing left loose in this house did not last a morning. Lio was at the Fenner girl's and would be back at the ninth. Tamsin had her feet under her and had not put a seam on the ripper, which she did every evening of her life, and that was how he knew.

"Sit down," she said.

"There is a thing I have to ask you about the mark."

"I know there is. I have known there was since the sixteenth of the rains and I have been waiting eleven days to see whether you would get round to it, and you have not got round to it once, and I have been sitting here with a book open for two evenings deciding what to do with a boy who will not ask a question." She put the weight off. "So I have done it my side. Sit down and look at it and do not touch the page with a wet sleeve."

It was a house book, in her hand, about the size of a hymn book, with a canvas spine gone soft at the corners. It had been in the family for longer than her, and it was not a Tower thing and it had no office's name on it, and inside the front cover, in a hand that was not hers, somebody had written a date and the word *keep*.

She opened it at a page about a third of the way in.

The columns were ruled in ink and there were five of them and the headings were in a domestic hand and none of them was the word anybody in Bell Stair would have used. The first was *in*. The second was *out*. The third was a date. The fourth had no heading at the top, only a line, ruled straight, and the fifth said *carried* in the same hand as the columns, small and level.

And in the fourth column, on the third day of a month with no number written beside it, there were four marks.

Four notches, cut into the paper with the corner of a knife, or drawn so short and so evenly that the difference did not matter, at even intervals, in a straight line down the column, the way a person rules a line and then makes four small crosses against it because four is a small number and four does not need much room.

Beside them, in the second column, in a different ink, a name.

And the whole line struck through, from the first column to the last, with a single stroke in a third ink that had gone brown and sat on top of everything else on the page like a hand laid flat.

"That is it," said Tamsin. "That is the entry and I have taken it out twice in thirty-one years and put it back twice, and it is the only page in that book I have ever been frightened of, and I have never once been able to say why, and I have stopped trying, which is not the same as being at peace with it."

Marek did not touch the page.

"The word in the fifth column," he said. "What does *carried* mean."

"It means carried."

"That is a heading. It is not an answer."

"It is a heading and it is what my grandmother's mother called it and I have never seen it anywhere else, and I have had this book since I was nineteen and I have gone through it with a glass four times looking for other columns like it and there are four pages of them and three of them are about a horse." She took it back and shut it and put the weight on. "There. You have seen it."

"What I want to know is whose name is in the second column."

"No."

"Mother—"

"You have been asking me about a mark on your arm since you were eight years old," said Tamsin, "and I have refused you eleven times, and I am going to refuse you now, and I am going to tell you the reason, because you are twenty-eight and suspended and I have run out of ways of saying things to you in the middle of a sentence."

She put both hands flat on the book, which was the way she held a thing she was not going to let anybody take.

"You have never once asked me a question. Not one, in twenty-one years, and I have been waiting for it since you were seven. You have asked me *what is it* and *when did I get it* and *why is it that shape*, and every one of those is a question about a mark on your arm, and I have said no to all of them, and I was not hiding anything. I was refusing the question."

"What would the question have been."

"You did not ask it either, so I do not know, and I am not going to hand it to you." She took her hands off the book. "And I am not going to tell you whose name is in that column, and it is not because it is a secret. It is because the moment I say a name out loud in this room, that is what the mark is for the rest of your life, and you will carry the name and not the thing, and you are twenty-eight and you have a hand that can hold a pen and a book with four unusable things in it and I am not going to be the one who gives you a name to hold instead of a question to ask."

"You could be wrong about what I would do with it."

"I have been wrong about you four times and right about you eleven, and I am not going to be drawn on it tonight." She picked up the ripper and put a foot under her. "And Marek. Look at the line. Look at what is done to it and who has done it and what it is sitting on. Somebody struck that line out. Not the same hand that wrote it. And I did not do it and my grandmother did not do it, and there is no account of it in that book and there never was. So you have a page with a struck line on it and no authority behind the striking, and you know exactly what that is, because you have spent a month looking at one, and you are about to say something about it, and I am telling you now that you are not going to."

He said it anyway, and it took about nine words, and it was about a document.

"I will not," he said, and that was the whole of the answer he gave for a long time.

---

The arithmetic he did later, on his own stair, with the lamp, and it took eleven minutes and it was the last time for a month that he used his own head instead of anybody else's, and it produced nothing whatever, and he set it down here because it is what happened.

Four notches. In the fourth column, between a date and a heading that meant *carried*.

His own mark had four notches. He had carried it since he was seven, and the mark that came up on the fifteenth of the rains had four notches at the same intervals in the same place, and Sera Holt had said in one sentence that a notching is the kind of mark a yearline makes on a person when a delay has been put in the body and left there, and had then said, in the lane, in a hurry, that there was one line about it in a vault book on the second floor and it was the shortest line in the book and it did not say what it was for.

So the number four was on his arm twice, and a third time in his mother's hand in a book that had a *keep* on the front cover and no office's name on it anywhere.

And every one of those three was a record of a thing that had been put into a body and left there. He had known that since the twenty-fourth, when he had written *there is no form* in his own book in pencil, and he had not put the two halves together then because there was no room in him for the second thing.

There was room now. It made an answer and the answer was four and the four was a *count* of something, and a count is what a man writes when he cannot write a name, and the family book had a name in the second column and four marks in the fourth, and the name was struck through, and the striking was not the same hand, and the fourth column had no heading at the top of it.

It made an answer and he did not have it, and the answer it made was the shape of the answer, and he could feel the edges of it the way you feel the edge of a table in the dark, and the edge of it was the striking.

Somebody had struck that line out. Not the hand that wrote it. And no account of the striking anywhere, and not a signature on it, and no form for it. A line removed by somebody with no recorded authority and no stated reason, in a domestic book, on a page nobody was ever meant to read, and a woman in her forties had inherited the page and inherited a name she would not say.

He got the book out of his coat and opened it at the back page and looked at the four things on it, and then he shut it and put it away.

The first was a sheet with a footnote copied out of a page no clerk may copy, saying *copied from the original* in his own hand, worth nothing at all. The second was three renderings of a labour schedule that eleven households had never seen. The third was a certificate of posting with *Yes* written on a party query by a man who could no longer sign anything, which was the only one of the four that any office in this city would look at and was now the reason there was a slip on the posting board. The fourth was a sentence of nine words under a wrapper in the levy office's own formula for a cause.

There was a space on that page. There had been a space on that page since the sixteenth of the rains. It was the first thing he had ever been able to put anything in, and the four things were all either uncitable or stolen, and the fifth thing would have been a line out of a household book in his mother's hand, and it was not an instrument, and it certified nothing anybody could act on, and every word of it carried the bar with it.

So it stayed out. He did not copy it and he did not write down the date or the word or the four marks, and he made himself be quite clear, in his own head, standing on a cold stair, that this was a decision and not an oversight, and that a clerk who decides not to write a thing down is a clerk who has decided what the thing is.

He went down and helped his mother with a hem and did not ask her one question, and she let him.

---

On the sixth, in the copy room, Hesper Rann asked him whether the school would reopen.

He said it was the week bought by the difference in the record and it shut again on the sixth, and Hesper said *ah* and went back to his tray, and Marek stood there and understood that the man had asked about a school because he had been told about a school by somebody who had been told about it by a Fund's room, and that there is no form on which that is recorded, and that it had made a circuit of about four hundred people in a fortnight through a woman crying in a kitchen with a Fund form in her hand.

And that evening Lio, at the table, drawing, said the thing that finished the week.

"Everybody at the school is talking about you and I have had to sit in a room for six days while they did it, and I have not said a word, and it has been the worst six days of my life and I have done it because I said I would." She did not look up. "So you owe me one fact. Not a favour. A fact. And you may say no and I will not ask you again and it will still have been worth asking."

Marek looked at the mark on his forearm, which he had stopped hiding inside a coat three days ago, because a suspended clerk has nothing to be careful with.

He could have told her about the book. He could have told her that his mother had a page in a house book with four marks in it and a name struck through, and that nobody in Morrowfen could say whose, and that he had decided not to write it down.

He said: "I have not got one yet."

And the girl of nine nodded and went back to her drawing, and Marek Vale sat in a warm room above a lockkeeper's shop with a document in his mother's book and a mark on his arm and a sentence in his own head that he had not said out loud in nine days and was not going to say in a room, and it was the sentence Hesper Rann had given him in a supervisor's room on the twenty-sixth, and it was the only thing anybody had said to him in a month that he had believed.

*A man who hides a mark has decided what it is.*

He had not been hiding it for a fortnight. He had been carrying a page.
