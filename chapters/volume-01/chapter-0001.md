# Chapter 1 — The Ninth Day

The afternoon bell had rung twice, and Marek Vale had eleven receipt bundles left on his bench, and two of the three copyists who were supposed to be helping him had gone down into the wards at the first bell and had not come back.

That was not unusual. In the ninth day of rain the district copyists went where the water was, because a copyist standing in a doorway copying a list of dry goods is worth exactly as much as a list of dry goods. The Chamber of Accounts did not lose its people on purpose. It lost them the way a leaking roof loses plaster.

Marek did the work anyway. He had the good bench, the one under the second window where the draught came up off the stair and took the edge off the ink, and he had a method, and the method was the only thing about his life he had ever been able to describe without hedging.

A receipt audit was small and it was not exciting. A borrower walked in at a counter-desk and drew on a loan — drew on a year of being younger, or a year of a trade, or a year of ordinary recovery from a winter's worth of small damages. The Tower gave the effect now. The receipt went into a case. The case came up to Bell Stair, and Marek read the receipt against the sealed terms, and if the two agreed, the receipt was countersigned and the case went back down to the case-rack, and the entry was copied into the public register where anybody could come and read it without asking anybody's permission.

The sealed terms always had the same five parts, and Marek could find them with his eyes closed.

*Need.* What was to happen that could be seen or measured. Not health. Not prosperity. Health and prosperity are not things you can point at on a Tuesday. A gap in a gate that should not leak. A hand that should still work in the twelfth hour of the eleventh day. *This gate stands six hours.* That you could test.

*Pledge.* What was offered to pay for it, named and bounded. Three years of a trade. Ten days of a season's wages. A named man's recovery, twelve weeks of it, taken against his own body and nobody else's.

*Scope.* Where, how long, and over what. A contract for one gate cannot quietly power a second gate. A clerk who cannot say no to a scope is a clerk who has stopped reading.

*Witness.* Somebody who understood the promise and the immediate cost and was not being paid by the borrower. Not a saint. Somebody who knew what they were agreeing to and had not been handed the wrong page.

*Settlement.* The event at which the future consequence came due, and by what it was paid. Life, labor, care, restitution, or a transfer to a public pool that had agreed to take it.

Five parts, and a loan missing one of them was not void. It was worse than void. It was *false-witnessed*, which meant the Tower could not hide the responsibility of the contract in a ledger line, and so it went looking for a person.

Marek had never had to think about that last part in a case that mattered. He had checked witness boxes against witness statements, dated the entries, and once — once in four years — caught a date that had been written over with a different ink and a drier nib, and the clerk responsible had been moved to the copying floor and told never again to touch a receipt with a settlement clause.

Marek's eye for that was the reason the supervisor's desk knew his name. He could hold a page at arm's length in poor light and see the places where two inks had disagreed about the same year.

The eleventh bundle he opened belonged to a woman in the Tanner's yard who had drawn a fortnight of recovery on a lifting injury in order to finish a season of gut scraping. Need: fourteen days of a back that had already been ruined. Pledge: her own, twelve weeks, taken against her own yearline, and the form said so in the second line. Scope: labor only, no vigor, none. Witness: a tanner named Ovsek who had said, in his own statement, that he did not understand the first half of it but understood that she was the one paying. Settlement: at the end of the scraping season, and the amount.

The receipt matched. Marek countersigned it, and wrote the date in his own hand, and put the case in the down-tray.

Then, because Ovsek had said he did not understand the first half, Marek did the one thing the job allowed a third-grade clerk to do that was not a countersignature, which was to write a note on the file saying that the witness had declared partial understanding of the need and full understanding of the pledge, in those words, so that if anybody ever went looking, the honest limit would be in the record.

It took him ninety seconds. It would have saved her nothing. He had never expected it to.

By the time the fifth bell came, the bench was clear.

The public register wanted a hand-copy of every countersigned receipt, and the register was the reason the city could not be robbed in the dark, and Marek copied all eleven entries in a fair round hand without lifting the nib twice in the same place. He wrote the year as MR 412 and the day as the ninth of the rains and the hour as the fourth bell, because that was the hour he was finishing, and a receipt entered late and dated early is the first thing an auditor looks for and the last thing an honest clerk does.

Then he went to the posting board.

The posting board was a sheet of lacquered deal four feet by three, hung on the north wall of the copy room by four nails and one of those nails was not original. On it went every settlement date the Chamber had certified that week, in the order they would come due, so that anybody who wished to object to a settlement had a date to be in the building for.

Two of his were going up. One was Rainmarket, seed and store, small and clean. The other was a Crown Terrace water-main repair, which was the most expensive kind of paper he ever touched and had a scope that named four spans of pipe and nothing else, and he read the scope twice because on a Crown Terrace repair the extra clause was where people got hurt.

The board was clean. That was the pleasure, and he was honest enough to admit it was the pleasure. Eleven receipts, eleven clean entries, a board with nothing on it that would come back at him in the morning. Somewhere in this building there was a person who had been careless, and today it had not been in his part of the hall, and the Tower would go on functioning to exactly the extent that a clerk in a cold room did his work in the correct order.

He put his hand flat on the board to take the last of the small satisfaction out of it, which was a thing he did most days, and which nobody had ever thought to envy him for, and which was in its way the whole of his religion: a board is a board. Four feet of lacquered deal and four nails, one of them not original, and what is nailed to it is what was certified, and what was certified is what happened, and the two are the same thing because somebody in a cold room checked.

It was a very good sentence. He had never tested it, and it had never once occurred to him that a board could come perfectly clean and the fact behind it be dirty, and at the fifth bell on the ninth day of a flood, with forty-one wet petitions in a sack at his knee, he was not the man who was going to think about it.

He looked at the board, and he felt the small clean warmth of it, and he thought, without words, in the way you think a thing you have thought ten thousand times: *this is what the job is for.*

The tray under the posting board held the day's petitions, and the petitions had come up wet.

Someone had put them in a sack. That was the part he could not get past. A sack is what you use for coal.

He counted forty-one. He read the first nine and stopped, and then read the rest of the titles anyway, because stopping was how clerks lost a file.

*Petition of the water-yard captains of Tallow Ward, that the distribution at the four shared yards be reduced to the standing schedule and not below, and that the reduction be made public before it is made.* Eleven signatures, and Aldis Kern's among them, in a hand that had been taught by a clerk and did it very well.

*That the low kitchens at the Tallow Gate be supplied from the trunk main at the full schedule during the coming high water.* Thirty-one signatures.

*That the ward's water be not diminished in a time of flood.* Four hundred and six signatures, which was the whole ward, in nine hands.

*That the Yearspire account for the reduction made on the third day of the rains and for the pipe at the Fuel basin that has run dry twice since.*

He wrote each of them into the petition book in the order it came, with the hour of receipt, and the number of signers, and the ward, and the relief asked for. That was the whole of his authority that day. It was not much. It was enough to be worth doing, because a petition that is entered by the hour can be called for by the hour, and a petition that sits in a sack in a corridor is a rumour.

The clerk who took petitions at the Threshold had put a note on the top of the bundle in a hurry. *Wet, do not stack. Ward, water. Nobody upstairs wants these.*

Marek read the line twice and decided that it was a fact about a corridor and not about the ward, and he entered the bundle into the book, and he sent the book down to the desk of petitions where it would be routed to a hearing on its own time, because the routing was a matter of a hearing officer's morning and there was no such thing as accelerating it without lying in the margin.

He had done everything right. He stood there for a moment with his hand flat on the board.

Then Hesper Rann came out of the supervisor's room and stood beside him, which was a thing Hesper almost never did, and looked at the board, and said, "Leave the ward bundle. It'll go in the morning tray."

"There's a wet note on it."

"There's a wet note on everything this week. It'll go in the morning tray, Marek."

Marek looked at the forty-one. He looked at the note. He had four years of service and a clean record and the specific intolerance of a man who cannot leave a thing out of order, and the tolerance of a man who knows exactly which fights are worth having.

"It goes in the book," he said. "It goes down to petitions. It doesn't go in a morning tray."

Hesper's expression did not change at all, which was how Marek knew he had heard.

"Fine," Hesper said. "Leave the bundle. Don't carry it."

Then Hesper went back into his room, and at the door he said, without turning around, "Bring me the Tallow file when the bell's done."

Marek's hands had gone still on the board.

The Tallow file had not been assigned to anybody. There were four unassigned files in the rack behind him and eleven assigned ones, and he had asked for the Tallow one by name before it had a name on its assignment slip, which was not a supervisor's way of working, or it was a supervisor's way of working and the difference was that supervisors were not supposed to need names.

He got it out of the rack. It was heavy, which meant it had a schedule in it. He carried it back to his bench and looked at the assignment slip, which was blank, and then at the cover, which said *TALLOW WARD — WATER — SETTLEMENT FILE, SEASON 412*, and the copper clip at the throat, and the strip of sealing wax across it, broken and pressed back together once by somebody in a hurry.

He had time before the bell. The bell was at the sixth, and after the sixth the hall emptied fast.

He put the file on Hesper's desk instead. He did not put it in Hesper's hands, which would have been a performance, and he did not carry it out with the bundle under his arm, which would have been a theft. He put it on the desk of the senior clerk of the third grade and he said, "It's the Tallow file. It isn't assigned. There's a schedule in it."

Hesper did not look up for a moment. Then he did look up, and he was not a large man and he had a way of looking at you as though he had already read the page you were about to write, and he said, "Thank you. Shut the door on your way."

Marek shut the door on his way. He had been working in the hall for four years and that was the first time in eleven months Hesper Rann had thanked him.

Behind the door, he stood in the corridor and listened, without meaning to, the way you listen to a door.

Then he heard Hesper's chair, and the sound of a file being opened, and then a long quiet, and then the small dry sound of a pen being uncapped and a nib being charged.

After that, nothing at all for a while. Then Hesper's voice, raised not much, at the counter-desk end of the room: "Junior. The ward entry needs a countersignature on the totals."

And a young voice, careful: "Reading it now, supervisor."

"Read it fast. The bell's going."

Marek did not go back in. If he had gone back in he would have had to stand there while the totals were read, and he had already read them — he had read the whole file's schedule on the way up the stair, out of professional habit, and the totals were in order, and he would have said so, and the conversation would have taken four minutes he did not have.

The bell began. Six strokes, unhurried, the same six strokes it had rung every day of Marek's working life.

The young voice again: "Totals are in, supervisor. They balance."

"That is the definition of my question, junior. Sign."

Pencil, not pen. He heard that too. A junior countersigning a settlement entry with a pencil because the pen had not been issued to him yet, and then the file came out of the room and went into the case-rack, and the case-rack had a slot for unposted settlements, and the case went into the slot.

Marek went to his bench. He had left his coat on the back of his chair and he put it on, and he buttoned it, and he did not take his eyes off the closed door for a second longer than was natural.

Then he went down.

He had a rule about that, too, and it was the only one of his rules that was about the world instead of the work. He did not trust a file he had not walked. It was a clerk's rule, not a good man's rule; it had kept him from two bad arguments in four years and it had never once cost him anything he had wanted. So he went down off Bell Stair, through the Standing Market where the gutters ran like drains, and down the Sill Road with the rain going sideways off the eaves, and into Tallow Ward, and along the row of the fuel yards to the Gate yard, where the tank stood and Aldis Kern was standing in front of it with a lamp and his hands on his hips and eleven people behind him and no water in the tank at all.

"Clerk," Kern said, before Marek had got his coat open. "It went dry at the fifth bell. Not the fourth. The fifth. There's water in the main. Go and look at the main."

Marek looked at the main.

It was running. He could hear it, a low steady knock in the iron, the sound of a full pipe, and he put his palm on the standpipe where the frost had been painted over every winter for longer than the yard had existed, and the standpipe was cold and hard and moving, and the tank two yards away was dry to the sludge, and eleven people were standing in the rain watching a clerk decide which of those two facts was real.

He took out his book, and he wrote the hour, and he wrote what the tank read, and he wrote what the standpipe read, and then he stopped with the pen on the page, because he was a clerk and he knew what the two lines together said, and no file in Bell Stair had a column for it.
