# Chapter 16 — Nineteen Years

Hesper Rann's room was two doors along from the copy room and had been his for nineteen years, and it had a window that gave onto the water and a desk that had been somebody else's, and on the wall behind it there were four framed certificates, one of them older than the office.

"Sit down," he said. "Not the chair with the leg. That one's mine and I have not been honest with it."

Marek sat on the stool.

"Before you say anything," said Hesper, "I am going to tell you what I know, and then you are going to decide what to do about it, and I would rather we did that in that order than the other one."

"Go on."

"Somebody has been copying pages out of the Tallow file." He said it without any weight, in the voice he used for a housekeeping matter. "Not the ward's copy, which the ward has. The file's. And a person cannot take a page out of a held case, and a person cannot copy one either, and I have been a clerk for twenty-two years and I know what the copy room sounds like at night, and it sounded like somebody in it for two hours and ten minutes last night, and the light under the door went out at the fourth bell of the watch."

Marek said nothing, because there was nothing to say that was not a lie, and he was going to have to be in this room for a while.

"And I know who is asking the questions," said Hesper. "Because the questions are good. Whoever it is has gone at the file in the order a careful person goes at a file, and the order is correct, and about three people in this building could have found it, and one of them is a Rainmarket appointment with no reason to care about Tallow Ward and a reputation for caring about Tallow Ward."

"Neris Kade," Marek said.

"That is who I would have said."

Marek took that in, and put it where it could be reached, and said nothing else, and it was the first lie he told that cost him something, and what it cost was not the lie. It was the discovery, in the middle of making it, of how completely he had become used to it — six days of holding things, and this one fitted into the shape of the others so smoothly that he got it out with his chin level and his hands still on his knees.

"Yes," said Hesper. "I know about the hand."

"I know you know about the hand."

"I know you carried him down a lane instead of through a door." Hesper put his pen down. "That was the correct decision. I would like it entered that I said so, and I would like it entered by somebody who is not me, and I do not expect that to happen. The clinic's a mess and there were nine people in there with cuts they got carrying other people's furniture and every one of them is going to be treated free for a fortnight off a fund that the hall's clerks pay for out of their own wage at a rate of a halfpenny in the pound, and a great many people in this hall did not know that until last night, and that is the only good thing that has come out of this week."

He turned a file round on the desk and pushed it two inches towards the end where the hand could reach it.

Marek did not touch it.

He was aware of the ache starting up behind the right eye, of the hall going hard-edged at the corners, and of the fact that the file was lying open on a desk with the cause line facing up, and of the arithmetic he had been doing all week without admitting he was doing it: five times in five days, the tenth to the fourteenth, and a parcel in his coat he had eaten and could not remember, and a quarter of an hour of the twelfth night, and an hour on the night of the fourteenth with a man's voice in it that was gone for good. He had begun, on the seventeenth, to budget for the fee. That was the horror of it, and it was so ordinary that he had told nobody: he was a third-grade clerk with a good eye for mismatched ink, and he was working out what he could afford to spend on the thing that let him stop using it.

He took his hands off the desk and put them in his lap.

"No," he said. "Not that one."

Hesper looked at him.

"Not ever again, or not this week?"

"Not this week."

"Because I'll tell you what it will say, since you've decided to pay for it with your own eyes instead. It will say the totals are the totals." He put his thumb on the page. "That column is correct. It has been correct since the ninth, and it was correct in pencil, and the boy added it up twice, and everything on this paper that is a figure is a figure that will stand up. That is what the file will tell you, and it will tell you nothing else, because there is nothing else wrong with a figure. I have watched you go at this file for eight days looking for a number that isn't right, and there isn't one, and I have had that answer in my hand since the ninth and I have not been able to hand it to you because you would have taken it as a favour and gone away."

Marek read it by hand, at arm's length, in bad light, for the four minutes it took a careful man to be sure of a thing he already knew. It took him four minutes and it was exactly as Hesper had said. Every figure on the page stood where a figure was supposed to stand and agreed with every other figure on the page.

Which meant that the file was clean, and that the ward was thirsty, and that both of those were true, and that the second one was the reason the first one was available.

"Now," said Hesper. "Sit there and be twenty-eight for a moment."

"I can't."

"You can." He took a sheet out of the second drawer and squared it and put it in front of Marek and did not write on it. "Here is the thing, and I am going to say it once, and then I am going to let you think about it for a day, and if you come back tomorrow wanting to talk about it we will talk about it and if you do not, we will not, and neither of us will mention it again, because I am not a man who repeats offers."

He said it in a voice so ordinary that Marek had to stop listening to the words for a moment and listen to the voice.

"Your brother's settlement schedule," said Hesper Rann, "is cleared."

Marek waited for the rest of it, because there was always a rest of it.

"Not the years. Listen to me carefully, because if you hear this wrong in a room later I will deny it and you will have to take my word for it, which you have never once had cause to. The years are gone. The fourteen-day draw posted on schedule and the vigor has been working since and it is working now, and the years of the north road are in it and they are not coming out. Nothing I can do this morning or any morning returns a year of a man's recovery. I have no authority over that and neither does a Deputy Steward and neither does the man who wrote the reference volume."

"What, then."

"The schedule. The road work. The eleven days a season that services the loan after the draw, at the ordinary wage of the district, with the work named and scheduled and a witness against it who is not your mother and not your brother and not you." He put one finger on the sheet. "Written out. Named. Scheduled. A start date the twenty-fourth, if it holds dry, and a gang, and an independent witness, and a schedule that a man can read aloud in a room and be believed. It is the only thing in this building anybody can still give away. Everything else in that contract is spent. That is a schedule, and it is worth something to a bridgewright who has been paying for his own vigour out of a pot, and I am offering it to you, and I am not asking for anything."

"You are asking for something."

"I am asking you," said Hesper Rann, "not to file what you found last night."

And then he said the other thing, in exactly the same voice, and Marek heard it arrive and could not get his hand out of the way of it:

"And Marek — the ward's water was never the problem. The ward's *bill* is the problem. The water came and the water was always going to come, because the low kitchens are there and the basin is there and the river does all the arithmetic eventually. What is the problem is that somebody put a number in a file that has to be paid by four hundred people, and I put it there, and I would do it again, and I have thought about it for six days and I have not found the alternative and I have looked."

"You found the gate."

"I found the gate," said Hesper. "You want to know the truth? I found the gate and I found four dead people. That is what the arithmetic was. I did it in about nine minutes and there were four dead people in it and there are not now, and that is the entire moral content of the transaction, and I have not slept properly since the ninth and I have not stopped for one minute."

Marek stood up without meaning to.

The sheet was still lying in front of him, blank, and the offer was in a room with two doors and no second person in it, and it was the single most attractive thing that had ever happened to him.

"Write it down," he said.

"What?"

"Write it down. One line. The schedule, the twenty-fourth, the witness, the thing you want. A date on it and a form number." He heard his own voice do the thing it did, and he heard it, and he was frightened of it in a way he had not been frightened of anything in six days. "Because a verbal offer made by a supervisor to a third-grade clerk in a room with no second person in it is worth nothing, and I know that, and so do you, and if either of us ever has to use it, one of us is going to have to say the words *he offered it to me* and the other one is going to be able to say *he said that in a room on the seventeenth and there was nobody there*."

Hesper Rann looked at him for a long moment.

Then he took the pen and wrote on the sheet:

*W-4 (supervised work, settlement schedule). Vale, T. — north-road span. Schedule written out, named, scheduled; independent witness, third party. First dry day, the 24th. Countersigned, H. Rann, supervisor. S. 17th.*

He turned it round, and did not offer a pen, and waited while Marek read it four times, and then said:

"That is on paper and your name is nowhere on it and there is no second copy, and I am not going to tell you to take it and I am not going to tell you to leave it, and both of those are things I would have done at your age."

Marek left it on the desk.

He got to the Tally Stair and stopped on the Chair, four storeys up, in a stone stair that smelled of lamp oil, and stood there with his hand on the rail, and worked out, honestly, in the way he would have worked out a total, that he was frightened of how much he had wanted it.

Not of Hesper. Not of the file. Of a form with a date on it.

He had carried it six days. Six days of not being able to say out loud in a room *I suspended a settlement that was false-witnessed and a boy lost his hand*, and every hour of it had been building toward a thing he had mistaken for courage, and it turned out that what he had actually wanted, since about the eleventh of the rains, was for somebody else to be the man who said it.

And a supervisor in a room two doors along from the copy room had been about to give him exactly that, in exchange for a piece of paper that had been lying face down on a bench for six days.

And then, after a while, without taking his hand off the rail, he worked out the other half of it, and the other half was the worse one.

Not one of them had said the boy's name.

Hesper had known since the ninth that a junior of the third week had put a pencil to a column, and he had gone clean over it twice in that room — once in the middle of a paragraph about a clinic that would treat nine people free for a fortnight, and once in the middle of four dead people — and had come to the offer anyway. Marek had said *I carried him down a lane* and *I know you know about the hand* and had meant both, and had not once said *Ondra*. Two men in a room with a door in it, being entirely reasonable with each other over a sheet with a date on it; and the years out of the middle of a boy's hand, which was the whole of what this had cost anyone, done to him by two careful people who were discussing a schedule.

That was the part he would not be able to say out loud afterwards. It had not taken a villain. It had taken a supervisor with a schedule in a drawer and a clerk with a reason to want it, and it had taken about eleven minutes, and nobody in that room had been unkind, and the boy had been sitting one line further down than either of them was looking.

Down below him, off the Basin road, the north road went up out of the ward toward the span, and the two days of hard standing in the weather had given them four days' work to do, and there would be men on it at first light, and one of them would be his brother.
