# Chapter 35 — One House at a Time

The ward could not do one thing, which was ask a question in a room, and it turned out on the morning of the fifth that it could do another, which was walk up its own lanes and knock.

That was all the method was. There was no form, no standing, no clerk and no seal, and the ward had been unable to petition, appeal, be sworn or be cross-examined since the ninth of the rains, and on the fifth of next month four hundred people began at the Gate tank after the first bell and went out into the ward in nineteen directions, two of them to a sheet, with a piece of charcoal and a bit of string.

The rules were written out on two long sheets at the tank, one to copy from and one to read out from, and they had been taken off the back of a complaint sheet off a trestle table at that same tank on the twentieth, and the yard did not know that and had not been told.

*Ask at a door, one household at a time. Ask the household, not the yard.*
*Write the answer in the words it was given in. Do not improve it.*
*A stopping is a stopping. It is written down as a stopping and nobody comes back to it.*
*Two of you. A keeper's sheet and a reader's sheet. Never one keeper, never one reader, and a sheet does not go to a third person's house.*
*Nobody is to be asked why they are refusing, twice.*

And underneath, at the foot of each of them, in a hand that was not a clerk's: *An independent witness signs. Not paid out of the district account. Not living in the yard.*

That last line was the one the yard could not fill, and the yard argued about it for two hours and settled it the way it settled everything, which was by refusing to solve it and putting the shape of the hole in the place where the shape of the hole could be seen.

"There is nobody in this yard who can fill it," said Milan Vray, at the tank, to sixty people. "Any one of us would do, and that is the whole reason none of us can. A witness has to be somebody the ward cannot reach and cannot pay, and there is no form on which a yard asks the city for a person, and there is no office in this city that will send a witness to a tank because a yard asked it to. So the box stays empty, and the box being empty is on the sheet, and anybody who reads the sheet in six weeks' time will see a rule that was not filled, which is worth more to them than a name the ward found for itself."

Marek Vale stood at the end of the yard with a new coat and no assignment for the fourth day running and did not offer to help, and was not asked, and understood by the second hour that not being asked was the correct position of a man of twenty-eight who had been suspended on the strength of a word he had written on a query, and that a yard that takes advice from him now is a yard that has a suspended clerk at the end of its table, and that this is the single most useful thing he has done in a month.

Neris Kade was in the yard for two of the four days with a slate under her arm and did not get a slate of her own and did not sign a sheet and did not go up a lane, because the first thing she said at the tank, to all of it, was:

"If I hold one of those sheets, then in nine months there will be a return with a column in it, and the column will be headed with a name, and somebody in an office will say the examiner took it. I have watched that happen to a village in the Red Uplands over four years. The two of you hold both of your sheets between you, and neither of you lets go of either. I will do the thing I am for."

"And what is that."

"Standing where the asking is," said Neris Kade, "and not being able to help, which is the first time in nine years and which I am extremely good at."

Marek had one exchange with her across two days and it was at the tank on the sixth, in front of a hundred and forty people, and it was about arithmetic.

"You are the clerk of the day for nothing," she said.

"I am a man in a hall who cannot sign a form, which is a different thing and I am aware that nobody in this yard can tell the difference and that I have stopped trying."

"You are a man in a hall who cannot sign a form," said Neris Kade, "and if you put a hand on those sheets it is in a fortnight a fact about a suspended clerk in a ward's own bookkeeping, and in a year it is the reason the ward's own answers could not be produced, because they were produced with a man's hand on them."

"I have not touched them."

"I know you have not. I said it out loud so that sixty people heard me say it, because in nine days somebody in a room with a door is going to ask whether the clerk had a hand in the ward's consent, and the answer is going to be in the mouths of sixty people and not in a document, and that is the only way the answer survives." She put the slate under her arm. "You are welcome. It is not a compliment and it is not a warmth and it does not make us anything. It is a thing you did correctly, and there is not a great many of them in this hall, and I have started a list."

---

Sera Holt came down on the seventh at the second bell, in a Warden's coat over her own, and stood at the back of the yard for a quarter of an hour without saying anything, and then asked for the rule sheet, which is the one with the empty box at the foot of it.

"You are borrowing a rule about bodies," she said, "and you are borrowing it in a yard where no body has been looked at since the twentieth of the rains, and I have come to say the thing that nobody in this yard can say for you, which is that the rule you are using is the rule about a man lifting a pail, and a levy is not a pail."

"Then what do we do with the rule," said Vray.

"Use the half of it that is about a person. A person may stop. A person is asked once. A person's answer is written in their own words and not improved." She put the sheet down on the tank. "And here is the half you have not got and cannot get. A scar is dated, not counted, because a count is a number and a number can be carried into a room by somebody who was not in the yard. You have no scar in this. You have a decision, and a decision is the only thing you are allowed to count, and if somebody comes to a room in six weeks and asks how many of you agreed, you will be able to say a number, and that number will be made of households and not of people who were frightened. That is the whole of what your yard has learned in a month that a hall has not learned in nineteen years."

She wrote her name in the box at the bottom of one sheet and not the other, and said, "Once, and only once, and it is not because you asked, because a yard cannot ask and every one of you knows it. And if anybody tells you I examined anybody, I did not examine anybody."

---

They asked at forty-one doors in three days and got twenty-nine answers, and the twelve who were not there were not the same twelve twice, and one of the answer sheets has a line on it that reads *not at the door, at the yard, at the third bell* and another that reads *not at the door, gone to the low kitchens, asked there and the answer is on the back.*

The nineteen pairs came back to the tank on the fourth day and read their own answers out in the open, one pair after another, and Kern wrote them onto the two long sheets while a woman from the low kitchens called each line back to him before he put the charcoal down. It took the better part of an hour and a half and two hundred people stood in the wet for it. Nobody kept a sheet alone and nobody read one alone. The twenty-nine answers went onto two lines in the words the households had used, the three refusals onto a third with the reasons written under them, and the nineteen answer sheets went onto a nail by the tank with their strings still on, because a record a person can lift off a nail is a record and a record a person has to ask somebody for is a rumour.

Three households said no on the first pass, and the three reasons were three different kinds of no, and two of them were perfectly reasonable, and the third one was the finding of the week.

The first was at a door at the back of the Tanner's yard, from a household that had been on the roll since the ninth and had never once been told what the roll was.

"We are not being difficult, we are being somewhere. The work you are describing is in the fuel yards and at the Gate. I have a sister at the Gate and I am not going to have her say to my mother that my mother's days are on the Gate tank, because that is a thing a yard says about a family. Put the work in our own yard or put the days in the Gate yard and let us be there."

"That is fair," said Vray, writing.

"And nobody is going to have it, and I would like it on the sheet that I asked for it anyway and was told no inside a minute."

The second was a household of five at Ropewalk that had carried pails for twelve days and had not been asked to by anybody, and whose answer was given standing in the doorway with the door open behind them.

"We will take the work. We do not want the days."

"That is the whole of the levy."

"Then the whole of the levy is the wrong shape." They said it without heat, the way people say a thing they have been thinking about for a fortnight. "You have come here to ask us for water again. Not for the water we drank. For the water we did not drink, because that is the figure on your roll, and I have looked at the figure on the roll and I cannot get it to make sense with the pails I carried, and neither can anybody else in this lane. And now you have come for it a second time in a different coat. No. Not that. Take the work and leave the days alone, and if you cannot do that, do not come up this lane again."

The third was Nadja Ostrow, and it took the rest of that day and most of the next, and it was not reasonable at all, and it was the only one of the three that nobody had seen coming.

"You are asking me to take a public levy in days at the ordinary wage." She said it standing in the low kitchens with two hundred people behind her and a ladle in her hand. "I know what that is. I have heard the word all week. A day of mine is a day off the district account, and the district account is the account my daughter's place comes out of, and my daughter is off the roll since the twenty-fourth of the rains."

"Nadja—"

"I am not finished." She did not raise her voice and the room went completely quiet, which is the thing about two hundred cooks. "I have got her name in a margin of a finding. I have got her name in a margin in a hand I cannot check and a number against it that nobody has ever read to me, and that is the entire reason the clerk in that building has been able to put us all in a yard for a month. And now there is a man from a Crown office coming down the hill to make a room, and the room wants the ward to take a levy, and the levy comes out of the same account that took my daughter's place in a school, and I want to know which of you came up this lane to ask me for it and did not know that."

There was nobody in the kitchens who could answer that, and the silence went on long enough that a child at the back of the room started crying, and Nadja Ostrow turned round and looked at the child and then turned back.

"Write it down," she said. "I am not saying no to the work. I am saying no to the days, and I am saying it because you cannot ask a woman for a day out of an account that has already taken her child, and if you write down any other thing I have said then you are worse than the office and there are four hundred of us who will know."

The yard wrote it down in her words, which took nine minutes and two people, and it went on the sheet with the rest, and no more was asked of her, and nobody came back to it, and on the fourth day Kern had a line drawn through the yard's tally at Ostrow's door in charcoal, and the drawing of the line was his own hand and it was not a cancellation, and he told the tank that it meant *asked once and the answer stands.*

---

On the eighth at the second bell the two long sheets went on the board at the Gate, under the yard roll and the oiled account of the fourteenth, and the nineteen answer sheets went on the nail beside them with their strings on, and the tally of doors hung off the same nail, and the three no's and the number of no's were written in the open where anybody could count them, which is the first time in four hundred years that a number about this ward was published by this ward and it was a number of decisions and not a number of people.

Nineteen came past the board before the lamps and eleven of them said a sentence about the column with nothing at the head of it, and every one of the eleven was a new one, and the top of the column was still blank, and the yard voted in four minutes, and against anybody's advice, to leave it blank, on the grounds that a heading is the one thing on a sheet that cannot be put in afterwards by anybody who was not there on the day.

Marek stood at the end and did not go up the hill to write any of it down, and the two things he had come down to the yard with were in his coat and stayed in his coat, and the reason is worth saying because it is the reason he was in the yard at all.

He had come because a ward that is going somewhere is a ward he has a place in, and the roll of the pail water was in forty hands on a board and could not be carried into a room, and the oiled sheet of the forty items was in forty hands and could not be carried anywhere either, and the four things in his own book were worth nothing to any of them, and the fifth thing was in his mother's book and was not his to give away.

And he had come down to the yard, on the fifth, with a suspension slip in his coat and four things in a book, and the ward had gone out into nineteen lanes and come back with twenty-nine answers, and one of them had asked for a figure that only exists on a piece of paper nailed in a frame on a wall in Bell Stair, and had refused to pay until somebody wrote that figure down in a room where she could hear it said out loud.

There was a schoolmistress in the city who had been asked for exactly that number on the thirtieth of the rains and had refused to say it, in a copy room, in front of a clerk and a district accountant, in terms.

The finding of the week had come up out of a lane in Tallow Ward and it was a demand for a figure, and the only person in Morrowfen who had refused to supply that figure was the woman it belonged to, and she had refused for a reason nobody had thought of either.
