# Chapter 33 — A Schedule of What Comes Back

The back room of the Fund's office over the chandler's shop had a bench, a bad lamp, a shelf of empty forms, and after the sixth bell on the third of next month it had a table, because Ondra Kelb had brought a table up the outside stairs on his own with a hand-book maker's boy and would not let him carry the other end.

The rule on the sheet of paper was the whole of it.

*1. A thing is not done again until it has been done clean three days running.*
*2. A stopping is written down as a stopping, in the hand of the person who stopped.*
*3. A day that cost nothing and was worse than the day before is a day. It is written down as a day.*

Four columns and a ruled line. *Day. Hour. What. What it cost me.* He had ruled it with a straight edge and a nail and the hand-book maker's boy had held the straight edge, and the columns were as true as anybody in the Bell Stair could make them.

Marek came up at the seventh bell with the lamp and put it on the table, and Kelb turned the sheet round so the top of it was facing him, which was a thing he had decided on about the eleventh and had never said out loud.

"Third of the month," he said. "I told you on the twenty-eighth I would do the column today and you said nothing, which I have decided was the correct response."

"You told me you were going to do a thing to your hand on a day you picked in advance and record the cost, and I said nothing, because a boy who has picked a day in advance and written a rule for stopping is not a boy who needs a man in the room to hold the door."

"I am a man with a table, a bad lamp, and a rule I wrote myself, which is half the men in this hall. You are the witness."

"I am suspended."

"You are the only person in this city who is not paid out of the district account and does not live in the yard." He said it without any weight, in a young man's administrative voice arranging facts. "The rule says an independent witness. Vendl is paid by a levy on the hall. My mother lives on Sill Row. You have a stoppage in your record and it is the only thing about you this month that has made you independent of anything, and I have decided I am not going to apologise for it."

"I am not going to be thanked for it either."

"No, and you will not be." Kelb took the sheet back and squared it. "Sign at the bottom, where it says *witness, not resident in the yard, not paid out of the district account*. And you are not to witness anything after today, because on the seventeenth your hearing is, and because if you sign a second one you will be the first person to come up here since the ninth of the rains and everybody in the Fund's room will know what it means." He put the sheet down. "Right. The hand-book."

Zorka Vendl had got it made on Ropewalk by name, which had cost her a phone call and had cost the Fund a rate she had entered in the book without comment, and it came to the back room in a paper wrapper with a maker's mark on it and a line about the fingers.

Kelb put it on.

It was a good thing. Leather at the palm and across the first joint, laced up the back of the hand and around two fingers, made for a hand that was going to do fine work again and needed to hold a pen for six hours without letting go of it. He laced it with his left hand in about forty seconds because he had watched it being laced on a bench in Sill Row for about a week.

Then he took the copy room's spare pen out of his coat and wrote four lines on the test strip.

The lines were steady and level and as good as the hand-book could make them, and the maker had a name in this city that people waited six weeks for, and Kelb wrote at the speed of a man in his forties and the ink did not blot.

Zorka Vendl was in the doorway.

"There," she said.

"That is the wrong hand, Mrs Vendl."

"Mr Kelb—"

"Watch." He turned the strip round. It was a fair fast hand, every letter of it in place, the sort of hand the copy room could use on a schedule. "That is what I have been doing with my left one for four days. That is *my* writing, and it is a nine-year-old's, and it is slow, and I can do about a page of it before my shoulder goes and then I cannot do any more until I have slept. And the one you have just watched me do is a stranger's, and it is better than mine, and it is not mine."

He took the hand-book off and put it on the table and put his right hand flat beside it, and the right hand sat perfectly still, warm, and did not shake and did not do anything.

"Eleven months ago that hand would have written the four lines as well and then written nine of them again because the first four were not in my best hand, and it would have kept the column in a desk's book for two hours without my shoulder knowing it was in use." He put the strip down on top of the hand-book. "So the apparatus is working. And that is the entire finding, and I have written it up, and I would like you to read it, because I have written it in the column headed *what it cost me* and it has cost me nothing at all, and I want a witness to have read that sentence before I stop believing I wrote it."

---

The experiment was set for the fourth bell and it took eleven minutes and it was the only thing that happened in the back room that day that was not the table.

He had a column of twenty lines off the issue ledger — volume numbers, dates out, dates in, and the pencil tally of pages, which was the work he now had, and which nobody had thought to check and which he had checked twice anyway, because he is a clerk and it is not in him not to. He had done that column in the right hand a hundred times in a copy room on two floors.

"Twenty lines," he said. "I stop at twenty whatever happens, because past twenty it stops being a column and starts being writing, and I am not testing writing. And then four days off the right hand whatever it does, and if the four days are the fourth then the experiment was wrong and I write *the experiment was wrong* in the third column and that is the result too."

"And if the right hand does nothing at all for four days."

"Then it has bought four days I did not have and I write that as well, because a hand that costs nothing is not a hand that does not cost, it is a hand that has not been asked yet." He squared the ledger. "That is the rule. That is the whole of the rule. A man who has not looked at a thing cannot know what it costs him, and my mother's rule is that you do not touch a person you are not looking at, and it is the only one I have ever heard that makes sense."

He wrote.

The lamp was bad and the table was the wrong height and the pen was the copy room's spare and the strap of the hand-book was off, because he had decided in advance that the experiment was the hand and not the hand-book, and a man who adds a variable to an experiment because the variable is one he owns is not doing an experiment.

Twenty lines. Four seconds, nine, eleven, and then it went, and the figures came out with the top halves of them too large and the ones and the sevens joined at the top and the tails of the sevens going the wrong way, and every one of them was correct.

He put the pen down.

"Right," he said. "Four seconds."

He wrote it out in the schedule in the left hand, in the fair fast nine-year-old's hand he had got good at by the second, and it took him nine lines to say the whole of it.

*Third. Fourth bell. Column of twenty lines from the issue ledger, ordinary hand, no strap. Took four seconds, nine and a bit. Correct throughout. Figures the shape of a child of nine, which is the shape they were at on the ninth, and which I have known since the fourteenth of the rains when I tried to write my own name in a clinic tray.*
*Cost: unknown, to be established. Nothing felt at the time, which I am told is not information.*

Then, underneath, before he could stop himself:

*Note. My mother cannot lift the basket now and I cannot, and that is the cost and I have put it in the wrong column because there is no column for it and the column there is, is the wrong one, and I do not know what the right one is called.*

He read that back twice and left it in, and Marek read it over his shoulder and let him.

---

The next four days are the rest of the account and they are not dramatic.

The right hand was dead for all four, which is the physician's word and the only one in this account. He could move it and it would take nothing and it would hold nothing, and he did the work with the left, and by the second day the left tired at about the two-hour mark and by the fourth at ninety minutes, and he wrote that down on the fourth with the hour on it and nothing beside it, which was the rule.

On the second day his mother tried the basket and could not do it, and stood in the laundry holding forty pounds of wet linen in two hands and looked at it.

"The experiment," she said.

"The experiment. I could have asked somebody to hold the straight edge this morning instead of asking for the basket back."

"You could have." She put the basket down square, the way a person puts a thing down when the next thing that happens is going to be somebody else's decision, and said, "And I have four sheets to wring," and went back to her board, and neither of them said one word about it for eleven days, which was longer than she had ever gone.

On the third day he came in to the back room at the seventh bell and found that a man from the copy room had been and gone and had left on the table a strip of paper with a figure on it, and it took him about eleven seconds to work out that it was a man from the counter-desk, three weeks his senior at the same desk, who had come to look at a schedule of a man he had watched go down on a stair, and who had written down how many pages of a fair book he could copy in an hour in his ordinary hand, and had left it, and had not signed it.

Kelb put it on the board over the table where the yard could not see it and where Vendl could not take it down, and copied the figure onto the schedule under a heading he had to invent, and the heading was *what somebody else can do*, and he noted underneath that the rule in line three did not provide for this case and had therefore been extended, in his own hand, on the third, by him, with no authority whatever, and that this was the first rule in the whole business that he had broken.

---

On the seventh day Marek came up with the lamp, with the right hand still dead, and Kelb turned the sheet round.

"Read the third day," he said.

Marek read it.

*Third. Fourth bell. Column of twenty lines, ordinary hand, no strap. Four seconds, nine and a bit. Correct throughout. Figures the shape of a child of nine. Cost: unknown, to be established.*

And under it the note about the basket, and under that the two lines of the third rule.

"You have made a finding," said Marek.

"I have made an observation and I have drawn a conclusion and they are both in the same hand and I am not sure they should be." He took the sheet back. "You will tell me that the right hand is gone, and you will be right, and I have known that since the fifteenth of the rains and it is not a finding, it is a thing I was told by a physician with the door shut. What I do not have is the order. You do not lose a hand all at once. You lose a season of it, and then a season of something else, and there is a shape to the order and the shape is different for every man, and the shape is the only thing anybody is going to be able to hand me in eight years when I am twenty-eight and deciding what to be."

"Who is going to hand you a shape."

"Nobody, and that is the sentence I have been getting at since the fifteenth and have not been able to say out loud until the third." He put his right hand flat on the table, palm down, and did nothing with it. "So I have made a table, and columns, and a rule about stopping, and I have four days on it, and I have what somebody else can do written down by a man who came up here and would not give his name. And one column has nothing at the head of it, and I have known that since the second and have not told you because I did not want to be told."

Marek looked at the sheet. There were five columns. The fifth was headed *what it cost me* and the fourth was headed *what somebody else can do* and the third, between *what* and *what it cost me*, was headed with a line ruled straight and nothing above it, and under it, over four days, he had entered seven words.

*what I am for now.*

"Explain that to me," said Kelb, "or refuse to, but do it in one of the two ways."

---

Marek did it in the second way and it took him a moment to get into it, and when he came out of it he was aware that he had been somewhere he had not expected to go on a Saturday evening over a table in a storeroom.

"I cannot answer that," he said.

"Can you not answer it or will you not."

"There is no difference between the two this time."

"That is not true and I am going to let it stand for about a week, because you are the only witness I have." Kelb put the sheet square. "I will tell you why you cannot, and then you can do what you like about it. You have not got one either. You have got a book with a page at the back of it and on that page a column with a ruled line at the top of it and no heading, and it has had no heading since the twentieth of the rains, because forty-one people came past a table in that yard and gave you forty-one answers and you wrote all forty-one down and left the top blank on purpose, and you have been carrying it about for a fortnight and have not once asked anybody to fill it in."

Marek did not move.

"I know there is a column," he said. "I did not know you knew about it."

"I know that a man with four columns in front of him and a fifth with no name on it is a man who has run out of names, and I have watched you for three weeks not ask one person in this building one question they could not answer, and I have decided that is not a virtue. It is a man who has run out of paper." He put the pen down. "And the two of us are sitting here with the same thing, and it is not the hand. We are twenty and twenty-eight, we have a table and a rule and a column with nothing at the top of it, and neither of us is going to get an answer this month. The difference is that I have written mine down with the date on it."

He put his hand over the column, flat, so that Marek could not read it, which was the first unkind thing he had done to anybody since the fifteenth of the rains.

"You may look at it again next Saturday," he said, "and if you have got a name by then I do not want it, because I want to see whether you go and ask anybody. That is the whole of what I have wanted out of the eighth of this month, and it has nothing to do with my hand, and you may tell the Fund's room that it has nothing to do with my hand, because they will ask."

Marek went down the outside stairs of the chandler's shop into the rain with a lamp he had carried up four floors and carried down again for nothing, and stood on the Sill Road pavement and understood the shape of what he had just been told, which was that a boy of twenty had asked him for the one thing in Morrowfen that Marek Vale had failed to produce in public in the last forty days, and had asked for it as a favour, and had told him in advance that he would refuse the favour if it arrived too easily.

He had four things in his book and he had been asked for a fifth, and for the first time since the ninth of the rains he was aware of what the four had cost him, which was that a book you cannot produce is worth exactly as much as the sum of what you have been unable to do with it, and that the sum was a column with a blank head and a boy of twenty sitting in a storeroom with a lamp that was not good enough and a week to get it right.

He did not go and ask anybody.
