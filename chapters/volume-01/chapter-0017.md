# Chapter 17 — The Vigor Bill

The north road above Tallow had four days' work in it and a gang of nine on it and a foreman who took his orders from a bridgewright who was also a clerk's brother, which is a sentence that had never once been a problem in Tallow Ward before the ninth of the rains and was now the first thing anybody said when they came up the hill.

Marek came up it at the first light on the eighteenth with the twenty-fourths in his head and a sentence ready about a man's hands.

He had eleven days, and the eleven days began with a man carrying a barrow, and the barrow was a hard thing to carry because the going had gone to churn and there was no stone laid on the two hundred yards above the Tanner's, so a man had to put his weight in and walk it up and the work at the top of that pull was a bridgewright's own work and Tomas was doing it, because it was his span and because he had a crew of nine and one pair of hands that could do what a pair of hands does.

He had the sleeve over the left wrist, because the right hand was the one that was going.

"Vale!" Tomas called down without stopping. "If you've come about the settlement, you can go back down, and I'll tell you why in front of these nine so that you can tell your office, and then you can tell them you did."

"I haven't come about the settlement."

"Then you've come about my hands, and it's the same office that gave me the schedule in the first place." He set the barrow down and came down the pull backwards, unhurried, and got to the bottom of it, and looked at his brother. "Say it, then. You've been saying it in your head for a day and a half. *Tomas, you must stop. Tomas, you have a family. Tomas, a man who cannot hold a barrow is a man who cannot be the only bridgewright in a ward.* Say it, because I want it on the road, where nine men heard it, so that when I say no it isn't just me being a stiff."

"Stop for the winter term," Marek said. "Not for good. Eleven weeks. The span will stand and the crew can be paid out of the gate levy and you will be no use to anybody standing on a cold deck with a bad hand, and that is not a plan, that is arithmetic."

"That's your arithmetic. I'll do mine." Tomas wiped his forehead with the back of his wrist. "There is a schedule, is there. A written one. Supervised road work, named, dated, with a witness on it who isn't family."

"There is."

"Then I don't want it." He said it easily. "Do you know what a named schedule is, on a contract like mine? It's a sentence with my name and a date in it and a wage in it, and it's true, and it's the difference between a man who is owed something and a man who is a *line*. I've been a line for thirty-four years. My father's a line. My grandmother's a line — she's on that roll of yours, seventh of eleven, still counted, and she's been dead eight years and nobody has ever struck her out. So I know exactly what it is to be a line in a book, Marek, and I would rather keep the vigor and pay it myself out of a pot than have a witness on me with a date in it that a room can read out loud."

"They're not the same—"

"I know they're not the same," Tomas said. "I signed a good contract. I read it, I had it explained, and there were two witnesses and one of them was our mother and she said *I am signing anyway*, and I'd have said it too. Nobody in this family got talked into a vigor clause. I want that on this road. Whatever else gets said about the Vale family in the next month, that's the shape of it, and if it gets said here it gets said with me standing in front of the nine who watched me refuse it."

then the gang went back to work in a way that made it clear the whole of it had been for Marek, and Marek stood on a wet hill above a churning yard feeling, with a completeness that frightened him, that he had come up here to do two things in a particular order and had done the second one first and had thereby lost the first.

Then the lift on the Tanner's side went wrong.

It went wrong the way these things go wrong, which is that nothing happened for a long time and then a thing happened very fast. Bohum Ostrek, thirty-three, six years on this road, a hand who had been raised in the salt country and had never once in his life asked anybody for anything, had the barrow end of the winch and had it up on his knee because that is where a barrow end goes, and the chain came off the drum an inch and he put his weight back to hold it, and both of his hands locked.

Not cramped. Locked. The hands went into a shape they had never been in and stayed there, and the winch came down an inch onto his knee, and it took Petross Oleksen, two other men off the gang, and Marek — who was on the hill and came down it faster than he had come up it — to get the drum stopped and the chain off him.

It took about forty seconds. In the eleven seconds before Petross got there, Ostrek sat down in the wet on his own initiative, which is a thing a man does when he understands what is happening to him, and said one word, twice, and it was not a scream and it was not a name.

Then he lay on his back on the mud and looked at his own hands, which were still in the shape.

"Right," he said. "Right. That's the fortnight, that."

"It is what?"

"Two weeks." He said it perfectly level, lying in the wet, looking at his hands. "I took the crew's draw on the sixteenth of the rains. Same form your brother has. The bond's the gang's and the draw's the man's, and I went down to the counter-desk on the Wednesday and signed for twenty-six months of my own recovery, scope the north-road span and the load path, two witnesses, one of them Cveta, and the settlement is eleven days a season at the ordinary wage. And the pay chest took my Tuesday's worth of the four-day draw out on the second, which is a fortnight ago, and I have been strong as a bull for a fortnight, and the body's been putting the whole of it in now, and it isn't going to put it in a piece at a time."

Nobody said anything.

"So that's the answer to your question, clerk," said Ostrek, from the mud. "Ask me if I'd have signed it on the Wednesday with somebody telling me what it was going to cost. No. Not that week, not with a stop on it. Ask me if I'd have signed it on the Wednesday with nobody telling me anything, the way the form reads, which is what happened, because the form reads like a hire contract and there's a line at the top that says *you will find the terms reasonable* and I have never in my life not found terms reasonable."

Marek got down into the wet and knelt beside him.

"Mr Ostrek, I am going to write your name, the date, and the terms into the public petition book this afternoon, in the ordinary way, in my own hand, under the north road's name, where anybody may read it, including yourself. I am not going to ask you for anything and it will not help you. Will you let me?"

"You'll be throwing a stone at your own house."

"Yes," said Marek. "That is exactly what I am going to do and I have thought about it for two days and I am going to do it anyway, and I would be obliged if you did not tell anybody I asked, because I am not brave, I am just tired of writing things that go in a drawer."

The petition book is a bound volume on a lectern in the copy room by the intake end, and it is the one piece of furniture in the Tally Halls that anybody at all may write in, and every clerk knows that it is read by nobody and that it is a legal record of the fact of a complaint having been made and is therefore a document that can be produced in an appeal. It is not evidence of anything except that the complaint was made. In four years Marek had written in it four times. He wrote a fifth.

He wrote it slowly, in his own hand, with the date and the hour and the terms as Ostrek had stated them at the foot of the page, and he wrote *North road, above Tallow* as the place, because a petition wants a place, and then he read it back to the man across the lectern, who was a young man in his second month, and who read it and looked up.

"That's a good one," the young man said.

"It is an accurate one."

"It's a long one." He looked at it again. "Most of them are one line. There's a woman in here from the Underbank who's written *is my son written down* and that's all she's written, eleven days running."

"Then somebody should answer that one soon," Marek said, and shut the book, and wrote in his own pocket book the number of the page, because a thing filed is a thing that can be asked for later, and he had not filed anything in six days and he was going to have to start.

He did not tell Tomas. That was the second thing he did on the eighteenth that he would have to carry, and it was the smaller one, and it weighed more.

He came down off the hill at dusk with his coat soaked and went along Sill Road to the rooms, and Lio was on the step with a slate, and she went into the front room and got her coat and came out and walked beside him as far as the corner, which was as far as she ever walked with him.

"You've got another one," she said.

Marek stopped.

"It's got four in it," said Lio, "and it's the same four as the other one, and it's lower down, and you never told me and you said last Thursday in a room that you would tell me and then you did not tell me."

"You did not hear me say that."

"You said it in front of Tamsin and I was not asleep," said Lio, with enormous accuracy, "and then you did not tell me, and I have thought about it for two days and I have decided it is because there is no part of it that is a story you could tell me. So you are going to tell me the part that is a fact and leave the rest, and I am going to say thank you, and then you are going to do it and be no use at all."

He looked down the road at nothing at all for about four seconds, and then he heard himself, with the flat clarity of a man who has watched himself do a thing and has no time left to stop it, produce a technically accurate answer that omitted the entire question.

"It's a mark the tower puts on people who hold things up," he said, "which is what I did, and it's not a wound and it doesn't hurt and I have to write down the date of it, which is the fourteenth, and that's all."

"What does it say?"

Lio waited for it with her whole face.

"That I've been busy," Marek said.

Inside the rooms his mother was doing a seam under a lamp with a bead in her mouth, and she took it out, and she looked at his forearm where he had pushed the sleeve back to reach for the door handle, and she said absolutely nothing about it.

"You've come in wet," said Tamsin Vale.

"He's refused the schedule."

"I heard." She put the bead back in her mouth and then took it out again. "He'll be right about the witness."

"Which witness."

"The one that isn't us," said Tamsin Vale. "That's the one he'll not have." She picked the needle up. "And Marek. You'll go and you'll be late, and you will be late in a way that she'll notice, and you will say to her *the account is overdrawn and I have made it visible*, and she will say *thank you*, and you will both of you be pleased with each other, and you will not once have said out loud that there is a name in this building that ought to be in that conversation."

His mother sewed a seam four inches long without looking up.

"I am not asking," she said. "I am telling you what I have noticed, and I have noticed it since the fifteenth, and I am going to bed."
