# Chapter 28 — The Sentence He Would Not Say

They re-read it at the eighth bell on the twenty-ninth, in a room, with the party present, and Renata Vole read it herself, which was the whole of what Ilya Senn had asked for and it took her nine years to be able to do.

"The ward channel was supplied by the party without authority between the second and the twenty-fourth. The make-good water has been carried to the district account and is now the district's charge. No further action arises."

"There is a man reading it," said Ilya Senn. "Thank you."

"I am going to say one thing in front of the party, so that it is not said to you behind your back at some point in the next month, which is how this office usually does things and which I intend to stop doing this year." Vole put the paper down. "If you do not accept, this account is not closed. An open account is read at the year end against whoever is standing in the room, and a year-end reading is a finding, and a finding can be made against a person as well as a plant. I have four of those this year and one of them took a hand. So the terms are not only a tidy sentence. They are the difference between an irregularity on a valve and a finding against the man who opened it."

"And if I do accept it, what does it cost the ward."

"The charge is entered, and the district account carries two hundred and some hundreds of pints a night for twelve days at the ordinary rate, and the district account is the one the ward's school and the ward's water come out of, and the ward's levy is drawn out of it as well, and you do not need me to say the rest."

"And if I do not."

"Then the charge is not entered, the account is open, the ward's bill of two hundred and seventy-nine hundreds and forty pints of excess stands with four households payable this season, and the two hundred is nobody's charge until somebody in this city decides to make it one."

Nobody in the gallery said anything, and it was Aldis Kern, at the end of the table, as a person, who said it.

"Then the ward pays either way," said Aldis Kern, at the end of the table, as a person. "I would like the room to have that in the same sentence as the rest of it, because I have been telling four hundred people for three days that this office would treat the ward decently, and it will not, and I would rather say it here than have it said in a yard."

"Thank you, Captain," said Renata Vole.

Ilya Senn stood up and did not touch the paper.

"No," he said. "And I want to be careful about what I am refusing, because the room has been careful with me and I am not going to be careless in return.

"The third part of that sentence is false. Not dishonestly. False. There has never been no further action arising out of the fourteenth of the rains. There is a storehouse under nine feet of water that the water office holds no record of, because condemnation of ward property is not its business. There is a boy of thirteen with a shin in two pieces. There is a sheet of forty items in forty hands nailed to a board in a yard that no proceeding in this building can admit. There are a hundred and forty people who moved water for twelve days and whose names were never taken by anybody, including me. If I put my name to a sentence saying no further action arises, then that sentence becomes the end of the list. Not the end of the water. The end of the list. I have watched a plant do that to a man twice and I am not doing it to myself with my own hand on a Tuesday morning.

"And the first part of the sentence is true, and the second part is true, and I have signed worse than both and would sign them again this morning. If you want the fact, it is there. I held the valve from the second to the twenty-fourth against an order of the seventh, and I have four letters from a Crown Terrace water-clerk in this coat, and you may have them, and one of them is the reason I knew you would send an inspector and not a clerk."

He did not put the letters on the table. He put his hand flat on the coat instead and left it there.

"What I will not do is be the villain of somebody else's account. The ward's captain will tell this room what the ward took and what it paid and what it lost, and it will be in his mouth and not mine, and if I get in front of him I will have taken from a man the one thing he has spent two days getting ready to say, and a ward cannot say it for itself because a ward is not a person. I have had twenty-seven days on a platform to think about the fact that in this city four hundred people who were saved cannot say so in any room in this building unless one man stands up and says it for them, and that man is him and not me."

"Your offer was left on the table yesterday," said the hearing officer.

"A yard and a half a year, twenty-two years, and this hand. I know what it is worth and the room knows what it is worth, and I am leaving it there because a man who has held a valve open for twenty-seven days ought to be the first name on the account. Put it in the file as an offer made and not accepted. Do not put it in as a sacrifice, because it is not one, and I will not have it read in a year as a man who gave something."

The officer wrote it. The account was not closed, and the terms lapsed at the fourth bell of the fourth of next month.

And then Renata Vole did a thing Marek Vale did not understand for about ten seconds: she asked the clerk of the day to read the ward's part into the record again, the paragraph, the four hundred and forty, the hundred and forty, the *not lawfully delivered*, and she asked him to read it aloud in the room, and he did, and when he came to the sentence that named the ward's captain he stopped, because the sentence named him and he was not going to read his own name out loud in a room where Ilya Senn was standing.

"Go on," said Ilya Senn.

He went on.

---

The account had not closed, and the ward had been told so at the second bell, and it had been argued about in doorways and at the ends of the pails all afternoon by people who had been arguing about it in doorways since the sixth, and at the last of the light the yard filled, and Aldis Kern stood on the tank steps with two hundred and eighty people under him and a yard roll in his hand that had four lines on it and a great deal of space left over.

Marek got there late and stood at the back, because the yard had been his on the fifteenth and it was not his any more, and because there was nothing in this yard that a clerk could contribute and he had learned that in four days at the cost of one refusal from a man with forty items on a sheet.

"This is the sentence the ward has agreed to," said Kern. "I am going to read it and then I am going to stop talking, and then you may do as you like in the yard, and nothing that any of you says in the next hour is going into any room, because I have tried and I cannot make it go, and because a ward cannot file."

He read the yard roll.

*The Tallow Ward will not name the man who held the valve. The ward's water came down a channel above the ward meter and it was not lawfully the ward's, and the ward will pay for it at the ordinary wage, and the ward has offered to pay in the hearing and the offer stands whether the account is closed or not.*

*The ward does not forgive anybody and the ward does not blame anybody and neither of those is a decision, it is a fact about a yard.*

*The ward will keep its own roll of that water, dated, and it will keep it in the yard.*

*This is the whole of it and it was taken in about a quarter of an hour and I am not going to improve it.*

He got down off the steps and stood at the bottom with his arms folded, and the yard took about four seconds, and then Nadja Ostrow was on her feet.

"He has fed us for twenty-two days," she said, "and I have stood at that gate every night of it with a ladle in my hand and two hundred children behind me, and I am not going to stand in a yard and hear a roll read out that says the ward will pay for it. He is not a person to me, he is a valve with a man on it, and the ward is not going to pretend otherwise, and if the Trust wants to hang it on him then God help them, because he will not be able to say no."

"He is not a person to you because he has to be a valve," said Neris Kade. "That is the whole difficulty and it has been the whole difficulty since the sixth. Mrs Ostrow, if the ward says that valve saved us, then the ward has received stolen water, and a ward that has received stolen water is a ward that has to give it back, and there is no arrangement of this building in which that is not true. If the ward says the water was not lawfully ours, then he is a man who broke a sealed order to keep three hundred people alive and the ward has decided to help him pay for it. You cannot have the valve and the innocence. That is not a moral fact. It is a shape."

Milan Vray stood up in the second row and did not raise his voice and got the whole yard anyway.

"I carried pails," he said. "Twelve days. I have a boy at my table who is thirteen and who has a leg and who has never once asked me why we had water in the fourteenth of the rains, and he is not going to ask, because he does not know there was a question. And I will tell this yard one thing and then I will sit down. I do not want it called a gift. A gift is a thing a man gives a man. That water came out of a channel above our meter and our four counters have said so for three weeks and nobody in this city has read four private books kept by yard men. If we call it a gift we are saying it was ours, and it was not, and my tar is at the bottom of that cellar and no man in a good coat has asked me about my tar."

"So we pay," said Kern from the bottom of the steps.

"We pay for it," said Vray, "and we do not say thank you for it, and those are two separate sentences and I would like them kept apart, because the moment they are one sentence the ward has been given something, and if the ward has been given something then somebody gave it, and a man who gives things away does not get a sealed order broken in his coat by a water-clerk with four letters in it."

Nobody spoke for a while, and the river went past the yard, and the yard worked it out in the way yards work things out, which is slowly and with more than one person talking.

Marek Vale heard all of it and did not once think of writing a column.

The roll went onto the board before the lamps were lit. Four lines, no more, and under them a table with three columns and eleven dates, and in the first column the date, and in the second the number the four counters took, and in the third the number of people who had carried water, which was written as a figure of a hundred and forty and had been counted three separate times in nine days by three separate people and had never once been taken down by anybody. The bottom of the table was blank, because the ward had not decided when it stopped, and it was going to have to decide that, and Marek thought about that in a way that he could not afterwards explain to anybody.

"Examiner," said Vray, to Neris Kade. "You will keep it. It should be you."

"No," said Neris Kade.

"You made the sheets. You put ninety-one bodies in a Trust file on Tuesday and you stopped keeping them in your own hand, and that is the right thing to have done, and I am asking you to do it again."

"No, and I will tell you why, and then I will go and stand in the road." She did not raise her voice either. "The day an examiner keeps a roll is the day a district's roll becomes a return. They will not use the word, and they will not have to; there is a box somewhere in that building and the box has a name for a thing that was kept by a person and not by an office, and I have watched it eat a village in the Red Uplands over four years, and the village thought it was being careful. You keep it. All of you. Two of you on a night and one of you off, and the yard sees the sheet, and the yard is the thing that makes it true, and if the yard ever stops seeing it then it was never true and nobody can prove it and that is the correct arrangement."

Vray looked at her for a second, and then at Marek, who was standing at the back with his hands in his coat pockets and had not been asked for anything.

"Clerk," said Vray. "Not you either. I know what that face is. You have got a book in that coat with four things in it that you cannot show anybody, and I have been in this ward since the second night of the rains, and I would rather have this roll in a yard than in a book, and I would rather have it in a yard than in a Trust drawer, and if it ever comes into a drawer I will know who put it there and so will you."

---

Ilya Senn was on the platform of the Third Sluice at the ninth bell of the twenty-ninth with the water down in the channel and his back against the wheel housing, doing nothing, at ease, for the first time in four weeks, and the lamp was hooked to the rail and he was not doing anything at all.

That was how Marek knew.

"You closed it."

"Closed it at the third bell, on the hearing officer's instruction, and the intake is running and the head is above the bank, and the ward is on the town main off the north gardens' branch for the first time since the second of the rains, and they will find that out in a week and will not be pleased, and I do not care." Ilya Senn did not get up. "Do you know what the first thing was, in that room, at the ninth bell? A clerk would not read a sentence out loud because my name was in it. I have been a man in that building for twenty-two years. Nobody has ever declined to read a line on my account before."

"The ward's roll went on the board tonight," said Marek. "The ward will not name you. The ward will pay for the water, and the offer stands whether the account closes or not, and they argued for two hours about whether the ward was allowed to say thank you, and it is not, and they have decided to pay and not to thank, and Vray kept them to that."

"Ah," said Ilya Senn.

"You do not want that."

"I have been on this platform since the second of the rains telling the truth to about ninety people a day, in a raincoat, with a lamp, for twenty-seven days, and I have never once been asked what I would prefer." He got up then, and put his hand on the wheel the way he had every day for four weeks, except that it was shut now. "I would have preferred the ward to say nothing. I wanted them fed and I wanted them grateful and I never wanted to be thanked, because a ward that has been given something by a man is a ward that has been given something, and the giving puts him in the frame, and I have been in the frame since the second and I know exactly how much room there is in it.

"And now they have written down that they will pay for it, and the ward's bill goes up by two hundred a night for twelve days, and their four households at the bottom of it, and in six weeks somebody in that building is going to look at a rising bill and a rising river and a closed valve and work out in about nine seconds that the sensible thing would have been to shut it. And I will not be able to say no. And it will not be a lie when they say it, and I will not be able to argue, and the ward's own roll will be the paper that says it."

Marek stood on the bank in the dark and found that he had come up to say something and that the something had been taken out of the room by the man on the platform, in four sentences, with no complaint in it at all.

"You did not manufacture this," he said.

"No. I made a valve the wrong way on the second of the rains and I have been making it the wrong way every hour since, and a Warden's competence taught me a long time ago that I should make the terrible decision before anybody else can get to it, and I have never once been wrong about the water, and I have been wrong about everything else on that platform. Put it in whatever you are going to say about me that I never wanted the frame. I only ever wanted the valve."

He turned the wheel a quarter turn out of pure habit, and it did nothing, because a shut valve has nothing to do with a man at the last of the light.

"Go down and find out what the ward is doing at the Gate, and tell them from me that the town main is thinner than they think and they will be short on the Ropewalk standpipe by the end of the week, and that they should start boiling on a ration again on Wednesday and not wait to be asked." He looked at him. "And then you can tell them the man who fed them says so."

Marek went down the bank in the dark. At the bottom he stopped and looked back up at a man standing in a lamp's worth of light beside a closed valve with twenty-seven days on him and no relief and no thanks, and understood the thing he had been carrying in a book he could not show anybody since the sixteenth of the rains, which was that a man who takes the terrible decision first is the safest man in the building for exactly as long as nobody has been hurt by it.

He went down to the Gate and told the ward what the Warden had said about the Ropewalk standpipe, and nobody thanked him for it, and that was correct, and he had a form in one pocket with a date on it that had lapsed on the twenty-fourth and a form in the other from the twenty-second, and he wrote a word of it down nowhere.
