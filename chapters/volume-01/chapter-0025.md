# Chapter 25 — Four Dead People in Nine Minutes

Hesper Rann's room on the twenty-sixth looked like a room in which a man had been sitting for four days, and it was a Saturday, and there was no one outside the door, which Marek noticed and then wished he had not noticed.

Hesper had the Tallow entry open on the desk and a reading book beside it with a wedge in it, and he had been up since the fourth bell and had not gone home, and he said so before he was asked, in the tone of a man who has learned that stating a fact about himself first saves him the trouble of being asked for it.

"Sit down. Not the chair with the leg, the leg has been worse since the twenty-second." He pushed a cup across, which was cold. "How's the arm."

"It is closed."

"That is not what I asked and you have known that for three weeks." He looked at the sleeve. "You are still hiding it under the coat when you come in. It has been closed for eleven days and you are twenty-eight and it will not be the arm that finishes you. It is going to be a desk a year covered in your own handwriting, and there is a place in the copy room where you put the whole of it down every evening, and I have walked past that desk four times a week for twenty-two years, and the worst thing I ever saw on it in all that time was a man doing it honestly."

Hesper put his hand out flat on the desk, palm up, the way he had done it in a thousand rooms in this building, and said nothing, and waited.

Marek pushed the sleeve back four inches.

Hesper looked for a long time. He did not touch it and he did not ask.

"That is not the cut," he said. "That is something else, and I have known there was a second one since the twenty-second, and I have watched you carry it under a coat for eleven days without once saying a word about it to a person, including me. And I want you to hear why I have never asked. A man who hides a mark has decided what it is. A man who has decided what it does not need me to guess at it."

Marek put the reading book on the table between them, open, with the wedge in the second of the rains.

"I want to ask you four questions," he said. "I want to be in the room for all four and I am not going to leave early."

"Then ask them, and take the coat off if you are going to stay, because I am forty-six and my knees are a matter of public record, and I have a bad shoulder and that door is draughty and I am not going to be defeated by a window."

---

"You did not cause the over-read," said Marek. "That is settled. There is a take-off above the ward meter with no meter on it and two hundred hundreds of pints a night going up into a garden, and it has been going up there for eleven years, and it went up faster from the second of the rains for a reason that has nothing to do with you. The screen was foul and the ward paid for that in pails and the ward did not know it was paying. The cause line on the fifteenth is not true."

"No," said Hesper Rann. "It is available."

"That is not a distinction anybody outside this room can hear."

"It is the only distinction there is, and I have been making it for twenty-two years." He turned the entry round. "The cause line is a sentence about where a number came from, in a box on a form, entered in a desk's book, and it is read by whoever opens that book. It does not have to be true. It has to be capable of being acted on. That is all a cause line is for and you have known it since the twenty-first and I am not going to insult you by pretending otherwise."

"Then what is true," said Marek, "is that the ward was short and the river was up and the tank on the Gate was dry, and that the lie did not make any of that worse, and that it made the ward's bill look empty. I have had that for two weeks. What I cannot get past is the notice."

He put his finger on the plate of the entry, at the first line, where a ward entry names the instrument it is answering.

"The Tallow Gate," he said. "Thirty days' notice to the parties, and it expired on the twelfth, and the gate was to be shut on the fourteenth, and if the settlement had not been sealed before the twelfth there was no settlement and there was no gate, and there is the Fuel basin and the low kitchens under eleven feet of the Silt by the morning of the fifteenth. You have told me that twice. I have written it down twice. I cannot find the place where it stops being true, and I have looked for it every night since the twentieth, and this is my third time asking and you are going to answer me as if you have never been asked."

Hesper Rann put both hands flat on the desk.

"Nine minutes," he said. "On the ninth of the rains, at about the fourth bell of the afternoon, I walked from the Sill Row steps to the Fuel basin. I found four people in nine minutes. A carter's boy of fifteen in the run-off at the Tanner's steps, who had gone into the water to get a rope. A woman of eighty-one in a ground-floor room at the Fuel basin with the door open and nothing done to her, which means she had been in there two days. A ferryman of thirty-eight who had gone back over the side for a horse that was already dead. And a tanner's hand of nineteen in the Sill Road drain, which I am told he went down to clear, and which I am not going to argue with anybody about, because he was nineteen and it was a drain and there was four feet of water in it and he cleared it."

He said it in the flat voice of a man reading a list he has read before, and then he said:

"Every one of them was dead before I wrote a word. I want that in the order I have put it in. I did not cause them and I did not prevent them and I have not been sorry for one of them in a way that anybody in this building can put in a file, because I did not know them, and I have found out since that two of them had families at the Tanner's and one of them had a mother in Ropewalk and I have gone and stood in the lane of each of those three, because a man who has read a list of names in a room all afternoon goes and stands in the lanes."

Marek looked at the plate of the entry and did not say anything.

"Now your turn," said Hesper. "You have had that since the sixth, and since the nineteenth, and in a yard on the fifteenth in front of four hundred people, and it is a good argument and it is the truth. And here is what you have not done, and I am going to do it for you, because I have four days and no hearing until the fourth of next month and I have nothing else to do.

"Ask the other question. Ask me what would have happened if the notice had run. You have stood at that plate twice now and read it as a date and not as an answer. Go and stand there a third time and work out what the ward's ground looks like at the sixteenth, and then come back and tell me, and I will write it down, and you can put it in your own book with a date on it. Do not do it here, because you will do it badly in here, because I will interrupt you."

Marek stood up, and then sat down again.

"No," he said. "I am not going to do that. I have looked for three weeks and I am not going to go and look for it with a man who has told me he wants me to find it. What I am going to do is ask you the four dead people question straight, because you have made it into a list and I have made it into a list and neither of us has said it out loud in a sentence yet. On the ninth of the rains, at the fourth bell of the afternoon, you found four dead people. On the ninth of the rains, about two bells later, you took an unassigned file off a bench and wrote a false recital in it. Which of those two facts is the reason the ward is not under water."

Hesper Rann looked at him for a long time.

"The second one is the reason the ward is not under water and the first one is the reason I wrote the recital," he said. "That is the whole answer and I have given you the whole answer, and I would like it noticed that the answer is not a defence of anything. It is an explanation of two men doing arithmetic in the same hour, one of them with a list in his hand and one of them with a notice period. If you want me to be sorry, I am not. I have gone through it four ways since the ninth and there is no version in which I do not write it, and if you want to know what I would have done differently I will tell you that too, since you are going to ask. I would have written it in a better hand."

---

"Third question," said Marek, and his voice was not very good. "Ilya Senn has held the Third Sluice open since the second day of the rains against a sealed Crown Terrace order of the seventh. He has four letters from a Crown Terrace water-clerk in his coat that anybody can take out of his coat. The gate that is on the plate at the head of your entry is a different valve at a different end of the ward, and the supply of the low kitchens is coming down a channel the Trust owns, and a hundred and forty people have carried pails from the second night onward. So the ward was saved twice on the fourteenth and only one of the two times was a gate, and you and I have spent three weeks in this building treating the gate as the one that happened. Did you know about the sluice on the ninth."

"I knew there was water in the low kitchens on the ninth," said Hesper. "I did not know where it came from, and I have never asked, and I am not going to now, and I would like you to hear the reason and then never ask me for it again, because the reason is the only thing in this building I have been protecting in the last fortnight and it is not the ward and it is not you and it is not me."

"Say it."

"The Trust has no instrument for a valve." Hesper put the reading book aside. "There is no form on which a Warden can be asked to explain an open sluice, because a sluice is not a loan and there is no pledge, no scope, no witness and no settlement, and if it is not a loan then it is not a subject this office has standing to inquire into. The moment somebody writes down that valve as a thing, it becomes a maintenance irregularity, and an irregularity has an account, and the account is the ward's, and the ward's water becomes water the ward was given by a man who had no right to give it. And then the ward drank water it had no right to drink, and the ward is a beneficiary, and a beneficiary pays."

Marek sat very still.

"So you have been protecting him."

"I have been protecting the ward. There is a difference and it is not a small one, and it is the only difference in this business that I am still able to afford." Hesper's voice did not change at all. "And I will say the other half, since we are being honest on a Saturday and it is costing me. If the valve is never written down, then in six weeks somebody in that building will look at this entry and see a ward that drank four hundred and forty against a lawful hundred and sixty, and there is nothing whatever on the paper to stop them seeing a thief, and I do not have a better answer for the ward than an incomplete file."

---

"Fourth question," said Marek. "You know what I am going to ask, so I will ask it anyway, and you will answer it as a Trust officer and not as a man, because I have not got the right to the other thing and I know that."

"You have got the right to the other thing and you are frightened of having it, which is a clerk's disease, and ask it anyway."

"On the twenty-second the Deputy Steward offered me a form. Twenty-nine words. It says the Tallow file was never assigned to me as clerk of the day. The assignment is a letter in your hand in the desk's book and it says *V.* and you wrote it at the sixth bell of the ninth when you gave me the file, and there are four people in this hall who will swear to it, and the woman who made the offer told me so herself, in her own office, with a pen in her hand. I would like you to say, in this room, that the offer is improper, and I would like it in writing, and I have been three days working out how to ask for it in a way you could answer."

Hesper Rann did not move for a moment. Then he took his coat off the back of the chair, and put it on, and said:

"No."

"You have asked me for a lie every day since the fourteenth."

"Yes."

"And you are going to ask me for another one in about nine days, when a door is ready for it, and I know that, and you know that I know it, and we have been very civilized about it."

"I am not going to refuse you," said Hesper. "I want to be exact about what I am doing, because you have earned exactness and I have not given anybody exactness in twenty-two years. I cannot. I am a Trust officer and the Deputy Steward is above me and if I put a word of objection on that form it goes to the intake floor and Sedge signs it in the dark in about a week, and Sedge is a better man than I am and he will do it, and you will be suspended in a month for a statement that has somebody else's handwriting on it. That is not me protecting the office. That is me telling you the price of asking me, so that you can decide what to do with it."

He stopped at the door with his hand on the frame, and for once in his working life said the thing he actually meant.

"Vale. Your form lapsed on the twenty-fourth. I am not re-dating it, because re-dating it is asking you a second time inside a fortnight, and a man who does that is running a campaign, and I have never run a campaign in my life and I am not starting at forty-six." He went out into the corridor and stopped again. "Go and read the plate. Not for me. I have had that number since the ninth, before you did. Read it because you will not be able to carry anything else in your head until you have, and then come back and be as angry with me as you like, and I will not put your name in anything."

---

Marek went down the hill in the rain at the seventh bell and walked out to the Tallow Gate, which is a stone headwall with two iron leaves and a plate on the keystone, and the plate is not a notice and it has not changed since before he was born.

It says what the gate is and when it is shut and how much notice the parties are entitled to, and it says the notice is thirty days, and the last line of the plate gives the date on which the current season's notice expires, and the date is the twelfth of the rains.

That is all it says. There is no reserve on it and no cause and no clerk's name. It is a plate on a wall with a date on it, and four hundred people had walked past it nine times a day for the whole of the flood season and not one of them had read it, and a man who was a clerk had read it perhaps twice in four years, and it had been in the file since the ninth in the first line of the entry, in the fair fast hand, and he had put his own certificate of posting under a lie that was leaning on a blank line in an old book when the plate on the wall at the bottom of the hill had said the whole thing out loud in a date.

He stood in the wet with his hand on the iron and read it twice, and it was true, and there was nothing whatever to be said about it, and it had been true the whole time, and that was the thing he went upstairs with, and it was the thing he would carry into every room for the next four weeks: that the man he had spent thirteen days trying to destroy had been right about one enormous thing, and had written the rest of it down anyway, in a fair hand, in the rain, with four dead people in his pocket, and had been right about the plate and had been right about the pails and had been right about the notice, and had put a boy's pencil under it, and had not cared in the slightest that he was right, because being right had never been what he was after.

He got back to Bell Stair at the ninth bell and let himself into the copy room and the lamps were going along the north wall, and the desk was where he had left it with his own book open on it, face down, where anybody in the building could have come and looked, and he turned it over and shut it and put it in his coat, and thought about the four lines of the notice on the posting board, and the four dead people, and the sixty pints of difference between a ward's roll and a wall book, and the fact that the Trust wanted one villain and that he now had two men in his coat and neither of them would fit in a box with a cause at the top of it.
