# Chapter 18 — Four Names and a School Year

He was at the second bell on the nineteenth, in the schoolroom, standing against the stove wall, because he had told a laundress's daughter on the fifteenth that he would be there and had spent the whole of the eighteenth working out how to say it.

Jitka Kelb was in the second row and she did not look at him, and about forty other children did, and Pella Rusk came in behind him with a slate under her arm and shut the door.

"Vale." She had not been introduced. "Sit down. You are stood against my stove and it is the only warm place in the room and you are making it draughty for everybody."

"Sera Holt sent me."

"Sera Holt has been in my schoolroom four times in nine years and twice it was about a boy who had fainted." Rusk put the slate on the desk. "You are here because you said a date in front of a child, and the child has repeated the date to me twice, and a date from a clerk is worth more than a date from anybody else in this building. So: what do you want, and be quick, because I have ninety-one children and a warden coming at the fourth bell about the term."

"Your term is not closed."

"My term is suspended from the twenty-fourth, which is a different thing and a worse one, and it is on a sheet of paper that two young men in good coats put on my step on Tuesday." She turned the slate round. It was the district account. "Look at the over-draw and then tell me what you'd have told me on the fifteenth."

It was a page of ordinary figures in a district account: lamp oil on the Basin road, the ward's yard repairs, the school, the water-yard candles, a line for the standpipe at Ropewalk, and at the foot, in a heavier hand, the collection of the ward levy's unpaid days at the ordinary wage.

Two hundred and forty per cent overdrawn. Not a projection. A shade over three times what the account may lawfully draw, on figures that came out of the water office on the fifteenth and were struck in a hand on the eighteenth, in the middle of the flood, with the reason written in the margin as *obligation in course of settlement*.

"That is the whole of the levy on one line," Marek said.

"That is the whole of the levy on one line and it is the only line anybody drew anything on, because the ward's levy is a levy in days, and days get drawn at the ordinary wage, and the ordinary wage comes out of the district account, and the district account is this." Rusk put her finger on the school line. "And the account is two hundred and forty per cent over, and the account has to be balanced by the end of the month, and the office does not come here and ask me what I can do without. It takes the cheapest line in the account. And then the next cheapest. And there are four lines, Mr Vale, and the school is the cheapest of the four, and the word for what has happened to me is *saving*."

"You are not a saving. You are a line."

"I am the line that balances the account," said Pella Rusk. "That is exactly what a saving is, and I have been arguing about the word for nine years, and the word is not the thing. The thing is that the ward's bill is being collected out of my children by removing them from the building, and it is done by arithmetic, and no one in this story has to be wicked, and I have never once been able to get at the person who did it, because there isn't one. There is a form and a schedule of lines and a rule that says take them in this order, and the rule is in the water office, and I have written to Rainmarket four times, and Rainmarket sends me a letter about the district's ability to meet its obligations."

The schoolroom door opened and Jitka Kelb came in late, wet, and sat down in the second row without looking at the wall, and Pella Rusk looked at Marek.

"Four names," she said. "You have come about four names. Sit down and I will read you the list, and then you may do whatever clerks do, and I have nine years of clerks and I will tell you the difference in about a minute."

"Read them."

"Wren Ostrow, nine. Tibb Vray, eight. Salle Prent, seven. Iven Marsh, ten." She said them the way she said everything, without weight, and then she said: "Two of them are off for arrears that are two years old and are about half a farthing a day. Two of them are off because the seat went, which is a line in an account and a child is a line in an account. I can give you the arithmetic in about a minute. I have given it to four Trust men and one Chamber clerk in nine years and I have never been able to give it to anybody in a room where it mattered, and you are in a room where it might matter, so do it quickly."

Marek did it in about a minute, and he did it well, because it was the only thing in nine days he was qualified to do.

"Your school is paid out of the district account. That account is overdrawn by two hundred and forty per cent, which is a shade over three times the lawful draw. The levy is drawn out of the same account at the ordinary wage, in days, and the days are not paid. So the office balances the account by cutting lines, in an order that is written down somewhere in the water office's standing practice, and the school is the cheapest line in a district account, because a school year is a fixed sum and a yard repair is a variable one. That is the entire mechanism. There is nothing else in it. Nobody has done anything to those four children. Nobody has come to this room. Nobody has taken an hour off anybody's day. The levy has taken a school year out of this account and a school year comes off in the only way a school year comes off, which is that a place is not paid for and therefore is not there."

"That is right," said Pella Rusk. "Now say the next part, which is the part you have not said and which is the only part I care about."

"The next part is that it is not a person," Marek said, "and that is worse, and you are right that it is worse, and I would not have believed that eleven days ago."

Rusk looked at him for a while.

"Right," she said. "You are the first one."

"What I have got," Marek said, "is a document. On the fifteenth, in the Trust's third floor, under Renata Vole's hand and seal, both copies of your roll were entered in the record of the proceeding as the same document, and the difference between them — a page the file holds and the ward does not — was noted at the foot of the record as a difference, and was not adjudicated. It is in the file. It is permanent. And a district account cannot be closed over a standing difference in a Trust record, because the difference is the account's own subject, and if somebody closes this account this month they will be closing it over a document that says the ward was assessed on a page it never got, and that is a thing that can be asked about in a room with a door in it."

"For how long?"

"A week. Maybe less. A difference on a record holds a closure for as long as somebody keeps asking about it, and that is not a victory, it is a week."

"That is a week," Rusk agreed. "A week is four of the school weeks. It is not the term and it is not the year. But it is four weeks that I can use to write to every mother in this ward, and I will."

And that was the whole of what Marek could do in that room on the nineteenth of the rains, and it was more than anybody had done for her in nine years, and it was obtained for a reason he was not going to explain to her.

The complaint board at the Threshold is a sheet of deal under a shelter at the left-hand end of the intake front, and any person in Morrowfen may nail a paper to it and any clerk in the hall must carry it to the officer of the day before the sixth bell, and it is read, in the sense that it is carried.

Pella Rusk nailed up two sheets on the afternoon of the nineteenth. The first was the district account and its over-draw and the order the lines were cut in. The second was four names and four dates and, at the foot, in a schoolmistress's hand, the sentence: *No child at this school has been asked to do anything. Four places have been removed from an account.*

"You do not have to sign it," Marek said.

"I know. That is why I wrote it that way." She drove the last nail in with the heel of her hand. "If I had written *the ward's bill has taken my school year* it would be an accusation and an accusation can be answered. I have written four true sentences and a clerk's account, and there is nothing in it to answer, and it will be carried to the officer of the day and it will be in the file and nobody can take it off the board without their own name going on a record."

Neris Kade read it over her shoulder on her way in, and said nothing, and looked at Marek, and he had a fairly clear idea of what the look was for, and did not like it.

At the fourth bell, when the warden came about the term and had gone away again, and the room had emptied down to the two of them and a sweep, Pella Rusk squared her slate and said:

"Now. You have been in this room forty minutes and you have not asked me one question about the roll, and you have been in it forty minutes and you have said the word *surety* twice, and there is one surety column in the whole of the ward's file, and I have been arguing about that account since the ninth and I have read every page the ward has ever been given."

Marek did not move.

"Does page three have my name on it," said Pella Rusk.

He could have said yes. He could have said no. He had been carrying the answer since the fourth of the rains in a book in his coat, and it was eleven lines long and hers was one of them, and there was a bond's witness cell under it with nothing in it, and she had a daughter in the second row and a boy of twelve in the third, and she had taught in this room for nine years, and the ward was owed the truth by a clerk.

"Page three is a labour-pledge schedule," Marek said. "Eleven lines, four columns: household, days, surety, witness. It is cited in the entry. It was never disclosed to the parties the assessment was posted against, and yesterday afternoon a Deputy Settlement Steward set every one of its lines aside for the purposes of the proceeding because the ward was never served with it, and the ward's copy of the assessment runs one, two, four and five. It is in the file. Anything in a Trust file is a document that any person with standing may apply to read, and the ward has standing, and the ward is not a person, and that is a difficulty that people who are not lawyers are told is a technicality and that is the first thing in nine years that has made me want to be sick."

"That is not an answer," said Pella Rusk.

"No," Marek said. "It is not."

She looked at him across a schoolroom with ninety-one chairs in it and eleven of them stacked on the last row because the term is suspended, and something arrived on her face that was not anger and that he was not going to be able to answer for, and she picked up her slate.

"Then you will bring it to my room," she said, "or you will tell me you won't, and either one of those I can use."
