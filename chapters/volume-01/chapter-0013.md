# Chapter 13 — The Totals Are the Totals

The Trust's third floor is a long room with a table down one side and windows on the water, and it is built for sixty. Four hundred people came up the causeway in the rain, and Renata Vole had them stopped at the door, and the room's own regulations were invoked in a voice that had run out of patience some time before the day began.

"Seventy in the chamber," said the hearing officer. "The rest in the corridor and the north stair landing. The ward's captain, the party, the ward's witness, and whoever the Rainmarket appointment has brought."

"She has brought a list," said Renata Vole.

"She has brought a list, ma'am."

"Then the list is a document and the list stays in the corridor, and if that examiner wishes to speak she may stand at the end of the table and say what she came to say and be quick about it, and that is not a right, it is a courtesy, and she should know which one she is being given."

Vole was fifty-seven and had been a Deputy Settlement Steward for nine years and she came into a room like weather coming into a yard. She did not look at four hundred people. She looked at the file, and then she looked at the clock on the table, and Marek, who would have bet his place in the hall that she had arranged the morning around that clock, did not tell the ward so.

The room was called at the eighth bell. The parties were seated at the ninth, because the Deputy Steward's office had a Crown Terrace irrigation account in front of it that morning, and because a man in a wet coat can be made to stand in a cold corridor for an hour and a half without a word of it being said about him, and nobody in the tower had to be cruel, because cruelty takes an effort and this took none.

Marek had the ward's copy in his left hand. It was in a clerk's binding with four pages in it and the roll, and it had been carried up four flights by a boy of twenty on the night of the fourteenth, and there was no page three in it, and there never had been.

On the table in the middle lay the file's copy: five pages, all of them, and a sixth document on top of them, and the sixth document had Kelb's name at the foot of it in pencil.

"Clerk of the day," said Vole. "Tallow Ward. Put them down together and let the ward look."

He put them down together. He did it with both hands, the way you lay a thing down in front of a witness, and he put the ward's copy down first and the file's copy on top of it, and for about four seconds the whole room was looking at two pieces of paper that were the same document and were not the same document, and the difference was a hole.

"There," said the hearing officer. "The ward's copy: pages one, two, four and five. The file: one, two, three, four, five. The ward has been served with a notice that cites a five-page assessment. The file contains a fifth page. Is there a dispute about the fact?"

"No dispute," said Vole.

"Then the page is a page, the ward has never had it, and it was never served. Clerk of the day, is it a term of the assessment?"

"It is cited at the foot of the entry's fourth clause, ma'am."

"Then it is a material term." Vole turned a page. "And the ward was posted against an assessment that did not disclose it to them. That is not a forgery, because a forgery is a false thing, and I have no finding that this page is false. That is a *disclosure failure*. Under the standing order on service, a schedule not disclosed to the parties at posting is a schedule the parties cannot rely on. The ward cannot take an obligation set out in a document they were never shown. Every one of the eleven lines in it is set aside for the purposes of this proceeding, and the ward's bill is read on the four pages the ward has, and I expect the clerk of the day to be grateful for that, because eleven unsigned sureties would have been very bad for this ward in a room with a door in it."

Neris Kade, standing at the end of the table with her hands behind her back, said, "It is bad for the ward, ma'am. It is simply bad for the ward later."

"You may have the room, examiner, when the clerk of the day has finished."

"I will wait."

"Where is the boy?" Somebody at the back of the seventy. "The one off the counter. Is he in this building?"

"He is at the clinic," said Vole, without looking up, "and a party is not obliged to put a man in a room because his name is convenient, and if anybody in this room has an account of what happened to his hand I would like it on the file in writing before this afternoon is out."

"Someone will have one, ma'am," said Neris Kade. "I have."

Marek had the page in front of him and it was the third time he had seen that page in his life, and the first time had been with the original under his hand, and the second had been from memory on a stair at four in the morning, and this one was Kelb's writing, and the hand was the levy office's, and the four words at the foot of it were not there because a hand-copy is not a copy until it carries the four words at the foot that say what it is a copy of, and the four words had not been finished, and the word that was there was *Copied*.

He set it down square with the file's copy and it lay there for the rest of the afternoon being a second document.

"Next," said Vole. "The ward's complaint is the water. Is there anything on the water?"

Aldis Kern stood up. He had his sheet of oiled paper and he did not open it.

"There is on the water, ma'am, and I'll say it in the order I wrote it." He was extremely formal and had clearly practised on the causeway. "Tallow Ward got its water. I will not stand here and tell this room the ward went short, because it did not, and any man in this room who says otherwise can come and stand at the Gate tank. The tank is full. The screen is holding. We had ninety-one people out of the low kitchens on dry beds for a day and a half, which is a Monday, and no yard went under, and I will take the ward's thanks for the fourteen nights be said out loud, and I do not know to whom this room would like me to give them, so I will give them to the record."

Marek shut his eyes for a second.

"The water did not come from a clerk," Kern said. "It came off a valve. The Third Sluice, held open by a Lantern Warden named Ilya Senn since the second day of the rains against a sealed Crown Terrace order dated the seventh. He has broken that order every hour since and kept the Crown Terrace water-clerk's four letters in his coat, and I have asked that this room hear it, because the moment the file is closed somebody is going to write that we drew water through a broken valve, and that is true, and it is not the whole of it, and the whole of it is that the intake at the head of that channel is Crown Terrace and if the valve shuts that intake fills and the low kitchens have nothing in them. I have a copy of the order in my coat and I have a copy of my captain's count, and my captain's count is that the water came off the basin by hand from the second night onward in the low kitchens, by a hundred and forty people, and nobody has put a name to that either."

Nobody in the room said a word for about six seconds.

"Next," said Vole pleasantly.

Then the officer read the cause.

He read it well. He read it the way it had been drafted, which is to say without emphasis, and the cause of the Tallow Ward excess was as follows: the excess arose from a failure of routine maintenance upon the junction screen and the bypass apparatus at the second junction of the ward trunk main, those works being the maintenance responsibility of the district's irrigation contractor upon Crown Terrace account 4-Crown, and the account and its condition being a matter entirely outside the ward's knowledge or control; the assessment having been raised against a measured delivery that the ward's own records did not support, the delivery figure having been in error, and the deficiency having been corrected on the thirteenth by works carried out at the water office's expense, the excess draw in the intervening period standing as an operator's failure for which no party in this room is responsible.

"Hask and Rundle," said the hearing officer, "irrigation works, account 4-Crown."

And Marek Vale, third-grade clerk of the day for Tallow Ward, understood the entire architecture of the thing for the first time in its finished form, sitting at a table in a cold room with a copy of the roll under his hand.

It was very good. It was better than the first one.

The first lie had been a reserve: a sum that did not exist, held against a line in an old book that nobody could read. It was clever and it was in the entry and it was signed by a supervisor.

This one was in the clerk's office, and it did not require a supervisor's signature, and it was not in the entry at all — it was a *cause*, and causes do not have to be true, they have to be available. There was a Crown Terrace irrigation account, and the screen and the bypass were on it, and Petross Oleksen had cleared the strainer and the reset bypass on the thirteenth with a Warden's crew and four yard men on a work order nobody had the right to give, and the water office had paid for it. There was a contractor. There was a maintenance responsibility. There was a date, and a work order, and an expense borne by the office that had been over-reading the ward for nine days.

And every word of it was true.

That was the part that took him by the throat. Hesper had not written that cause line. There was no false hand in it. There was no invented reserve in it. It was the truth about a stuck screen, told in such a way that the only person left on the table with a deficit was the Tallow Ward.

And it was load-bearing. The moment the Trust said *operator's failure, and it was corrected on the thirteenth,* the ward's over-draw became somebody else's bill and the ward's excess became a thing the ward had to pay, and the reason the ward had to pay it was that the true cause of its thirst was a Crown Terrace gardener's maintenance contract, and that meant Hesper's reserve was never reached. The lie had a fence in front of it now, and the fence was true, and you could not get at the fence without first proving that the ward had been in the wrong place on the ninth of the rains, which nobody in this room was going to try, because the ward had drunk the water.

"The ward's own records," said the hearing officer, "the yard counters, were not before the account. Is that right, Clerk of the day?"

"They were not. There are four sets of yard counters, kept by the yards' own clerks. They are not the account."

"They are evidence."

"They are four private books kept by men the water office does not pay."

"So they are not evidence. The court will take the account." The officer said it without unkindness. "Clerk of the day, I am going to give you the form of that, and I am going to give it to you exactly, because this is the point of the afternoon and I would like it understood by everyone in the corridor.

"A counter-signature does not verify a document. Nothing in this building has ever pretended it did. A third-grade counter-signature certifies two things and two only: that the document was before the desk, and that the clerk at the desk looked at it. That is the whole of its meaning and that is why we are so careful about who is permitted to do it. It is not an endorsement of the figures. It is a statement about a pair of eyes and a length of time at a counter. In this matter the totals on the ward settlement entry were added at the counter-desk and were countersigned at the counter-desk, and the ward's copy and the file's copy were both compared, and the counter-signature is good. It certifies that the paper was before the desk. It does not certify that the reserve recital in the fourth clause is money.

"And here is the sentence this room is going to need at some point in the next fortnight, so I will say it now while the officer who says such things is in the room. The chain follows the instrument, not the folder. That is the rule. A case is a bundle and a bundle is nothing. The tower reads one paper at a time and asks what is on this paper and who has put their name on it. In this file there are two signed papers. One is the ward settlement entry. The other is a one-page certificate of posting, countersigned by the clerk of the day on the eleventh. They are in the same folder, in the same clip, under the same copper, and they were applied in that order, and any person in this room who cannot say which of those two papers carries the defect has not read the file.

"The certificate of posting is not a term of the entry. It is a certificate that the entry was lawfully posted. The entry carries the recital. The certificate carries nothing but the clerk's hand and the hour. If this entry is ever found to be false-witnessed, the collection walks the entry, and the entry was countersigned at the counter-desk, and the clerk at the counter-desk is the living officer of this institution whose act the counter-signature was. The certificate of posting is not on that paper, and no amount of being in the same folder puts a man's name on a paper that does not carry it."

He looked up.

"Which is not a finding," he said. "That was procedure. Take it or leave it."

Marek took it, and put his hand flat on the table to stop it shaking, and it was the single most useful thing anybody had said to him in six days and he was not going to be able to use it for about a month.

"Marek Vale," said Vole.

"Ma'am."

"Your party is the ward's clerk of the day and your file is a suspended case with a defect in it that you have described to nobody. You have filed nothing. You have answered no question about the cause. You have stood at the end of a table for two hours as a procedure, and I want to know what you came here to do."

He thought about lying. It took him about a second and a half and it was the first time in six days that lying had seemed like a reasonable thing to do, and that fact went into him and stayed.

He asked for both copies to be entered as the same document.

"Explain."

"Both the ward's copy and the file's copy are the Tallow assessment, and the ward is not represented by a counsel and I am not a counsel, I am its clerk of the day. I ask that both copies be entered in the record of this proceeding as the same document, and that the difference between them — a page the file holds and the ward does not — be recorded as a difference between them, and not resolved."

Vole looked at him for slightly too long.

"That costs me nothing," she said, "and it is good practice, and there is no reason on earth why it should not be done."

"Then I ask that the ward's copy be marked as the copy served on the ward, with the page numbers as they are on it, one, two, four, five."

"Granted."

"Both copies in the bundle," said the hearing officer, "with the difference noted at the foot and not adjudicated. Noted at the foot and not adjudicated. Clerk, that is a page the ward never had sitting on a table in a Trust room, and it is going to be in the file for ever, and I would like you to understand that I have never known a clerk in four years to ask for less."

And then Renata Vole, who had been reading her own file for two hours, put her thumb on a line near the bottom of the fourth page and said:

"Deputy's certification of the Tallow Ward water settlement for the peak night, in the terms proposed by the levy office. The standing reserve as recited is an account of the water office and is honoured as certified. There is no signed delivery certification for the peak night and the Deputy Steward notes the fact; a ward's lawful requirement for a night is its standing schedule, and Tallow Ward's standing schedule is one hundred and sixty hundreds of pints. The balance drawn at the ordinary wage is a lawful settlement and stands."

"Say the number," said Neris Kade from the end of the table.

"I beg your pardon."

"Say the number the ward actually took, so that it is in the record of this room and not only in yours."

There was a silence.

"The junction account certified the delivery," said Vole, "at six hundred and forty hundreds."

"And the four yard counters," said the examiner, "show four hundred and forty. I have the yard clerks' books in a satchel and I will leave them with the officer and they are not the account and they are not evidence and I know that, and I am asking a Deputy Settlement Steward to say a number out loud in a room with a door in it."

She put the satchel on the table and the officer opened it there at the table in front of forty people, which no Trust officer had done in front of forty people in the whole of anybody's memory, and turned the four books face up, and did not take them away again.

And Renata Vole, who was fifty-seven and had been signing emergency settlements for nine years and who had watched half of Crown Terrace do exactly this last spring, said:

"Four hundred and forty."

The officer wrote it down. Four hundred and forty hundreds of pints in one night, in a ward of about four hundred people, whose lawful requirement for that night was one hundred and sixty.

"That is two and three-quarter times the standing schedule," said Vole, "and the excess is a lawful draw at the ordinary wage, and I am certifying the settlement as lawful, standing at the schedule, and I am not going to be asked a third time."

They got the ward out of the room at the fourth bell with the file closed and the water certified and nothing whatever done about the page, and it was in the corridor afterwards that Marek stood with his back against a wall and understood what he had done.

He had got a page the ward never had onto a permanent record of a Trust proceeding, in a building that kept everything, with a note at the foot saying the difference existed and had not been adjudicated.

And in exchange the record now said, in Renata Vole's hand and under her seal, that Tallow Ward's lawful water for the peak night was one hundred and sixty hundreds of pints, and that the ward had drunk four hundred and forty.

The ward had water, and would keep it, and from that afternoon forward, in every room in Morrowfen that was not that room, the levy would look like the bill of a ward that had drunk two and three-quarter times what it was allowed — and he had done that, with a small procedural request that cost the Trust nothing and was good practice, and it had cost the ward the only thing it had left to be angry about.
