# Chapter 19 — Reading a Hand

They began at the second bell on the twentieth at a trestle table under the eaves of the Gate yard, and for the first hour and a half they produced nothing whatever, and it was Neris Kade's opinion, expressed twice, that it was because of him.

"You have written sixty-one names."

"They asked to be examined."

"They did not ask to be examined, they asked to be seen by a physician, and you have taken a piece of paper and asked them their household and the date of their last draw and the form number, and now you have a list." She turned it round. "What am I to do with sixty-one names?"

"You are to take it to the Trust."

"The Trust will take it and the Trust will rule that the list is not in the form required, and then the Trust will rule that the list is a statement by a person of standing and not a document of fact, and then the Trust will rule that a year scar is not a thing that can be read off a body's history, because there is no office that certifies one. I have been to that room four times."

"Then what do you want?"

"I want to look at the hands," said Neris Kade. "That is what I do. I look at the hands."

So Marek put the paper down and for two hours the two of them went round the four yards and the low kitchens and the school house and were extremely rude to one another about method.

He asked the questions in the order the forms ask them. She read a hand the way a person reads a door: how it came off a barrow, which finger took the strain, whether the skin at the base of the thumb had gone shiny, whether a seam at the inside of the elbow was deep and warm or shallow and cold, and how a body took up a cup. She could put a season on a scar. Sometimes a week — a scar under a week is shiny and pink and has not yet begun to grow skin back over the edge of itself, and a scar of two seasons has the flat matte look of a repaired gate and can be told from a fresh one across a yard.

He had a column structure and four hours of it and could put a name in a box faster than anybody she had worked with. She had no columns at all. She had about nine questions and an order, and the order was the wrong way round.

"You read the instrument first," she said, at the second hour, at the Tanner's yard, with rain coming off the eaves onto the back of the list. "You are asking a man when he signed and then you are looking at his arm. That is how a clerk works. It is how a clerk has to work. It is how the tower was built and it is why the tower can be lied to in a sentence, and every single thing I have ever found was found the other way round."

"And the other way round is a man telling you how he feels."

"The other way round is a man lifting a pail." She put her hand out. "Give me the list. Do not argue with me about it, we do not have the day. Hold the pail however you would hold it, and lift it, and I will look at your elbow and I will tell you the season, and then you may write it in a box."

He gave her the list.

She looked at him across the trestle table with rain coming down on the paper between them and said the thing that became the whole afternoon.

"A scar is not a figure, Vale. That is what your problem is and it is the same problem as the file. A scar has a date in it, and a date has a cause in it, and a cause has an instrument behind it or it does not, and if it does not, it is a man's knee and it is nobody's business. That is all. A scar with no instrument behind it is somebody's private business. A scar with a schedule behind it is a line in a case. Yours has neither, because you have gone at it as a man writing down names, and a name is not a case and a case is not a name."

"I have four hours of daylight and four hundred people in front of me and a hearing that is not going to sit for another six weeks."

"You have four hours and one method that cannot be read aloud in a room by people who are not clerks." She picked up the slate. "Anybody who cannot read a column can be shouted at. A body is harder. Go and get the chairs."

They did it badly for another hour.

And then, at about the fourth bell, in the low kitchens, with two hundred people in the room and Sera Holt's people working the far end of it, Neris Kade put a name down and said, "That is the vigor clause. Form fourteen, drawn the fourth, witnessed by a Coutts and a Weaver. The left forearm. Season, not year — I would not put a year on that one without the form."

And Marek wrote it down and then stopped, with the pen on the paper, because she had said the thing he had not known she had.

"Everything with a form number goes in the case pile," he said. "And everything without one goes where?"

"Go and look."

He went and looked. He went round nine yards' worth of Tallow Ward with her and then he wrote the piles out on the back of the list in his own hand, which is a thing he has never in his life done and which is the only way he has ever changed his mind.

*The Silt's years.* Nobody's instrument. Nine days of a flood, a ward that was drawing two and three-quarter times its schedule on carried water and boiled pans and no sleep, and a room full of people whose bodies are paying a fortnight of the fourteenth and the fifteenth now and will go on paying for years. They are not owed by anybody. They are owed by the river.

*The vigor clauses.* Forty-one names so far, form numbers, dates, witnesses. Lawful, witnessed, informed, and the form reads as reasonably as any hire contract a man has ever signed. Twelve of them are already showing a season's scar. Nine of the twelve are fuel-yard or bridge hands, which is not a coincidence and is not anybody's fault.

*The hold's years.* Nobody's book. The fourteenth, six bells, one clerk, no form number, no instrument, and everybody's body.

"How many in the third?"

"Thirty-seven," said Neris Kade, "and that is a floor, because it will be a ceiling by Friday, and I want your clerk's hand on the list by dark."

"It is not the ward's," Marek said. "It will be every hand that was in that water on the fourteenth, and that is a different number, and I have not checked a tenth of them."

"Then we will not write a number at the head of the column," said Neris Kade, "and that is going to be the argument of this afternoon, and you are going to lose half of it."

He did it. It took him an hour and a half, and it is the only piece of work he has ever done that he would be willing to have his own name read out in a room beside.

He wrote his own in last, in his own hand, in the third pile, at the bottom, with the date, the fourteenth, the two places, and a description in Neris Kade's terms that he copied exactly, because she had said that the description of a thing is not improved by the person it happens to be doing it to.

Neither of them said anything about whose it was. She read it, wrote the season, said "Fourteen," and went on to the next arm.

---

They wrote the form out on the back of the complaint board's second sheet and it took them until the light.

*Examine the body before the instrument. A scar is dated, not counted.*

*Where there is an instrument, name the instrument: form number, date of draw, name of the certifying clerk. Where there is no instrument, the scar is not recorded here and the person is not asked to say.*

*A person may stop at any point, and the stopping is recorded as a stopping, in their own words or not at all.*

*An independent witness, not paid out of the district account and not living in the yard, signs the sheet. Two sheets are never kept by one person.*

They left it on the trestle table under the eaves at the Gate yard where the ward could check it and any Trust man who cared to come down off the causeway and take a copy.

"You have not published a number," Neris said.

"No."

"You have not published a number and you have not published a name and you have written down a method on the back of a complaint sheet and left it in a yard. That is the first honest thing I have seen a clerk do in six years and I am going to be difficult about it for the rest of the afternoon."

"Your third pile," Marek said. "It needs a description at the head of the column. It cannot be *unattributed* and it cannot be *ward*, and it cannot go on a Trust form the way it stands, because a Trust form has a place for a name and the person in that pile does not have one to give."

"It has a place for a name that the person in the third pile is not."

"Then the description is *the delay of the fourteenth, the standing hold of the fourteenth, six bells, held without authority*."

"And by that description a Deputy Steward will open a file, and a Trust man will write *delay held by a clerk, damage distributed*, and a clerk's delay is a clerk's arithmetic, and it will be a thing that happened to somebody, by somebody, and the ward's people will be a line in a case about a clerk's error, and that is the way a shared loss becomes one man's bad luck, and I have watched a village in the Red Uplands do it in about four years with a much smaller number." She put the pen down. "Write *the sixth-to-the-fourthteenth*. The dates. No name. That is what it was, and a date is not a person and a person cannot be an error."

"A scar with no instrument behind it is somebody's private business. That is your own sentence, three hours ago, and it is the reason a scar can be read aloud in a room without the person being made a spectacle, and I am not going to argue it away from you twice in one day."

"Then don't argue it away from me. Use it." She nodded at the sheet. "The delay has no instrument. So it does not go in the case pile. It goes in the ward's, with a date and a season and a body, and it does not go into a file until somebody can produce a form number, and there is no form number, and there is not going to be one, because there is no form. Write that at the head of the pile. *The delay. The fourteenth. No instrument. There is no form.*"

Marek stood at a trestle table in the wet with a pen and a wet sheet and looked at it for a while.

He was aware of the shape of it exactly, because he had built the same shape in a vault on the twelfth: a cost, described in a language in which no living person can be made responsible for it, leaning on a document that does not exist, so that no counter can be brought to a door.

"It is exactly like the reserve," he said.

"Yes," said Neris Kade. "It is. And that is the argument, and you have made it, and you did not make it as a clerk. Get your mother in, and the fuel yard, and the school, and anybody in this ward who has a body and a reason. A man who is owed has to be able to point at something. Ask them what they would put at the head of the column, write down every answer, and do not write your own down at all."

They did that until it was dark, and forty-one people came past that table in the wet and said a sentence about a column heading, and the sentence that appeared eleven times was *the sixth to the fourteenth*, and the sentence that appeared nine times was *the day the clerk took it up*, and four people said something much worse and one of those four was a woman of fifty in a Warden's coat who had been two nights at a sluice with a bucket.

Neither of them got what they wanted. He did not get a description he could defend. She did not get a list of names. They got a form, four sheets on the table, and a column heading written four different ways in forty-one hands with a blank space left at the top of it, and Neris Kade folded the pen and said, in a voice with nothing in it at all:

"You are the first clerk I have worked with who has made himself worse on the record on purpose. Do not thank me for it. I have not forgiven you for Thursday and I am not going to, and we are going to have a great deal more Thursdays, and I would like that on your list too."

"Noted," said Marek, and wrote it down, and it was the only entry he made that week that he did not have to go back and correct.

At the last of the light she said, without turning round, that Hesper Rann had sent for a recount of the counter-desk totals and wanted a third-grade clerk in the room, and that the clerk he had asked for was Marek, and that if he went, he would be the only person in the building who had ever seen a cause line written before the arithmetic was checked, and that he should think about what that meant for about an hour before he answered.
