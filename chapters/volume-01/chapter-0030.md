# Chapter 30 — The Ordinary Course

They served the finding on the ward at the fifth bell of the thirtieth of the rains, at the Gate tank, in the open, in the first mild evening since the flood, because the Threshold's regulations require a settlement finding to be served on the parties and the parties of a ward finding are the ward, and a ward is served where it is.

The clerk of the third floor read it out. He had a folding stool and a seal and a wind, and he read the whole of it twice, once for the yard and once for the people who had come in from the Tanner's and the Fuel basin and were still standing when he had finished the first reading.

"Finding of the Deputy Settlement Steward in the matter of the water account of Tallow Ward, ninth to the thirtieth day of the rains.

"First. The ward settlement of the ninth day is lawful. The standing reserve as recited is an account of the water office and is honoured as certified.

"Second. The excess drawn at the ordinary wage is a lawful settlement and stands. The delivery certified for the peak night is corrected to four hundred and forty hundreds of pints against a lawful hundred and sixty, and the excess is two hundred and eighty. The ward's own assessment carries two hundred and seventy-nine hundreds and forty pints, and the two differ by twenty pints in nine days.

"Third. The delay of the fourteenth night, six bells, held by a clerk of this office without authority and against an instrument that does not exist, is not within the terms of the settlement. No account of it arises under these terms and none is provided.

"Fourth. The collection from the eleven households named in the assessment will proceed in the ordinary course, at the ordinary wage, in the sums set out in the roll. Four of the eleven are payable this season, being the four named in the posting of the ninth day. The first four are: Rezek, of the Fuel basin. Ostrow, of the Tallow Gate. Prent, of Sill Row. Marsh, of the Tanner's."

The clerk read the last line and squared the paper and put the seal away, and it was Bohdan Rezek who made the noise.

Rezek was fifty-one and had been a fuel-yard foreman for twenty-two years and had seized in the copy room on the ninth of the rains and had not been able to close his hands properly since, and he stood at the front because he had come in from the Fuel basin early, and when the clerk said his household's name he said "Say that again" in a voice that made two hundred people go quiet, and the clerk said it again.

"Rezek, of the Fuel basin. Ostrow, of the Tallow Gate. Prent, of Sill Row. Marsh, of the Tanner's."

"Right," said Rezek. He put his hands out in front of him and opened them, slowly, both of them, the way a man does in front of strangers and cannot help doing, and they did not do what hands are for. "Twenty-two years I have taken a barrow off that basin with these and I have had a fortnight where I could not hold a cup, and they have written my name at the top of a list of four, and that is correct, and I have come here to say so and to say it out loud, so that the four of us are not four people who heard about it."

That was the only thing said in the yard for about a minute.

Then Nadja Ostrow asked the only question anybody was going to be able to ask for three weeks, and she asked it to the clerk and not to Marek, which was correct.

"Third one," she said. "It says no account of it arises. What was the six bells, then."

"It was a delay of the settlement," said the clerk, from his stool. "Held on the night of the fourteenth by a clerk of this office. It was not within the terms, and there is no office in this building that holds a delay, and so nothing arises under it and nothing is owed and nothing is claimed. That is what the third clause says."

"Then the six bells of the fourteenth do not exist," said Nadja Ostrow.

"They are recorded," said the clerk, "and no account of them arises."

She stood in the yard for a moment and then she said, "That is the cheapest sentence anybody has said to me in my life," and went and stood at the back, and Kern said nothing, and the yard said nothing, and a woman in the second row of the crowd said *four* out loud and was told by two people to be quiet.

Marek Vale stood at the end of the row with the finding in his hand — the ward's copy, served, with the seal, with the four names on the fourth clause — and understood the whole of the third clause in about nine seconds and it went into him and stayed there for the rest of his life.

There was no forum for it. Not an unfair one. No forum at all. A delay held without authority by a man with no office to hold it, against an instrument that did not exist, on a night when six bells of six hundred people's sleep had been put into a clerk's own body by a remedy in the margin of the oldest book in the tower, had produced a document. The document was a notice on a posting board with *no office of record* written on the line where the office should have been, and the office is vacant and has been vacant since the third century, and there is no box anywhere in the city for a claim by a man against a delay that no office holds, because a delay that no office holds is not a thing the city has done to anybody, it is a thing a man has done to a clerk on a stair, and he cannot produce a copy of it in any room in this building without producing the page he copied it from, and the page cannot be copied, by a standing order of the Yearspire Trust, and a copy of it is a second document, and a second document is not permitted to be produced in any room.

So there was no forum, and there would never have been one, and if thirty people in that yard had gone down on their knees in the mud outside the Threshold that same evening, they still could not have named a body to ask.

He had known that since the sixteenth. He had known it since the fourth bell of the afternoon of the sixteenth, in a copy room, with a wet elbow, with a sheet of paper in his own hand that said *copied from the original* and was worth nothing whatsoever, and he had gone on being frightened of that fact instead of using it, for fourteen days, and had used it today, and had got the delivery corrected by twenty pints and a district account corrected by twenty-eight thousand eight hundred, and had bought the ward a hearing on the fourth of next month, and the six bells of the fourteenth were still in his arm and would still be there when he was sixty.

---

He went up to the levy office at the sixth bell, and the levy office was on the second floor and it was the same room, and a levy clerk of about sixty called Ostrowsky was putting the thirtieth's returns into the day's tray, and there was a form on his desk with a pad behind it and a desk's book open at a column headed *recounts entered*.

"Ostrowsky. I want the file."

"You have had the file since the twelfth, clerk. It is the thirtieth." He put it down. "Finding's on top. Two of the pages are new, the delivery and the account. And there is a line at the foot of the fourth clause that says the collection may not be entered before the cause is entered, which is a new line, which the Deputy put in herself, and which I have read four times this afternoon and I am not going to pretend to you that I know what it means, because I have been the levy clerk of this hall for nineteen years and I have never seen that line."

"Who wrote it."

"She did. At the fourth bell, with a clerk present, and the clerk is me." He looked at the desk's book. "And I am to enter a recount. Not today. There is a sitting on the twelfth of next month, and notice goes on the board tomorrow, and the parties have twelve days' notice of the sitting, and the cause goes on the form at the sitting and not before, because a cause is written before the arithmetic and the arithmetic is at the sitting."

"Who is the clerk of the day for the recount."

"You are, clerk, and I put your name on it this afternoon because you are the clerk of the day for the ward and there is nobody else, and I am sorry about it, and I have been the levy clerk of this hall for nineteen years and I put it in and I did not enjoy putting it in." He squared the tray. "I will say one thing to you, and I will say it in procedure, and then I will go and do my returns. A cause has to be in the box, and a false-witnessed instrument is collected against a cause, and I do not have to tell you where a collection lands, because you were in the room on the fifteenth when the officer of that room said it and said that anybody in the room who could not say which of the two papers in the folder carries the defect had not read the file."

He turned back to his tray.

"The twelfth," he said. "Twelve days' notice from tomorrow. Do not be late and do not be clever, because a cause is not a place to be clever, it is a box with a sentence in it and the sentence is the whole of the case."

Marek stood in the levy office for a while after the clerk had gone back to his returns, and then he took the form out of the pad and held it up to the lamp, and it was an ordinary form, a desk's form, with a box at the top of it for a cause, and the box was empty, and there was nothing in it at all, and the pad had forty more behind it, and the twelfth of next month was twelve days away.

And in the back page of a book in his coat, in the levy office's own hand, under a wrapper, alongside a footnote copied out of a page no clerk may copy and three renderings of a labour schedule that eleven households have never seen, there were nine words.

*Cause: the totals of this entry were added at the counter-desk from a defective schedule.*

He had written them himself on the twenty-first at the fifth bell, in under a minute, with his hand going at the elbow the whole way, and he had not filed them and had not shown them to a living soul since, and he could not show them to a living soul now, and the reason he could not was the same reason he could not warn the ward at the Gate yard an hour ago, and the same reason he could not stand up in a room on the fourth of next month and say the thing he knew, and it was this: everything a man could say about a sentence that has not been written required a document, and a document was the only thing in this city that had ever moved anything at all, and he could not make one.

Not a copy of page three, because a copy of it is a second document and may not be produced in any room.

Not a copy of the cause line, because the cause line has not been written, and a private copy of a sentence that does not exist is a certification of nothing at all, and would be a fifth thing in a book that was already four documents a man could not use.

Not a warning, because a warning from a clerk to four hundred people in a yard is a sentence at a door, and in nine days somebody in a room with a door in it would ask him where the warning came from, and he would have to say *from a thing I cannot show you*, and a man in this building who says that sentence twice in a fortnight is a man whose file fills itself.

So there was nothing to do on the thirtieth of the rains at the sixth bell, and he did it, and it took about four seconds, and then he put the form back in the pad and squared the pad and went and stood at the window of the levy office for a while looking at the causeway with the lamps coming on along it, and there was a boy's name in the fourth clause of a finding he was going to have to be in a room about in three weeks, and there were nine words in a box that had not been written in, and he had not said one word of it to anybody in this building since the twenty-first, and he was not going to, and the reason he was not going to was the thing he had been carrying in a book for fourteen days, which is that a second document cannot be produced in any room, and a man who produces one is not believed.

---

Hesper Rann was in the corridor outside the copy room at the seventh bell and he had his coat over his arm and he was going home at seven o'clock in the evening for the first time since the ninth, and he stopped, and he was kind, because he was always kind.

"That was well done," he said. "The reading book. The take-off. The twenty pints." He put his coat on. "I would have taken a month over it and I would have got it wrong, and you did it in five days with two clerks and a field examiner who is not yours, and I want that said by somebody to somebody, so I am saying it to you."

"Thank you."

"Clerk of the day for the recount on the twelfth," said Hesper Rann.

"Yes."

"Well." He adjusted his cuff, and did not look at the levy office door, and Marek understood that he knew precisely which room it was and precisely how far along it was. "The thing about a sitting, Vale, is that it is not about the past. Everybody thinks it is about the past. It is about a box, and about who is in the room when the box is filled, and a cause is not a finding out. A cause is a reason to collect." He put his hand on the stair rail. "You read a book. You did a good day's work and the ward is twenty pints better off than it was and the account is twenty-eight thousand eight hundred honest, and I would not undo a line of it. And whatever is coming on the twelfth was coming on the ninth, and it would have come if you had never touched a wall book in your life."

"Mr Rann—"

"No." He went down one step and stopped, and he did not turn round, and what he said next was said to the stair. "I know what you have known since the twenty-first. You have not said it and you are not going to, and I have watched you for thirteen days and you have not said it once, and I am not going to be a man who helps you to say it." A pause. "I am not going to help you and I am not going to stop you. That is the whole of what I have for you this evening, and I have said it at a stair rail at the seventh bell, which is the only hour of this day on which either of us will be able to pretend it did not happen."

Then he went down the stair, and Marek stood in the corridor of the copy room on the second floor of the Yearspire with the lamps burning along the north wall, and the ward four hundred and twenty people strong was down at the Gate reading a finding with four names on it, and the cause line was not written, and there were twelve days.

He got home through the Standing Market in the rain at the ninth bell and up the hill to his own room above the lockkeeper's shop, where there was no soup and no wood and no light, and he took the book out and opened it at the back page and looked at the four things in it, and did not write anything, and shut it, and sat on the edge of the bed with his coat still on for a while.

Somebody in this ward was going to be named on the twelfth of next month in a box at the top of a form, and he knew the nine words, and he could not say them in a room, and he could not say anything else in a room either, and the ward knew that a bill was coming and did not know that the bill was aimed.

It was the last day of the rains, or the thirtieth day of them, and the river was down, and the lamps were lit along the Tallow Gate, and Marek Vale sat in a cold room and did the only thing left to him, which was to sit there and not be the man who said it.
