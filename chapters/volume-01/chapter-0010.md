# Chapter 10 — The Last Living Name

The settlement came due at the sixth bell, which was an hour and a half later than the peak, and that hour and a half was the whole of the mercy the night had in it, and Marek knew what the sound meant before it had finished, because he had spent four years learning to hear a due date.

Then he did the arithmetic, and it took about four seconds, and it was not arithmetic.

*The ward.* The ward was the obligated party and the ward's levy fund was the sum. The fund had been drawn down in the second week of the rains by the gate loan's upkeep, which is what a gate loan's upkeep is for, and the balance in the ward's account at the sixth bell of the fourteenth was nothing at all. The yards had been running at nearly three times their standing schedule for nine days on rationed water and carried water, and what the levy asked of the ward at the peak was nine days of standing schedule times four hundred people, and there was no road work in that account because there was nothing in it.

*No surety.* The entry named no surety. It had a schedule in it with a surety column, and eleven names, and not one signature, and a schedule with no signatures in it is a schedule of intentions.

*False-witnessed.* The entry had an offsetting recital in it that resolved, through a footnote on its own page, to the first line of the oldest reference volume in the tower, which had never been filled in, and it had left out a material term from the parties it was posted against. That is what false-witnessed means, and Marek had written the definition out in a clerk's manual four years ago and had never once had cause to use it on a person.

*The institution that certified.*

He put his hand on the entry.

It was the fifth time in five days and he did it the way you scratch a dog you have already scratched, without any thought at all, and the pain came up behind the right eye and the hall went hard-edged and the page went quiet, and the entry came up out of the paper with the fair fast hand and the four clauses and the nine signatures in the chain at the foot, and the Sight gave him the whole of the walk at once, in order, the way a clerk reads a chain, four steps, and nothing at all at the end of it:

> The file was countersigned. The countersigner is alive.

He took his hand off.

The headache came up and stayed up. There was a taste of iron again and his left hand was shaking and the lamp on the Chair landing was the only thing in the world with an edge on it.

But the Sight was right, and it was right in a way that he had known for five days and had arranged, with great care and total success, not to think about.

There were two signed papers in the Tallow folder. His own name was on one of them, in a small upright clerk's hand, on a one-page certificate of posting, dated the eleventh day, countersigned with a full stop of a pen stroke at the end of the hour column, and that paper was the paper that had made the entry due tonight instead of provisional, and it was in the same folder, in the same clip, under the same copper.

The other one was the entry.

And the chain followed the instrument, not the folder. He had known that since his second year; it was on the first page of the manual and it had a worked example under it about a house sale in the Rainmarket, and the point of it was that a case is a bundle and a bundle is a convenience, and the tower does not read bundles, the tower reads one paper at a time and asks what is on this paper and who put their name on it.

The invented reserve was on the entry. So was the omission: the term that was left out was a term of this entry and of nobody else. And the counter-desk totals were on the entry too, in pencil, at the foot, in the hand of a boy of twenty.

Marek stood in the copy room of the Tally Halls at the sixth bell of the night of the fourteenth of the rains and understood the whole of it, all at once, with the sick clarity of a man who has been given an answer to a question he had been asking the wrong way, and the answer was that he had spent four days worrying about the wrong signature in the wrong folder.

At the foot of the entry, in pencil, in a small upright hand, in the light from the Chair landing four storeys up:

*O. Kelb, counter-desk, third week.*

He had read that name on the ninth day through a closed door. He had read it on the tenth at a window on an island in the rain. He had had the man in front of him at his own bench on the seventh afternoon, in cuffs he was not used to, telling him with pride that he had added up a column with a pencil because the pen had not come, and Marek had said *the totals were in, you were right about that*, and meant it kindly.

He went up the stair.

Kelb was on the Chair.

He was sitting on it in the way a man sits on a step when he has been sitting for some time and has not decided whether to get up: his back against the wall on the city side, his legs out, a board across his knees, and four and a half pages of the ward's levy in longhand held down under his left forearm, and the roll, and the schedule, and his right hand in his lap, open, palm up, in the posture of a hand that has been put down and not picked up.

The lamp beside him was turned down to a bead. The ink had gone thick in the pot. He had the roll and the entry done and the schedule written out and the certification line ready in his head, and he had begun the entry's fourth page, and the tower's rule is that a hand copy is not a copy until it carries the four words at the foot that say what it is a copy of, and the boy had begun them, and had written *Copied from the orig*, and had stopped.

The words are the certification. Everything before them is a man's night.

"Kelb."

The boy looked up. He did not startle, and Marek understood, looking at him, that he had been aware of everything for some minutes and had simply been sitting with it, which was not a thing that twenty-year-olds were supposed to be able to do.

"It's the right hand," Kelb said, and then, hearing himself, "I mean — I think it's the right hand."

Marek came down the last three steps and knelt on the stair, which was an undignified thing to do on the Chair step and he did it anyway.

"Show me."

Kelb raised it. The hand was warm and dry and it was perfectly, terribly still, in a way that hands are not. Marek put two fingers in the palm and said his name, twice, and the boy's fingers did not answer.

"What's your name," Marek said, which is what you do.

"Ondra Kelb. Three weeks in the post, if anybody asks you, and I would rather you said it than the intake sheet." He said it clearly, and he said it the way a man says the thing he has been holding most carefully. "Mr Vale, I want to say the thing I should say and I want to say it now, while I can still feel my face, and I want you to hear it properly and not to write it down for me."

"Go on."

"I added up a column and it balanced." Kelb's voice was steady and it was very slightly too loud, the way a voice gets in a stone stairwell. "I want that written down first. Before anything else. I added up the totals of the ward entry and they balanced and I signed them and I did it with a pencil because the pen hadn't come, and if somebody is going to say I did it without looking—"

"You looked," Marek said.

"I looked at the column," Kelb said. "I did not look at who wrote the column. And I have been trying since the fourth bell to work out whether those are the same thing, and I have decided that they are not, and that I would rather say that out loud in a hall than have somebody say it for me. I am twenty. I am nineteen. I was three weeks in the post. And the figures in front of me balanced."

Marek put his hand flat on the boy's knee.

"Yes," he said. "That is what happened. I have to tell you something else and I am going to tell you now, and I am not going to soften it, and if you want somebody else to be here you can say so now and I will go and get them."

He told him. All of it, standing on the Chair step of the Tally Stair, in the flat voice he used for reading out a total: that he had suspended the settlement after the last of the light and before the first bell, on the authority of a remedy in a margin that named an office which had been vacant for three centuries, that he had had no authority and no witness, that a hold does not cancel a debt and does not stay the collection and adds nobody to the chain, that the delay had gone into him and into nobody else, and that the collection had just walked out of a false-witnessed entry and along a chain of four steps to the pencil at the foot of the fair fast hand, and that the pencil was the boy's.

Kelb listened to the whole of it.

He did not interrupt. He did not go white, or shout, or weep; his face was already doing something that Marek had to look away from, and when Marek looked back it had finished and settled into an expression of pure, tiresome administrative attention, and it was the expression of a young man who has been given a fact and is now arranging it.

"Right," Kelb said. "Then that's on the instrument."

"Yes."

"Not on the certificate."

"No."

"Then somebody is going to read those two papers in a room, and they are going to be in the same folder, and one of them is going to have your name on it and the other one mine, and one of us is going to be told to explain it." Kelb's hand lay open and upturned in his lap. "Mr Vale. Where is the hearing?"

"The fifteenth. Eighth bell. The Trust's third floor, and she is presiding over it herself."

"I'm not going to be at it," Kelb said, "so you will have to be the one who says it, and you should say it in that order. The entry first. My name second. Yours third. If you say yours second they will only ever hear yours."

"I will say it in that order."

"Right," said Kelb, and then his head went back against the wall and his eyes went to the lamp, and he said, with enormous twenty-year-old reasonableness, "I'm going to be sick," and Marek got him off the Chair step and down four storeys and out into the rain and as far as the causeway, and the whole of that walk he carried a boy and an armful of the ward's levy in longhand, and the pages were the ward's, and the boy was the chain's, and he had made both of them.


The Morrowfen clinic was a converted tannery on the Rainmarket side with a surgery door on the lane and a waiting room that holds fifty people, and it was full, because the fourteenth night of a flood is the night the city hurts itself, and most of what came through that door on the fourteenth was not hurt at all but frightened, and frightened people are heavy.

Neris Kade was in the corridor with her back against the wall and her satchel across her knees and a list in her hand, and when Marek came up the lane with a boy in his arms she did not get out of the way and she did not help, and she said, "Lane. Not the door. The door is for people who are walking."

He went down the lane.

Sera Holt was at the end of it in a bloodied apron with her sleeves above her elbow, and she took one look, and said, "Stair. Wall. Now."

They got Kelb onto a bench under a lamp. Sera took the right wrist and the left wrist and the jaw and turned the hand over four times, and asked him things whose answers she did not write down, and at some point she said "thank you" and made him lie down, and then she came out into the lane where Marek was standing with blood down the front of his shirt and a boy's pages under his arm, and looked at him, and said:

"How long have you been bleeding."

"Since the light went, on the fourteenth."

"That is a strange answer to a question about a nose." She wiped her hands on the apron and came and stood in front of him. "Do you know what has happened to that boy's hand?"

"He is not certain it is the right hand. He would like to be."

"It is his right hand." Sera said it flatly, because she had been doing this for twenty-six years and a physician who softens the sentence softens the thing that happens afterwards. "It is not paralysis. He can move it. He will be able to lift a cup at sixty. What he has lost is the years of it — the fine use of it, the part of a hand that learns what a hand is for, which comes at the end of a life and not at the beginning. His has been taken out of the middle, and there is nothing left in the ledger to put back, because it was not a loan. There was no pledge and no surety and no settlement and no day on the calendar called the fourteenth. It went the way a debt goes when the person who owes it is the one who signed for it, and the only thing a court can say about it is that a thing is missing and a hand is short."

"Is it permanent."

"I have had this eleven years and I will tell you what I tell the families." She held his eye. "The body pays what the file could not. That is the sentence I have, and I have never found a better one, and I would give the whole of my life to a building that understood it. The years are already gone. They went tonight. They are not in a fund and they are not in a court and they are not coming back on Thursday. And the only question left in that boy's life, for the rest of it, is whose they were."

Marek said, "They were the ward's."

"They were the ward's, and the ward did not ask, and you are the one who has to write that sentence, and you are going to have to write it in a room with his mother in it." She looked past him down the lane, and her voice changed, very slightly, and not to warmth. "You have put your hand on a live record five times in five days and you held up a settlement with a clause you were not entitled to. I will not ask what you thought you were doing, and I will not tell you I would have done it differently, because I have done it. Once, in ninety-one, in the winter. It worked. There is a woman in the low beds on the other side of this building who is forty-one and has the hands of a seventy-year-old, and I have never once been able to make that come out even."

"Then what do I do."

"You say it out loud to whoever asks. All of it, in order, tonight, to every person who comes into that hall, and you do not put it in a drawer, and you do not let somebody else say it in a room you are not standing in." She looked at the pages under his arm. "And you will carry something yourself. I would tell you now, so that you are not surprised in a month. Six bells of delay does not come out of a nose. It comes out of a yearline, and there will be a thing about you that is not there now, and it will not be dramatic, and I would like you to be sitting in a chair when you find out."

The waiting room door opened, and Neris Kade came out with her list, and stood at the lane end, and looked at Marek, and did not come closer.

"You carried him down the lane," she said. "Not the door. The lane."

"Yes."

"Four hundred and thirty-one names came out of the low kitchens tonight onto a list in my own hand, and I have been standing in this corridor for an hour keeping a frightened crowd out of a surgery lane, and I did that while you were carrying a clerk down a wall." She said it without any expression at all. "I am not thanking you. I want to be clear about that. And in a few hours there is going to be a hearing at which I am going to say things about a levy roll that you will find extremely unpleasant, and I intend to say every one of them."

"I know," Marek said. "Line seven is mine and I did not know it was mine when I read it out in your aisle, and I have thought about very little else since."

"Then we shall get on," said Neris Kade, and went back in, and shut the door, and that was the whole of it, and she had kept the crowd out of the lane for an hour while a man whose name was on the roll carried somebody else.


Two Trust men came up the causeway before light with a key-ring and a lantern and began closing the vaults, because the tower closes its vaults whether or not anything has happened in it.

The reference case was in the third alcove and the hasp was down and the lead seal had been broken and put back. Marek stood in the doorway with the vault clerk's lamp and looked at it for a moment longer than a man with nothing to do has any business doing.

Inside, in a sewn ledger with a limp sheepskin cover, the first page was still blank.

The line was ruled and the space for an opening entry was empty, and underneath the blank there were two characters cut with the blunt of a knife by a hand that had been dead for four hundred years, and a Trust order in a later hand saying that no clerk might erase the signature and no clerk might copy the page, and in the margin, in ink the colour of weak tea, the remedy that had cost a boy his hand.

He did not go in. He stood in the doorway and then he went up the cold stair and out into the morning, because there was nothing in the case but a page, and the page was not going anywhere, and the ward was going to be at the hearing at the eighth bell on the fifteenth, and the ward had water.


First light in the copy room. The lamps still burned along the north wall and the smell of the lamps was the smell that the room had every morning of his working life, and there were four sheets on the floor where somebody had put them down and no feet on them, and the board on the Chair step was still nailed to it with the ink dry on it and the certification unfinished at the foot.

Marek came in with his coat over his arm and the ward's levy in longhand under it, four and a half pages and the roll and the schedule and the holding notice, uncorded because a bundle is corded when it is finished, and put it on the desk of the duty clerk, and asked who had come in, and was told, by a woman who was not looking at him, that nobody had come in, and that a runner had been and gone at the first bell and had left something, and that she had not touched it.

It was on the corner of the posting desk, under a weight.

A ward roll. Not the Tallow one; Marek knew the Tallow one, he had read it at the Threshold with two hundred people in an aisle. This was a roll of the kind the ward clerks keep in their own chests at the bottom of a cupboard, a working roll, a thing nobody had ever asked to see, and it was about the thickness of four fingers and it had been rained on.

"Nim Rook," Marek said.

"She said to give it to the Tallow clerk and she'd come back for it at the ninth bell." The duty clerk's woman pulled her hand away from it. "Are you the Tallow clerk?"

"I am."

"Then that's yours. She's not yours though. She's nobody's. She's been in and out of this room all night and I've had two junior clerks ask me if I wanted to have words with her and I said no, because I've been in this room twenty-two years and I've seen her come in and go out four hundred times, and I could not tell you in a single case which of those four hundred times she did not leave exactly where she found everything."

Marek picked the roll up.

It was a ward roll, and ward rolls are the same everywhere: a district column, a number, a head-count, a yard or a street, a line for a repair, a line for a withdrawal, a line for a death, and a column at the far right for the assignment of the quarter's rolls to the tower, in the year, with an initial.

He was a clerk. He read columns. He read it the way he had read a receipt against its sealed terms, at arm's length, in bad light, looking for the two places where two inks disagreed about the same year.

The entries ran from the top of the flood to the sixth. District column: a ward and a number, over and over, in the yard clerk's hand, in the same fair small writing people use when they are writing something they expect to have to read again.

He did not know it.

He turned the roll over and looked at the far right column, at the assignment initials and the years, and he read them, and he did it twice, and then he did it the third time the way you do a thing when you have understood that you are not going to understand it and want to be very sure that you are not stupid.

He was not stupid. He was a third-grade clerk in the public audit office of the tower on Stillstep Island, which held, in eleven rooms and four floors of shelves, the complete record of every district in the city, its numbers, its rolls, its head-counts, its wards, its bounds, its first levy and its last, and the district column of a ward roll is a number and a name — four, Kallow; seven, Tallow Gate; nine, Ropewalk; eleven, the Tanner's — and every one of those is in the book on the shelf behind him, a four-hundred-page register with a spine that says DISTRICTS AND ROLLS in the stamped letters of the founding office.

There is no code in that book for the number in the district column of this roll.

He went and got the book. It is on the second shelf, it is four hundred pages, and he had copied out of it four hundred times in four years, and he went through the whole of the district column twice, the two figures and then the two figures with the letter, and he got to the end of the lettered ones and started again, and it took him eleven minutes, and there is no district numbered four called Kallow in that book, and there is no Kallow in the whole of the register, and there is no such ward, and no such roll, and no such district in the city of Morrowfen, and there are thirty-one entries in the roll in a fair small hand, and the last of them is dated the ninth day of the rains.

"Clerk," said the duty clerk's woman, "there's a man in the corridor who says he's the supervisor and he's asking for you by name, and he sounds like he's been up all night, and I would tell him you were in the clinic, and then he would ask you where the clinic is, and then you'd have to have this conversation twice."

"Which ward," Marek said, without looking up. "Show me the ward on it. Not the code. The name in the ward column, where the name is."

The duty clerk's woman came and stood behind his shoulder and looked at the roll, and she was patient with him in a way that he was not going to deserve.

"There's no name," she said. "There's the code and there's a line at the head of the district column that says 'assigned,' and then the numbers, and that's all. It's a working roll. It never got its names written in."

"It is a copy," Marek said.

"That's what they all say." She was already turning back to her book. "It is a copy, and the map is the copy, and I have been in this room twenty-two years, and there is a thing I am not going to argue with a man about at six in the morning."

"Then I'll tell you what I think it is," Marek said, "and you can do as you like with it. I think it is not a copy of anything. I think it is the only one. I think the name column was never filled in, and I do not know why, and I have never seen the like of it, and I have been in this room four years and I do not know what it is."

He stopped, because his voice had done something on the last clause that he had not intended.

The duty clerk's woman looked at him for a while.

"I am going to the hearing at the eighth," she said, "because I am the duty clerk's deputy, and I have a boy of eleven and no reason to be here at six in the morning, and I will tell you the one thing I know and then I am going. I have been in this room twenty-two years and I have never once had a roll come up that I could not place. Not one. Whatever this is, it is not from a desk, because a desk does not produce this."

She picked up her own satchel.

"You'll be careful with it," she said. "Whatever else it is, it is a roll of people."

Then she went, and Marek stood at the duty clerk's desk with the roll open in his hands and the copy room coming up bright along the north wall, and he knew the first thing and only the first thing about it, which was that he did not know what to do with it. If he put it in the Tallow file, a roll with thirty-one unknown entries became an exhibit in a hearing about a levy. If he did not, it was a piece of paper in a corridor that a nineteen-year-old girl would come back for at the ninth bell, and would take away, and he would not see it again.

He put it inside his coat, on the left side, against the small book of promises he had made to his mother, and under the four pages in his own hand that he had made in a vault in violation of a standing order of the Yearspire Trust: the footnote, the blank line, the knife-cut initials, and the remedy.

The corridor outside was full of grey light off the basin and the smell of wet stone. Below them the Tallow yards were running on a public levy and a screen that Petross was going to clear again on Thursday, the Gate tank was full, and the low kitchens would be on the basin for a week. The ward — all four hundred and thirty-one names of them, and a book of them tied to a string in a school house on Sill Row — had water, and would keep it, and the water was not going to cost them anything that anybody had written down. There was a hearing at the eighth bell on the fifteenth, in the Trust's third-floor room, with the ward's copy of the levy and the file's copy to be compared in front of them.

Ondra Kelb was in a bed at the Morrowfen clinic, twenty years old, three weeks in the post, with a scar on his right hand that would be there when he was forty, and it had been put there by a remedy in a margin that named an office nobody held, taken by a clerk in an empty building between the last of the light and the first bell, for six bells, with nobody asked.

Hesper Rann was standing in the corridor with his coat on and his hands behind his back, looking at the floor, twenty-two years a clerk, and he looked up.

"Is the junior clerk awake yet?" he said.

Marek said that he did not know.

Hesper Rann said nothing else, and went down the stair to the causeway, into a morning that smelled of the Silt, and did not look back.
