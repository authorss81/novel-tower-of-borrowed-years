# Chapter 3 — The Eleven Names

The causeway to Stillstep Island took forty minutes in good weather and an hour and a half in the ninth day of the rains, and Marek walked it in the rain because a clerk who sends his boots and his papers on the same errand arrives with wet feet or wet terms.

The ward bundle was under his arm in its sack. Forty-one petitions, entered in the petition book at the hour and the bell, of which he was bringing nine originals, because the rest had already been copied by the district copyists who had gone down into the wards, and had come back this morning damp and smelling of the tanneries, and had asked him, not once but three times, whether the Threshold would actually read any of it.

He had told them he did not know. That was the truth and it had the additional advantage of being unhelpful.

The Threshold was the part of the tower the public was allowed to see first, and it was built low and wide and open, because the first thing a city needs from an institution is that you can see into it while you queue. Six windows along the front, the intake desks behind them, the public complaint board under a shelter to the left, and above the whole of it the stone of the upper halls going up into the weather. The wet came in under the door in a long grey fan and stood two fingers deep in the aisle, and the queue had learned to stand in it, because the queue had nowhere else to be.

The ward was in the aisle before the bell.

Four water-yard captains, which meant the whole of Tallow's water management, and behind them a crowd that was not a queue and did not pretend to be one. Two hundred of them, maybe. Wet coats, wet shoulders, the smell of a hundred cold rooms coming off a hundred wet bodies, and the low murmur you get in a room where everybody is looking at a desk and has decided to keep looking at it.

Aldis Kern was at the front. Forty-four, heavy through the chest, a wet felt hat pushed back off a forehead that had been rained on for nine days, and a rolled sheet of oiled paper under his arm. He had the look of a man who has spent a night being reasonable on behalf of four hundred people and has reached the end of what reason can carry.

"Marek Vale," he said, before Marek had his coat open. "You'll be the one who wrote it in the book."

"I wrote it in the book."

"Then you'll want to know what's in the roll, since you wrote in a book that the water's short and didn't write what short means." He unrolled the sheet on the ledge of the window and weighted the corners with two flat stones he had brought for the purpose, which told Marek he had done this before. "Copy. Made by our own yard clerks, Wednesday, off the assessment they handed us. It's the ward's bill."

Marek looked at it. He was not the clerk for the petition yet; he was the clerk who had logged it, and there is a difference, and he knew it, and he put his hands behind his back so that nobody would think he was reaching for it.

The sheet was a levy roll. Eleven numbered lines. Each line: a household, a head-count, and the terms on which that household's future recovery would be drawn — money, or days of work, or a named year of something.

The first four lines were the ones paying this season. He read them and knew them as yards, not as names, because that is how a ward bill is written when it has been written by an office and not by neighbours: *the Fuel basin, Ropewalk, the tanner's yard, the Gate yard.* The first four took the bulk of it. The rest of the line was days and a wage. Two of the four carried a household name in the margin, because two households in Tallow had a name anybody in the city had heard, and the office wrote them in: the fuel yard's line was the fuel-yard foreman's, and Ropewalk's line was *Ostrow, Nadja, cook, of the low kitchens*.

The other seven lines were the same form with no season written on them at all. Nothing was crossed out. Nothing was blank. They simply carried no date, which meant they were not payable this year, and would become payable whenever the ward's water was next wanted, and the ward had not voted on that, and the ward had not been asked.

Line seven.

He read it because he read pages the way other people read weather, all the way down, without deciding to.

*Vales of Sill Row — five persons, two of them named in other rolls. Recovery by labor schedule, per attached page, at the ordinary wage of the district.*

Five persons. Tomas, Tamsin, Marek, Lio, and his grandmother, who had been off the ward roll since the year of the fever and still got counted, because nobody had ever struck her out. Two of them named in other rolls.

*Per attached page.*

Marek's face did not do anything. He had a very good face for this; four years of countersigning had taught him to keep the face of a man who is reading a total while his eyes are somewhere else. He looked at line seven for the length of time a man looks at a line he is going to have to remember, and then he moved his eyes down the column to the foot of the roll, where the ward clerk had copied the total and the date and the number of the page it came from.

*Pages attached: one, two, four, five.*

He read it again.

"Three's missing," he said.

Kern, who had been watching him and not hiding that he was watching him, said: "That's what I said to the assessor. Three's not in the copy. He told me three was a labor schedule and the labor schedule isn't given out because the labor schedule isn't settled. That's their word. Not settled."

"How many pages in the file?"

"Five. He said five."

"Then the ward's copy has four pages and the file has five, and the page that isn't in your hand is the page they don't want you reading before it's settled."

"That's what I said."

"And your roll," Marek said, "is a ward bill for eleven households, four of whom are paying this season, and seven of whom are not, and none of whom were asked anything, and the ward's copy doesn't have the page that says what the seven are paying with." He straightened up. "Who drew up the assessment?"

"The Trust's levy office."

"Which office?"

"Doesn't say. It's stamped."

"Does it have a name on the stamp?"

"It has a name on it," Kern said, and looked at him, and for the first time something came into the man's face that was not rain. "It's yours, clerk. Or it's your supervisor's office, and his office is his office. Read the roll again and tell me whose stamp is on the top of it and whose handwriting is on the bottom of it. I've been a captain sixteen years and I know the difference between a document and a document somebody filled in."

Marek did not pick the roll up. He wanted to. He put his hands behind his back again.

"Half the ward's out in this aisle," Kern went on, quieter, so that only Marek and the two captains nearest heard it, "and they've come to ask for the standing schedule. Not more. The standing schedule. The standing schedule is what we got on an ordinary year. They're not asking for a cistern. They're asking for the same as a year with no flood on it. And I'll tell you what the standing schedule is, since you clerks never say it out loud: it's forty pints a person a day for a person who works, and thirty for a person who doesn't, and for an infant, whatever the mother has, because the Tower's form for that says *as the mother is able* and that phrase is the only decent sentence in the whole book. Forty pints. That's two bucketfuls and a bit. That's what a man in a fuel yard can drink and still want to be at work tomorrow. We're at half that and the tanks are going."

The bell went, in the walls, unhurried, and the crowd shifted a little and did not go anywhere.

Then a woman at the second window said, clearly, to nobody in particular: "It is not a hearing."

Marek turned.

She was standing at the second window with a satchel of oiled leather and the posture of somebody who has been in a room for an hour and has let everyone else arrive. Thirty or so. Rainmarket grey, a coat cut for standing in a yard in the wet. Her hair pinned back in a way that had clearly been done in a hurry and then not undone, because there had not been time.

"I am Kade," she said. "Independent field examiner, Rainmarket appointment. I am not the tower and I am not the ward and I have no vote in this aisle." She looked at Marek. "You are the clerk who wrote the petition in the book."

"I am."

"Then I will ask you one question and you may refuse it, and it will not be the last one."

"Ask."

"Read the roll aloud. The eleven lines, in order, with the household on each."

The aisle went quiet in the way an aisle does when it understands that something is about to be said out loud that it has only been muttering about.

Marek did not. "The roll is evidence in a matter that has not been accepted. It doesn't get read in a public aisle before intake."

"That is a very good sentence," the woman said. "I have written better ones. Clerk, I am not asking you to break anything. I am asking you to read a piece of paper to a room. If you won't, I will read it myself, and then it will be a document that entered this room outside the procedure, and your sentence will have been wasted." She turned to the crowd. "Anybody here object to a public roll being read?"

Nobody objected. Somebody at the back said something approving in a Tallow accent that got a small laugh, and the laugh is the most frightening thing Marek heard that morning, because it means the room is already a room.

"Read it," she said to Marek.

He read it.

He read the four yards, and he read the seven, and when he came to line seven he read it in the same voice he had used for the rest of it, because the only dishonour available to him in that moment would have been a change of voice. *Vales of Sill Row — five persons, two of them named in other rolls. Recovery by labor schedule, per attached page, at the ordinary wage of the district.*

Somebody swore, thinly. Somebody else said a word his mother would not have liked. The woman with the satchel did not react at all, and Marek noticed that she was watching the crowd and not him, and that she was counting something.

"You knew," Kern said, flatly. "You wrote in the book and you knew your own house was on it."

"The roll was made Wednesday. I saw it this morning. It is the first time I have seen it."

"That," said the examiner, "is the sentence he should have said and did not, and it is true, and I want it in the record that I have no reason to think otherwise, because I have watched clerks for six years and every one of them would have lied about that, and I have learned to hear it when they do." She let it sit for exactly as long as it needed. "Line seven is not the interesting line. Line seven is the line that will be used to say the ward consented, because the ward is not a person, and one of the ward's own clerks is on it, and after today your ward will not be able to say a clean no. That is what I want stopped. The other ten lines are the interesting ones."

"Then ask for line seven to be struck," Marek said.

"There is no procedure to strike a line on a roll that has not been accepted. And your hearing officer is going to tell you that accepting a levy is not a matter of hearing, because a levy is not a loan. It is a standing obligation of the ward, and standing obligations are not challenged by the people who stand in them. I know. It is the single best sentence in the building and I have heard it in four districts."

The aisle made a sound like a rope under strain.

"So what do you want?"

"I want the roll entered in the petition book with the page numbers as they are on the copy the ward holds," the examiner said. "One, two, four, five. I want the ward's copy and the file's copy compared in a room with a door, and I want a hearing on the *water*, which is the only thing here that is actually urgent, and I want it before the fourteenth. Not after the peak. Before."

Then she went to the second window, where the day's intake trays were stacked, and put her hand flat on the top tray, and did not look at Marek while she did it.

"Your brother," she said. "Tomas Vale. Vigor. Twenty-six months of his own recovery, scope the north-road span and nothing else, two witnesses, one of them his mother, settlement eleven days a season of road work and then a residual priced at the ordinary wage of the district. Four months draw on the fourteenth of the rains."

She turned a page over with the flat of her hand.

"That is a good contract," she said, and still she had not looked at him. "That is the best-witnessed contract I have read this year, and I read it in the public register in about the time it takes to boil an egg, because it is written plainly, which is the one thing this building is proudest of and the one thing nobody living outside it believes. Whoever wrote that form wanted your family to understand it, and your family did, and I have read enough of this year's lending to know that wanting that is a rarity and not a law."

She squared the tray.

"Now. Your household is seventh of eleven on that roll. The roll is not a bill yet and in three days it will be a bill, and the clerk who certifies a bill is the clerk of the day for the district named on it, and the clerk of the day for Tallow is you." She looked up then, and it was the first time. "So I am going to ask you one question in a public aisle, and you may decline it, and it will be the last question I ask you that is not on a schedule. Should a man whose household is seventh of eleven be the one who certifies it?"

The aisle went very quiet. Somebody at the back put his weight onto the other foot.

Marek had an answer ready. He had had it ready since the seventh line, and it was a good one, and it was that a clerk of the day certifies a district and not a household; that the district of Tallow had four hundred people in it and that seven of them were not his; and that the whole of the tower rested on a man being able to hold a thing that was his own and write the hour against it anyway.

He got as far as opening his mouth.

"No," said the examiner. "That is not what I asked and it is not what I want, and you had it ready, which is how I know you have thought about it." She picked up her satchel. "I am going to move on, and you are going to stand there having not answered, and I am going to come back to it on the fifteenth, in a room, with your file open."

The hearing officer at the second window, a Trust man with a dry cuff and a face like a closed ledger, said: "A levy is not a loan. The ward's levy is not subject to a hearing until the assessment is certified for posting. The ward is buying water. The ward may buy water and complain about the price at the same time; half the Crown Terrace did it last spring. You will get a hearing on the twelfth."

"The fourteenth is the peak."

"The fourteenth is the peak," the officer agreed, "and the fourteenth is also the day the assessment is posted, if it is posted, and if the assessment is not posted the ward has no water and no bill, and the ward's problem on the fourteenth would be thirst, which is a matter for the Wardens and not for me. I am not the Trust. I am a window."

Marek had heard that argument before, in other words, from other windows, and it was always true, and it had never once been the whole truth, and the whole truth was that a hearing on the twelfth would be a hearing in which the only document in dispute was a page the ward did not have.

"Twelfth," the officer said. "That's the offer and it's the whole of it. Clerk, you'll enter the bundle. I'll take the ward's copy of the roll and it will be read into the record and it will be a part of the case, and when the assessment is posted the parties will be noticed and the parties may then say whatever they like about the roll, at which point it will matter very much indeed." He looked at his cuff. "That is how it works. It is not a conspiracy. It is a queue."

Then, to the examiner: "You have standing at this window and no standing at the twelfth, because the twelfth is a hearing officer's hearing and not a Rainmarket appointment. Bring a clerk who has standing and come back."

Kade turned to Marek.

"You have standing," she said. "You are the clerk of the day for the ward named on the roll. That is not an opinion, that is a fact about a queue, and it is the only reason you are in this aisle instead of behind that desk."

Marek felt the whole thing arrive, the way you feel the exact moment a floor decides.

"Then I'll enter the bundle," he said, "and I'll take the ward's copy of the roll, and I'll do what I'm required to do with it, and when the assessment is posted, the parties will be noticed."

"And in the meantime?"

"In the meantime it's a draft. It hasn't been posted. Nothing has been certified. It's paper."

"It's paper," she agreed, "and paper that is not yet a bill is the only kind there is in this building that nobody has to answer for." She held out her hand for the roll, and then, as he gave it over, did not look at him at all and said, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as the window and not one inch further: "The reason I'm here, clerk, is that I have been in your ward two days and I have read fourteen people and there are nine of them with the flat look under the eyes that a body gets when a future has already been spent on something that hasn't happened yet. That look is not in your file. It is not on your roll. It is in no building on this island. But it is in my notebook, in my own hand, with their names on it, and I am going to keep it, and I am going to hand it to a hearing, and if nobody in this tower ever reads it then at least it will have existed twice."

She turned and went to the back of the aisle and stood where she could see the whole room.

The hearing officer put his pen to the intake book. "Bundle."

Marek stepped up to the window and set the sack on the counter, and the officer opened it, and began to take the petitions out one at a time, and stamped each, and Marek watched him do it and knew, with the specific despair of a man who understands a system completely and can do nothing inside it, that nine petitions were going into a case that would be read in March.

"Clerk," the officer said, without looking up. "You've got a copy of a levy page in your hand. That's a ward document now, and you are a party."

Marek looked down. The ward's copy of the roll was in his right hand, and he had not asked for it, and the examiner had put it there, in front of a room, on purpose.

"Before you take it," the officer said, "I have to tell you what taking it does. You may not copy it, you may not carry it off the island, and you may not enter any part of it in any book until the assessment is accepted. If you take it, you become the clerk holding a party document in an unaccepted matter, which is a thing that is looked for in a review."

"Then I will not take it."

"You may look at it here. That is allowed. Looking is not taking."

Marek set it on the ledge, flat, and looked at it properly, the way he looked at everything, at arm's length, in bad light, for the two places where two inks disagreed about the same year.

There were three inks on the ward's copy. The stamp at the head, black, office-issued, applied with a press. The roll itself, brown, written in one sitting in one hand — and he looked at that hand for a long time, because it was a fair, fast, confident hand, and the ward clerks who drew up the roll could not write like that, and it was not Hesper's hand, and it was not his own. And the totals at the foot, in a smaller hand, and correct.

Then he looked at the foot of the roll and at the page line and read it a third time, because the first time he had been reading words and the second time he had been reading the number of the document, and now he read the shape of the thing.

*Pages attached: one, two, four, five.*

And under it, in the small careful hand of the ward clerk who had copied this out on Wednesday and who had been careful, because ward clerks are careful, and who had written the page numbers from the sheet she was given, and who had therefore written down, in her own hand, on the ward's own document, that a page was missing.

Marek took out his book. He stood at the window of the second intake desk on Stillstep Island with rain coming in under the door around his boots, and he wrote in the back of it, not the petition book, because the petition book was evidence and this was not:

*Tallow assessment, ward copy, tenth day. Five pages in the file. Four in the ward's hand. Page three is a labor schedule and is not given out because it is not settled. The roll totals are correct. The roll is complete except for the page that says who stands behind it. I have no procedure. I am a party.*

He put the book away.

And then, because he was a clerk, and because the entire shape of the next four days turned on a piece of paper he had not been permitted to see, he did the one procedural thing that was available to him and that nobody in the aisle thought of, which was to ask to inspect the witness chain on a levy that named his own household.

He asked it quietly, and he asked it correctly, and the hearing officer disliked it and sent a runner up the hill for it, and the runner came back wet and out of breath with the chain page of the Tallow Ward water settlement file in a leather sleeve, and slid it under the glass.

Marek read it.

The ward was named as the obligated party. The assessment was drawn in the name of the Trust's levy office. The entry was signed as supervisor by a name he had known for four years and had never once had cause to think about.

And the second signature was the counter-desk, which was where every settlement in the hall got its totals checked by the junior of the week, because there were not enough third-grade clerks in Morrowfen to do it any other way.

The junior of the week. Three weeks in the post. Somebody who had stood at a counter with a pencil and added up a column and found that the column agreed with itself, and had signed his name at the bottom, because that is the whole of what a counter-clerk's signature means, and because nobody in this building had ever asked him where the figures came from.

Marek had handed that file over at the sixth bell on the ninth day, and he had shut the door, and he had stood in the corridor.

He read the name. It was a name he had seen on a bench assignment sheet that morning and had not looked at, because there were forty junior signatures in the hall and he had four years of service and a clean record and no reason in the world.

Then the ward behind him, and the two hundred people in the aisle, and the woman with the satchel watching all of it from the back, came up on him together. He had put the ward's water, and his own house, on a piece of paper, and made the last signature on it belong to a boy of twenty who had been in the post three weeks, and had walked out of the room and down to a yard to look at water.
