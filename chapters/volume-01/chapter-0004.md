# Chapter 4 — Two Names

Bohdan Rezek came to the counter at the fourth bell with an instalment receipt in a waxed envelope and a request that it not take long, because he had four men on the fuel line and the rain was making the tar into something you had to cut with a chisel.

Marek took the envelope and did it properly. That was the whole of what he was good at and he had never once been tempted to do it fast.

An instalment had to be read three ways: against the sealed terms in the case, against the running account of what had already been drawn, and against the drawer's body, which was not a column but was the only one of the three that could not be argued with. Rezek's bond was a labor bond with vigor attached — the ward's fuel yards had run eleven years on the same arrangement, fuel first and the body last, and the form permitted vigor as a rider where the work was weather. The sealed terms said fourteen days of endurance per instalment, scope named to the fuel line and the two basins, witness a Crown Terrace water-clerk and the fuel-yard second, settlement at the end of the season in days of work.

The receipt said sixteen.

Not sixteen instead of fourteen. Sixteen *through* fourteen, in the fourth clause, in the same ink as the rest, with the two extra days set out separately as a wet-weather allowance and initialled in a small hand at the margin.

Marek read it three times. The clause was good. That was the thing nobody outside the hall ever understood about the Tower's forms: they were not frauds, they were carefully built, and the fraud, when it came, came in as a clause.

"You were sent sixteen," he said.

"I was sent sixteen," Rezek said.

"Your sealed terms give you fourteen. The allowance is a rider and it can be applied for, and it has to be applied for, and it has to be approved by the Witness Room, and there is no Witness Room in this weather that will sit for a fuel-yard foreman's wet-weather clause."

"Clerk." Rezek put both hands flat on the counter, which was a thing he did when he was about to be patient. "It is the ninth day of a flood and it is the tenth. Is the Witness Room sitting?"

"No."

"Then I would like it noted that the Witness Room is not sitting, and then I would like my sixteen."

Marek went through it. The fourth clause was properly drafted. The margin initial was the fuel-yard second's. The Wet Weather Allowance could be granted administratively for terms of less than twenty days by a third-grade clerk at the counter-desk, on the sealed terms and the running account, which was a real power and one of the few the Chamber had, and it was a power that made a clerk personally answerable for the two days. He had granted eleven of them that season. He had never been asked for more than fifteen.

He granted sixteen, and he wrote himself into the margin as the approving clerk, and dated it the tenth day of the rains, and pushed it through.

And then Rezek took the envelope back and opened it, because a fuel-yard foreman who has drawn a fortnight of future endurance is entitled to know what the fortnight is going to feel like, and it felt like nothing, and he said so, and laughed at himself, and started to fold the envelope.

His hands stopped.

It was not dramatic. Marek had braced for dramatic. It was the hand that had been holding the envelope, and the fingers went stiff all at once, from the knuckle forward, and the thumb locked against the forefinger, and Rezek looked at his own hand with the total surprise of a man who has been caught doing something.

Then the other one. It came up out of the wrist and closed on nothing, and Rezek said, "Ah," in a completely ordinary voice, the voice a man uses when he trips over a step, and then went down sideways onto the copy-room floor and took the desk edge with him on the way.

The floor of the Tally Halls copy room is stone, laid in a grid a clerk has to look at all day, and the sound a man makes on it is not a sound that stops people talking. It stopped forty people talking. The queue at the counter-desk, the three copyists at the far benches, the runner with the ward's wet bundle, two petitioners and a pension clerk and a man from the season-vaults office who had come up about a seal: everything in that room stopped, and then a great many people started at once.

"Somebody get a Warden."

"Don't lift him, don't lift him, his hands are—"

"Water. Get water."

"He drew," said a woman's voice, thin and furious, from the front of the queue, and it carried further than any of the others because she was close to the counter, and she was a Tallow woman and she had a good deal to say. "He drew, that's what happened, don't stand there. He drew on it and it took him."

"He drew sixteen of fourteen," somebody said.

"Two days," the woman said. "Two days. That's all. Two days out of a man's life for the *tar*."

Somebody else said, "It's legal."

"It's legal," said the woman, and began to cry, which is not the same as stopping, "it's legal, that's what they say, it comes out of your own—"

"Clear a space. Clear a space, please. Warden, the second stair, tell them the second stair—"

Marek was on the floor with his hand still flat on the receipt.

He had not put the receipt down. He was aware, in a distant and entirely unhelpful way, that he was still holding it, and that his palm was flat across the fourth clause and the margin initial and the date he had written himself, and that the room had gone on without him in it.

The page was not paper. That is the only honest way to put it.

The lines were where they had always been and they were the same colour, and the entries were exactly what they had been an hour ago, and the running account at the foot was the same, and the sealed terms printed behind it were the same, and over the top of all of it there was something laid on the page the way a man lays his hand on a child's head, and it was not ink and it was not light.

> Two names. The file names one.

Then it was gone, and there was a pain in his right temple, and the room came back at its proper size.

Marek let go of the receipt. He went on doing the things that came next in the correct order, and it was a long time before he noticed that the noon bell had gone. He got an arm under Rezek's shoulders and had him sitting up with his back against the desk, and he took the man's wrists and held them, and the fingers would not lie flat, and the wrists were hot and dry and not, in any way he could see, hurt.

"Rezek."

"Go on," Rezek said, through his teeth. "Go on, it's only a—" He stopped. He worked his mouth. "It's the fourth time I've done that."

"What is."

"Closed up. The hands. It's the fourth time this year. The first three I could get out of by the fire." He laughed, and it was not a good sound, and it was the sound of a man who has already been frightened by this and has been frightened alone. "Where is the fire."

"Keep them open. Keep them open, don't make a fist." Marek put both of the man's palms down on his own knees. "Somebody send a runner to the clinic. Sera Holt, and tell her vigor, tell her it was two days over."

Two men had Rezek up off the floor by then and were half carrying him, and Rezek was protesting that his men were on the fuel line, and somebody said his men were on the fuel line, and somebody else said his men were on the fuel line and he was on his back in a hall full of clerks.

Then it was happening, all of it, at once, in the way that rooms do. The runner went. The queue was pushed back and then forgot to be a queue. The pension clerk and the season-vaults man stood about looking sorry. The woman who had cried was the only person who said anything else, and what she said was: "You'd tell them. That's all. You'd tell them what it cost."

"I will," Marek said.

Nobody believed him. He could see that they did not believe him, and he understood that they were right not to, because he was a clerk on a floor and the promise had cost him nothing to make.

Rezek went out on a door-board between two of his own men, and the rain took him back into it, and the copy room began — slowly, badly, the way a room does after something — to be a room where clerks worked.

Marek sat on the edge of the desk with the receipt in his hand, and then he went down to the bakery on the half-landing, because the noon bell had gone an hour ago and he had not eaten and there was bread in the basket and it is not a thing a clerk can think clearly on an empty stomach.

The woman at the counter took his money.

"Your usual," she said.

Marek stood there.

There was a bakery on the second landing of the Tally Stair and he had gone to it every working day of the last four years, and he had gone at the noon bell, and he could not have said, if a man had asked him at the door, whether he had bought bread, or bread and cheese, or bread and cheese and a pickle, or whether he had gone up the half-stair at the noon bell and come straight back down again without stopping at the counter at all. The parcel was in his hand. He had a parcel in his hand. He had no idea what was in it.

"Your usual," she said again, and put the parcel in front of him, wrapped in paper and string the way it was wrapped every day of his life, and Marek said thank you and carried it back up to the bench and put it on the corner of the desk where nobody would sit on it.

It was not the headache. He understood that with a clarity that frightened him more than the pain did. A headache is a thing you can hold. This was a hole with a shape in it, and the shape was the shape of an ordinary thing he had done four hundred times, and it was gone, and it was not coming back, and nothing in this building would ever put it back, and no one in this building would ever know it was missing unless he said so.

Marek sat on the edge of the desk and put his other hand over his eyes, because the light had gone yellow at the edges and the middle of the room was the only part that would hold still.

It passed. He let it pass. He had a headache already, which was a headache he had had since the second bell and had been assuming was about the four petitions from Rainmarket.

Then he did the audit, because the receipt was on the desk and the audit was the job.

He read the fourth clause again and found nothing wrong with it. He read the running account and added it up twice and found nothing wrong with it. He read the margin initial and compared it with the fuel-yard second's signature on the sealed terms and found that it was the same hand, made at the same sitting, in the same ink.

Then he turned the receipt over, because a receipt that cites a schedule is not a valid receipt until the schedule is read against it, and that was the part of the third reading that nobody in that room had watched him do, and he had done it, on the tenth day of the rains, at the fourth bell, in the year 412, and it was the only useful thing he did that day.

The schedule came off the back of the receipt. A single folded leaf, wax-stitched at the corner, headed in the levy office's brown hand: *Labour schedule, Tallow Ward assessment, season 412.* Eleven lines. Four columns: household, days, surety, witness.

Line one: the fuel yard. Bohdan Rezek, forty-four days, *eleven days a season to the end of the following season.* Surety: a name. Witness: the fuel-yard second.

Marek read the surety column twice, because there was something in it he was not seeing, and then he read it a third time without the other columns, the way you read a single line of a table when you suspect the arithmetic.

Rezek. Rezek's forty-four days. Rezek's surety: *Rusk, P., of the ward school, Tallow Gate* — and the witness column: blank.

He read the whole column down. Eleven lines, eleven sureties named, eleven witness cells empty, every one of them the same shape. On the ninth day of the rains he had countersigned eleven receipts at that counter-desk, and he had read every one of the eleven totals, and not one of those totals had had a line in it like this.

He had a bundle of labor schedule in his hand with eleven sureties on it and no witness anywhere on the page, and every one of those sureties had been entered by somebody as though a surety were just a thing you wrote down, and one line in particular, one of eleven, was a schoolmistress who had stood in a yard and read numbers out loud to children's mothers every day for nine years, and her name was in a cell on a page that the ward had never been given, under a word that meant *a person whose future is answerable for this*, and she had never been asked.

It came back on him in the middle of the copy room, the way a thing does. Two names, and the file had one of them.

He was aware that he had stopped breathing. He started again.

Eleven sureties. And there were eleven lines in the ward's roll and four yards, and every household in the assessment had exactly one surety attached to it, and not one of the eleven had been asked, and the person who had written those eleven names into a surety column without asking them had done it in a fair, fast, confident hand that was not Hesper's and was not his.

The file that contained the schedule was in the unposted rack. It had been in the unposted rack since the sixth bell on the ninth day.

Marek looked at the door of the supervisor's room.

Then he looked at the schedule for a long time, and then he copied it.

Not onto Tower paper, and not into any book, and not into the petition book, which was evidence. He copied it onto the back page of his own book, in his own hand, small, with the column headings and all eleven lines, and he did it in about twenty minutes, and he checked each name against the wax stitch and did it again, and when he had it he folded the page in four and put it inside his coat, against the little book of promises, where the arithmetic from the night before was already.

He did not tell Hesper. He had a document in his hand that showed his supervisor's assessment was false-witnessed on eleven lines, and the supervisor's room was eleven steps away, and he took the document home instead.

At the sixth bell, on his way out, Hesper Rann caught him at the top of the stair with his coat already buttoned and the day behind him.

"You look as though the floor did something to you," Hesper said.

"Rezek drew sixteen of fourteen. I granted the wet-weather rider at the counter. He went down on the floor."

"Mm. And?"

"And the clinic has him. Two days over. It's a rider, Hesper, and I signed it, and if there's anything wrong with the rider I want to know tonight instead of at the hearing."

"Nothing is wrong with the rider. The rider is administrative. You granted it correctly, you granted it inside your own authority, and you wrote yourself into the margin, which is more than most men do." Hesper took a folded paper out of his coat and looked at it, not urgently, the way a man looks at a thing he has already decided about. "The ward entry is complete. It wants one thing and one thing only and I want it off my desk tonight: the certificate of posting, so that the assessment is a posting instead of a draft in the morning. The certificate needs a third-grade countersignature and the clerk of the day for Tallow receipts is a party to the matter as of this morning, which the posting desk was very good about reminding me of at the fourth bell."

Marek stopped in the stair.

"So route it," he said.

"Route it to Sedge. He's on until the second bell and he's got a clean week and he will sign it without asking a single question, because it is a one-page certificate, it says that the entry has been before the desk and appears complete, and it says nothing about the roll and nothing about the notice, and it will be exactly as true in his hand as it is in yours." Hesper's tone did not change at all. "And the ward's water will be posted for the peak night at first light, which is what the ward wants, and the ward's file will have a certificate on it, and in nine days none of this will matter to anybody except four hundred people who will have water."

"And in nine days," Marek said, "the eleventh surety on page three will still not have been asked."

There was a silence on the stair.

It was not a long silence. It was the length of a man deciding how much of a thing he is going to acknowledge, and Hesper Rann chose the whole of it, and looked at him with a mild and slightly tired interest, and said:

"What page three?"

The stair was stone and it was wet from people coming in out of the rain and there was nobody on it, and Marek stood on the turn of it with his hand on the rail and felt the exact shape of the trap he had walked into, which was that he had said a number out loud in front of a man who had written the number on a page, and there was no procedure in the building that could undo four words in an empty stairwell.

"An attachment," Marek said. "A schedule under the levy. Four pages in the ward's copy and five in the file. I read the roll at the Threshold this morning as a party and I am not permitted to enter it, and I have read what I am permitted to read, and page three is the page that says who stands behind the days, and the ward has not been given it. That's all I said."

"That is all you said," Hesper agreed. "And it is a very good answer, and it is an answer to a question I did not ask, which is a thing you do very well indeed, Marek, and I taught you nothing about it, which I have always thought." He put the certificate back inside his coat. "It goes to Sedge. Sedge will sign it and the water will be posted. Go home. You have been down on a floor today, and you are young, and you have a mother to feed."

Marek went down the stair.

On the second landing, in the window, he stopped, and he took out the receipt, and he read the fourth clause again under the wet glass with the rain going past on the other side, and it was exactly as legal as it had been at the counter, and the two days were exactly as legal, and there were eleven names in a column and no witnesses on the sheet, and at first light Hesper Rann was going to have a certificate of posting in front of a clerk, and the only question left in the building was which clerk signed it, and he knew the answer, and he had known it since the fourth bell.
