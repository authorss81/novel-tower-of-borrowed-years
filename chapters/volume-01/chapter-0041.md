# Chapter 41 — Nobody Is Sworn

The room was full at half past the seventh, which is when the yard worked out that it had better be, and the eighth bell was called on time, and the rain was not doing anything at all.

It was built for thirty and it held thirty, and the thirty were: a table of five at the wall, seven ward people in the front four rows of the body of the room, and eighteen more behind them, and there were about three hundred and forty people in the corridor and on the stair outside the door who had come up the hill in the night because a notice nailed to a board at a tank in a yard is the only way a ward is ever told anything.

Bel Harrow had the leather case open on the table and a sheet of his own paper beside it and no clerk, because there was no clerk, and he said so at the top of the morning in about nine words.

"I serve the minutes in this hand, and this hand is not good, and no office in this city will let a clerk serve the record of a body it is a party to, so from this morning nobody is to ask me for a copy of anything and everybody is to ask me for the reading of it instead, and if I read a thing wrongly you will all know because you will all have heard it."

Renata Vole had a form in front of her with a case number on it and did not look at it.

"Before the board sits," she said. "I have three points and they are short and two of them are conceded and I am only going to argue about the third."

"Deputy."

"The board may sit. The board may record. I have no objection to a room." She squared the form. "One. A finding certified by this office on the fifteenth of the rains is not appealable, and a returnpath requires a living holder of the obligation, and the holder of this ward's obligation is a schedule with eleven names on it and no witness against any of them. There is no appeal in front of this table and nobody at this table should spend the morning pretending there is. Conceded, and I would like it in your own hand rather than mine.

"Two. The matter was enlarged on the thirtieth without asking any party. I put that in my own finding because it is true. Conceded.

"Three. The ward's account." She looked up, and she looked at the four rows of the ward and not at the table. "A ward cannot be sworn. It has never been able to be sworn. There is no form in this city with a box in it for Tallow Ward and there has never been one, and that is not an injustice, it is an arithmetic, and the arithmetic is that a thing that cannot be sworn cannot give evidence. What the ward has is documents, and the documents are in this file, and the documents can be read. I am willing to have every one of them read aloud in this room, in front of the people it is about, on the record, and I am willing to do it this morning. What I am not willing to do is sit at a table for two days while four hundred people who cannot be sworn talk at it in turn, and have that entered in a finding as the account of a ward."

Harrow wrote something down and did not read it back, and then he looked at the front four rows for a while.

"Who is the person who told you that," he said.

"That a ward cannot be sworn?"

"That a ward cannot be sworn. Not this morning, in this room. Those are different sentences and one of them is about the law and one of them is about what a law is for."

"It is the standing orders of this office and it is nineteen years old."

"It is," said Harrow, "and I am going to rule on it, and I am going to rule on it in the way I rule, which is that I say the reason out loud first and then I read the ruling after, because in four years of March work I have never once been asked for a ruling without the reason and I have never been given one and it has cost me about four years."

He put the pen down.

"A ward cannot be sworn. That is true and it is why nobody in this room is sworn, and I want the whole of the room to understand what has just happened, because it has happened because of the ward and not in spite of it. There is no form on which a warden is sworn — and a ward is a district, a district cannot speak, and a person who cannot be sworn cannot be sworn to anything, and I am not going to have this table do by hand what the city cannot do by form. So the Deputy is right on the law and she is wrong about the consequence, and the consequence is this: if a ward cannot be sworn, then this board cannot take sworn evidence from anybody either, and I am not going to sit here for two days hearing four people give unsworn statements and a fifth person's answer pinned over the top of them."

"Mare—" somebody in the corridor started, and the door shut.

"So the ruling." He turned a clean page over. "Nobody in this room is sworn. Every person in this room is heard as a person, in their own name, and a person may answer or may decline to answer, and a declining is written down as a declining and nothing is made of it, which is the third term and which was carried three to one on the ninth and which I am going to use about four hundred times before lunch. And because no witness is sworn, no answer binds anybody but the person who gave it. The ward's account will be seven accounts, and the ward's account is not a party's account, and Mr Kern is not going to be able to stand up there and speak for four hundred people, and I am sorry, Captain, and I have looked for a way round it since the ninth and there is not one and I would rather tell you at the start of two days than at the end of one."

"Then who is going to be examined," said Aldis Kern.

"You will be. As a person, in your own name, in the fifth seat, and your answers will bind you and not the ward, and the Trust's will bind the Trust and not the ward, and in about an hour this room will have eleven people in it who are each liable for what they said and nobody who is liable for what anybody else said." He looked at Vole. "Deputy, do you object."

"I object to the word *liable*. It suggests these people are in danger."

"They are not in danger of anything except being wrong, ma'am, and that is the ordinary condition of a person who has been asked a question out loud."

---

The second war of the morning was about chairs, and it was shorter, and Renata Vole won it.

"There are twenty-five places in the body of this room," Harrow said, "and there are three hundred and forty people outside the door, and the office has three officers in it who are not members of this board, and I am going to rule that they stand."

"Mr Harrow—"

"I am going to rule it and then I am going to give you the reason and the reason is the reason you have spent nine years being right about. A member of this board is a member and not a witness. An officer of the office of the Deputy Settlement Steward is an officer of a party and is not a witness. And a person who is neither a member nor a witness is not a person in this room, he is the office, and the office has a chair at this table, because you are sitting in it." He put his hand flat on the table. "So the supervisor of the settlement desk, the district accountant, and the levy clerk will stand at the back, and they will be called when the board wants them, and when they are called they will come to the front and answer as persons, and while they are answering they are not officers of anything, and when they have finished they will go back to the wall. Deputy, that is your office standing at the back of a room for two days, and I would like you to look at it while it happens."

"I have looked at a good many things in a good many rooms," said Renata Vole, "and I did not know this morning that it could be arranged."

Grale went to the back. Hesper Rann went to the back, and did it slowly, and did not look at the table on the way, and stood with his back to the wall about nine feet behind a young hand of the Tanner's yard who had asked to be let in and had been let in because he is nineteen, and who was to spend two days in a chair hearing the inside of a file.

And Marek Vale stood at the back of a room that held thirty, with his coat on, because there was nowhere in it to put a coat.

---

What the room heard first was not the ward. It was Renata Vole's documents, and she produced them herself, in a bundle, in order, and gave them to the Chair, and the Chair read the list of them into the record, and it took four minutes, and it was the first document Marek Vale had heard read in a room since the twelfth of next month and it was nine words long.

*Item one. The ward settlement entry of the ninth of the rains, with the schedule cited at its fourth clause and the standing reserve recited at its third.*

That was all she said. One item, and the item was a name.

Harrow looked at it for a moment.

"Deputy, you have given this board one item and told us the rest of the file exists."

"The rest of the file is in the office and the office is a party and a party does not read its own file into a room where its own officer is standing at the back of it." She said it without any heat. "I have given you the instrument. I have not given you the arithmetic, because the arithmetic is four hands and the board recorded all four on the ninth and adjudicated none of them, and I am not going to be the fifth."

"Then what is this morning," said Harrow.

"This morning is the account of the ward, which you have just ruled is seven accounts, and I have said I will hear every document in the file, and the order is mine, because the file is mine." She put her hands flat. "Or the board takes the account first and the instrument at the fourth bell, and I will say no to that as well, and I will say why, and the reason is that an instrument read before an account is a document read to a room that has not yet been told what it is about, and this office has spent nineteen years reading documents to rooms and I am not spending one in a room where the ward's families are in it."

Milan Vray, in the second row, said, "We are all right with that."

"Captain," said Vole, "you are not a party and you may not tell this board what it is right with."

"Then I am a person," said Vray, "and I am right with it, and that is all I have got and it has taken me a month to have it."

---

They went to the account at the ninth bell, and it went in the wrong order, and it took until the second bell of the afternoon, and Marek Vale stood at the back of a room and listened to seven people describe the same six days in six different orders and understood that this was the shape of the thing and that there was no other shape available to them.

Rezek went first because he was the oldest, and he described a hand.

Ostrow went second and described a kitchen, and two hundred children, and a ladle.

Vray went third and described water on a road and a boy's leg and the fact that he could not tell anybody about it.

Rusk went fourth and would not describe anything at all for eleven minutes, and when the Chair asked her a second time she said one sentence about a term, and the sentence was that a school year is a fixed sum and a yard repair is a variable one and the office has never once been the place where a person was told anything.

Milena Kelb went fifth and said her son's name and did not say anything else for half an hour, and Hesper Rann stood nine feet behind her with his back against a wall and his hands at his sides and did not move at all.

The young hand of the Tanner's yard — Janko Dobre, nineteen, who had said his name out loud at the door and had been surprised that a man wrote it — said that he had carried a storehouse door up a cellar stair on the fourteenth with two other men, and that the stair was not the point, and that the cellar had a stock of condemned tar in it that nobody in Tallow Ward had known about until it was under nine feet of water, and that the water office holds no record of condemned ward property because condemnation is not its business, and that he was not able to say how much it was worth and had asked three people and got three figures.

And Ondra Kelb went last, and said that he had been at the counter-desk on the afternoon of the ninth with a pencil because the pen had not come, and that the totals were correct, and that the room could have that from him in his own hand or in a clerk's hand and he did not care which.

The Chair let him finish and then asked him one question, which was not about the ninth.

"Mr Kelb. Why did you come."

"Because my name is in a book," said Ondra Kelb, "and my mother has said it twice in a room and nobody has looked at me, and I have run out of other reasons."

---

Renata Vole asked her question at the fourth bell, and she asked it of the table and not of the ward, and it was the only question asked that day that had been in a script before the day.

"Captain. Does the ward accept that it received two hundred and eighty hundreds of pints of water it was not lawfully supplied, and that the value of that water is chargeable to it, whatever the cause of the supply was."

The room went quiet in the particular way a room goes quiet when four hundred people who are not in it have made the same noise at the same moment somewhere below.

"Yes," said Aldis Kern. "I have said it in a yard twice. We pay for the water we took. We do not know where it came from and we are not going to be told, because the account that would tell us was heard on the twenty-eighth and it is still open and the terms the Trust offered on it lapsed at the fourth bell of the fourth and were never re-offered. So the ward pays two hundred and eighty hundreds of pints of water and does not know the name of the man who held the valve, and if this room wants to make that an admission it is one, and I have made it twice and I will make it a third time."

"And does the ward accept that the four named households at the foot of the roll are the households that pay."

"No," said Kern. "I have four hundred people outside that door and I have asked forty-one of them, one door at a time, in three days, and I have twenty-nine answers and three no's and the three no's are on a board in a yard in a hand I did not write. I did not come here to sign anything. I came because a man in a good coat came into my yard and said the size of what he had brought out loud, and he was right about the size of it, and that is the whole of what I have got."

"Then this board has no bill to move," said Vole.

"You have a bill," said Aldis Kern. "It is two hundred and seventy-nine hundreds and forty pints and a schedule of eleven households with no witness on any of them, and my people will work it off for the ones that will work it off and the three that will not are the three that will not, and if you want their share on somebody else's account you are going to have to be the one who says so, in your own hand, in a room, and I am going to stand here and let you."

"It will go in my own hand," said Renata Vole, "in a form, in a room, and it will be correct, and I will not enjoy it."

The Chair called the recess at the fourth bell, and the room emptied into a corridor that had three hundred and forty people in it, and did not empty at all.

---

At the end of the recess Harrow stood up and said the thing that had been sitting in the room since the eighth bell and had not been said by anybody with a standing.

"I have no clerk for this board and I want the record to show that I asked, in the room, at the eighth bell, and that no person in it could tell me."

He waited.

"The Tallow file was assigned on the ninth of the rains by a letter in the supervisor's hand, to a third-grade clerk, and that clerk countersigned a certificate of posting on the eleventh, and the settlement desk's standing practice requires a third-grade clerk of the day to be present at a recount, and the recount was sat on the twelfth of next month, and the clerk of the day for it was the same man." He looked at the back of the room, where a suspended clerk with a coat on had been standing for five hours against a wall. "I am going to ask the room whether anybody knows the name of the clerk of the day for the Tallow file."

Nobody said anything for about four seconds, which is a long time in a room.

Then Renata Vole said, from the table, in a completely level voice, "Mr Marek Vale. He is suspended. He is in the room. He is not a member, he is not a party, he is not a witness, and he has been standing at the back of my hearing for five hours with a coat on, and I have not once looked round, and I am telling you that now because I am not going to be the man who finds out."

Marek Vale came four steps forward into the light of a window in a room built for thirty, and three hundred and forty people in a corridor heard a chair go over on the other side of a wall.

"I am the clerk of the day," he said. "I have been since the sixth of the rains. I certified the posting on the eleventh and the ground of my suspension is that paper, and I have not read it into this room and I am not going to, because it is not mine to read and because there is a woman here who may ask me about it tomorrow and a man at the back of this room who wrote the paper it is, and both of those are in front of me, and neither of them is going to get anything out of me today that I have not already written down somewhere and been unable to produce."

"And will you produce it," said Bel Harrow, "if the room asks."

"No," said Marek Vale. "There is no form on which a suspended clerk is a party, so the room cannot ask, and the paper will be in the record on Saturday whether I ask for it or not, because the room has already decided what the record is and it does not need my help."

He stopped there, in a room that held thirty, in front of seven people who had come up a hill in the night to be in it, and he had said one true thing and had not been thanked for it and had not expected to be, and the recess was over.
