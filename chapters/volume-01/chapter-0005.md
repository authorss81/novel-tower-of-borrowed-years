# Chapter 5 — Four Names, Eleven Households

The posting desk's query came up to his bench at the first light of the eleventh day of the rains on the back of the certificate itself, in the hand of a woman who had been copying since the first bell of the Reckoning and had run out of room.

*Certificate of posting, Tallow Ward water assessment. Countersignature required by the third-grade clerk of the day for the district named. Clerk of the day, Tallow: M. Vale. Note entered at desk: clerk of the day is entered as a party to the matter. Does clerk certify? Y/N.*

Marek read it twice. He had been awake since the fourth bell, which was when the rain stopped for ten minutes and then came back harder, and he had eaten at some point on the previous day and had no memory of it beyond a parcel in his hand, and the headache from yesterday had gone and left something behind it that sat at the base of his skull like a coin in a socket.

He read it a third time because he wanted to be very certain there was no second question in it.

There was no second question. The whole of the difficulty of the previous day had been compressed by a clerk who did not know what she was compressing into the two letters at the end, and the compression was exact, and it was the shape of a door with one handle.

He looked across the room. Hesper's door was shut. The light under it was on, which meant Hesper had been up since before the first light, which meant Hesper had sent it to Sedge and Sedge had sent it back, or Hesper had not sent it to Sedge at all and had spent the night on a different, more patient plan, and either way Hesper was in there with a posting that would go on a board or would not.

Marek took out the certificate.

It was one page. That was what Hesper had said on the stair, and it was true, and it was the whole of the trick of it. One page, and it did not mention the roll, and it did not mention the notice, and it did not mention page three, and it did not mention the eleven sureties or the eleven blank witness cells or the woman in the queue who had cried on a stone floor about the tar.

It said:

*The assessment in the case of Tallow Ward water, season 412, having been before this desk in full, and appearing complete in its need, pledge, scope, witness, and settlement, is certified as posted, and the settlement falls due on the fourteenth day of the rains at the peak.*

A settlement is not a bill. A bill is money, and a bill can be queried, disputed, appealed, paid late, or forgiven by a fund. A settlement is an event: a date at which the future consequence named in a contract comes due. The contract either has a named party who owes that consequence or it does not, and it cannot be argued with once it has been certified as posted, because the argument about whether it should be posted is an argument made *before* the posting, and the posting is the end of the arguments.

Before it is posted, the assessment is a draft, and any party may be noticed and may say anything. After it is posted, it is due. And a draft becomes a posted assessment on one page, with one pen stroke, by a third-grade clerk, whose entire function in the building is to be the person who is there.

Marek's hand was not shaking. He noticed that with a kind of cold disappointment. He had expected some part of himself to make this difficult for him and instead it sat in his chair and got on with it, the way it had got on with the wet-weather rider at the counter yesterday, and he understood then, and with a clarity that stayed with him for a long time afterwards, that he was extremely good at this and that this was the problem.

He took out the Tallow case from the unposted rack.

Hesper came out of his room while he had it open.

He did not ask. He put his thumb on the entry where the settlement date went, and he wrote it in his own hand — *fourteenth day of the rains, at the peak* — and he initialled the line, and he wrote the hour beside his own initial, and then he put the case down and squared it to the edge of the bench with two fingers, which he had never once done in four years.

"That is the posting endorsement," he said. "The date is the supervisor's. The certificate is the clerk's. A settlement is not posted until both are on it and neither of us can do the other's half, which is the only reason either of us has ever been any use at all."

"The labour schedule is in the case behind the entry," Marek said. "It is cited at the foot of the fourth clause. Is that housekeeping?"

Hesper looked at him.

"That is housekeeping," he said. "A schedule is cited by the entry and filed with the entry. Every levy in this hall has a schedule behind it and no party outside the levy office has ever been shown one, because a schedule is not a term, it is the working of a term, and the working of a term is not owed to a party." He said it in the voice he used for things that were both true and not the point. "I have been signing entries for twenty-two years and there has not been one without a schedule behind it and not one party has ever asked to see it. You are not asking to see it. You are asking me to certify that it isn't there."

"I am asking whether it is a term."

"It is not a term," Hesper said, "and you know the difference, and if you did not know the difference you would not be sitting in this hall, and if you did know the difference then you know that a schedule is the working, and the working is not witnessed, because the working does not cost anybody anything until it is done." He went to the door. "Certify the certificate, Marek. Do not try to be more than a clerk this morning. It is not a morning for it."

The door came to.

Marek read the totals. They were in order. The totals were, as a matter of plain arithmetic, correct: eleven households, four payable this season, the wage at the ordinary rate of the district, the whole thing footing to the figure in the ward's roll and the figure in the certified assessment and the figure in the copy the ward had been given on the Wednesday.

The pen was the ninth-day pen. The pencil was the ninth-day pencil. The signature and the date of the drawing were the ninth day's. The date of the settlement and the initials beside it were this morning's, in a different ink, because that ink was wet.

He put the case on the bench and laid his hand flat on the first leaf, the way he had laid his hand flat on Rezek's receipt at the counter yesterday, and it was not a decision, it was a habit, and his palm was already on the case when the pain came up in his temple and the page went flat and quiet.

The lines were where they were. The need: the ward's four yards supplied to the standing schedule through the high water, scope named to the Tallow Gate ward line and the four yards and no further, which was a good scope, tight as a boot, and he checked it and there was nothing in it that had no business being there. The pledge: future recovery of the eleven named households, in the forms set out, at the ordinary wage of the district. The witness: the ward's captains, the Trust levy office, and a Crown Terrace water-clerk whose name he did not know and whose statement was in the file and correct. The settlement: the fourteenth day, at the peak, in the sum certified.

The chain ran down the foot of the leaf in a clean unbroken line, nine signatures in order, and every one of them was a hand he could have described.

> The levy names eleven households. The posting names four.

Then the page went back to being a page.

He stood there with his hand on the case and waited for the thing he had been waiting for since the sixth bell of the ninth day, which was the feeling that the thing in front of him was telling him something. It did not come. The headache stayed at the base of his skull and thinned, and the copy room went on being a copy room, and forty people came up the stair past his bench and looked at the two of them, him and the case, and went on.

He read the file again by hand, all of it, slowly, with the schedule in his coat against his chest.

The entry was clean. He understood the entry and its author and the four minutes he had spent on a wet stair, and the thing the entry wanted from him was a sentence about the need, the pledge, the scope, the witness, and the settlement, and the sentence was true.

Here was the whole difficulty, and he could not have said it aloud if his life had depended on it, which is how he came to understand it a week later.

There were two things wrong with the entry, and one of them he could see and one of them he could not, and they were not the same size.

The one he could see was a page that was not in the entry. A column, eleven lines, eleven sureties, eleven empty witness cells, in a hand that was not Hesper's. The entry had not claimed to contain that page, and the omission of a material term is not a sentence. You cannot point at a page that is not there and say the page lies. You can only say the ward was not told, and the ward's bill is a bill about water, and the ward has four hundred signatures in an aisle at the Threshold and does not know that forty-four days of fuel-yard work is being asked of a foreman whose hands closed up on a stone floor on the tenth day of the rains.

The one he could not see was a single recital in the fourth clause, and it read: *the whole of the sum is offset against the standing reserve of the water office, and no household named in this assessment is drawn upon for the season.*

He had read that recital four times and it was a beautiful piece of drafting, and it was also a claim about money in a place where a third-grade clerk at a counter-desk has no standing to look. A reserve is not a figure. A reserve is a line somewhere else, and a footnote under the recital sent it somewhere else, and the somewhere else was a sealed case in the Season Vaults behind a signature nobody was permitted to erase. Nobody could audit that line at a bench, and that was not an accident of the hour. That was what a recital is for.

And the certificate on the desk did not say that the entry was true. It said that the entry had been before the desk *in full* and appeared *complete*, and every word of that was a word about a piece of paper on a table, and not one of them was about the Silt, or the Gate, or a foreman's hands, or a woman at a Tallow Gate school with her name in a column and no signature under it.

The ward had had its copy of the roll since Wednesday. The ward's copy had four pages. The ward's copy was the ward's notice. And by the time anybody got to the schedule the peak would be over and the posting would be the fact of the world.

Marek picked up his pen.

He thought about his mother's line on the witness page — *I am signing anyway* — and about the woman on the floor of the copy room saying *you'd tell them, that's all*, and about Sedge signing it in the dark without asking a question, and the entry going on the board for the fourteenth, and the ward not knowing until March.

And he thought, and here was the whole of it, and he was a man who would be able to be honest about this for the rest of his life, which did not make it any less the mistake:

If he signed it, then it was posted, and a posted settlement is due on the fourteenth, and a settlement that is due has a named party who owes it, and a named party who owes it is a thing that can be *seen* — seen in a room, by a hearing, by a board, by a person who is asking in public who the party is. If Sedge signed it in the dark, then it was posted, and the ward would find out about the schedule at the hearing, or in March, or never, and it would arrive on them in that form, which is the form in which a thing is absorbed.

Signing it did not fix it. Signing it only made it visible.

And the visibility would be his. That was the part he did not say to himself, and it was the part that was true: he would be the clerk who had put his name to the posting, and when the ward came up the stairs with its roll and its page three and its eleven unsigned sureties, there would be a third-grade clerk in that room who had known, and the difference between that room and any other room in the building would be that in that room somebody had a name on the certificate.

He signed it.

Before he did, he took the posting desk's query off the certificate and turned it round, and wrote one word on the answer line in the same small upright hand, and the word was *Yes*, because a query with a blank on it goes back down the desk unanswered and a query with a word on it is an answer given by the clerk of the day in his own hand, and he was going to be able to point at it for the rest of his life, whichever way it went.

He signed it cleanly. He did not hurry and he did not make a show of it. He wrote his name the way he had written eleven thousand names, in the small upright clerk's hand, without flourishes, and he wrote the hour, and he put the pen down in the notch on the desk where it went, and he put the certificate on the posting desk's spike, and the posting desk copied it onto the board in the same hand they copied everything, and the board had one line added to it, and the Tallow Ward water assessment fell due on the fourteenth day of the rains at the peak.

The posting desk's woman said, "Thank you," and did not look up, because there were nine lines of posting to do and the eleventh day of a flood was not a day anybody looked up on.

Marek went back to his bench.

Then he took out Rezek's receipt from the day's up-tray, where it lay with the other countersigned receipts waiting to be copied into the public register, and he unstitched the wax at the corner and took off the schedule, and he put the schedule into the Tallow case, folded once, on top of the entry, and he wrote on the docket strip in the ordinary way:

*Schedule of labour pledges cited by the assessment, filed with the entry to which it is cited. A receipt that cites a schedule is not valid without it.*

Which was true. It was a filing rule. It was in the clerk's manual, in the fourth section, and he had filed eleven schedules that season, and the reason for the rule was to stop a settlement being collected against a promise nobody could produce.

He wrote the note, and he hooked the strip, and he put the case back in the unposted rack, and it sat in the rack on top of the other eleven cases that were not settled and were not urgent, and the posting stood on the board, and his name was on the certificate, and the ward's four hundred signatures were four lines away in the petition book in a sack in a corridor.

He felt, for about a minute and a half, the best he had felt in nine days. There was no other word for it. It was not pride exactly. It was the specific and unarguable sensation of a job being done correctly by a person who knew how to do it, in a building where almost nothing was, and it came up out of him like heat off a stove, and he let it, and it was the last uncomplicated thing that happened to him for some time.

Then he went to the window and looked down the hill at the ward, because the rain had stopped again, and from the second window of the copy room you could see the Tallow Gate from the inside, the low roofs of the kitchens, the black flat of the basin at the fuel line, and the Silt coming up brown and slow and full of the sky.

He got his coat.

The north road above Tallow was the top of a levee, and the span was out at the Tallow end where the freshet had taken the kerb in the spring and Tomas had put a temporary span across the gap on his own money and his own winter, which was the whole reason for the vigor loan, which was in every way legal, and which was now holding eleven days of a fourteenth that had already begun.

He walked up in the rain because the buses had stopped running at the Tallow Gate.

Tomas was on the span with a gang of four, and they were not doing what a gang does on a temporary span in a flood; they were standing about in the way that gangs stand about when the man who tells them what to do is not telling them anything, and Marek came along the top of the levee at a fast walk and got close enough to see the whole of it.

Tomas had both hands on an eyebolt he was fitting, and he had it half up, and the wrench was on the deck.

He got it up. He got the second nut on and started the ring spanner and it went round once, and the second time the wrench came off the nut and he went to put it back and his hands would not close on it.

It was not dramatic enough for a gang to notice. He put his forearm against the rail and got the wrench into his fist with his sleeve and his knee and he got it on the nut and he got it round, and the whole of it took nine seconds, and Marek watched every one of the nine seconds, and Tomas never once looked up.

"Lift, you lazy," Tomas said, to the gang, in a voice with nothing in it at all. "That is a two-inch. Take the weight."

He took the weight. He worked the nut. He got it home, and he marked the wrench with a piece of chalk, and he said, "Good. That is a good nut. Hold that while it comes home."

Lio was sitting on the toolbox at the end of the span with her hood down, because there was nowhere for a nine-year-old to be on a day when the buses had stopped and her father was on the road; she had brought a paper with something drawn on it and she was chewing the end of her sleeve, which she did.

She watched her father work, and she saw nothing, because he had got very good at it in the last month, and Marek stood on the levee at the top of the span and watched his own brother hold a spanner with his elbow and his chin and understood, for the first time, that the family had been running a two-man lie since the spring, and that the only other man who knew was the clerk on the levee.

Lio looked up then.

"Uncle Marek," she said, "why is Dad holding his sleeve over his hand?"

"He's being careful."

"He's not being careful. He held the sleeve over his hand the whole way along. He did it at Ostrow's when the beam fell, and he told me afterward it was the cold." She frowned at the chalk mark on the wrench, the way she frowned at a line in a map that would not do what it was told. "It was not cold. It was June."

"That is a good observation," Marek said, "and you should not make it on a span."

Tomas turned round.

He looked at his brother for a moment with an expression that was almost gratitude and almost nothing else, and he said, in a voice for the gang, "You want to take the other end, Marek, or you want to stand there criticising my daughter in the rain?"

"I want to take the other end."

"Then take the other end."

They took it up together. It was heavy. It was heavy the way a wet two-inch is heavy, and Marek got his arms around it and his feet under him and Tomas got his shoulder under the other end, and they raised it onto the seat and set it, and the whole thing took a minute, and neither of them said one word to the other during that minute, and afterwards Tomas said, "Right," and "Good," and got his chalk, and did not look at him again.

They walked down together in the rain past the bus shelter with the timetable peeled off it, and Lio ran ahead with the paper over her head, and at the mouth of the Sill Road Tomas said, "You did the right thing coming up. The eye is holding. I would have had to take the whole span out on Thursday and I have not the timber for it."

"I know."

"You know what?"

"Nothing," Marek said, and then, because there was one honest thing left in the whole day and he had spent the rest of it, "You don't have to do it like that in front of her. She can see it. She's not stupid, she's nine."

Tomas stopped in the road.

He looked at his brother in the rain, and something in him closed, and he said, in a low voice that Lio, ten steps ahead under her paper, could not hear, "Do you have any idea what you are talking about? Your file. Your five parts. You could not have found the error, Marek, because there is no error, and I want to be very clear with you about that tonight, because I have watched you read that case for two nights like a man looking for a mistake in a *plough*."

"I know there is no error."

"Then leave it alone. Leave it alone and let me have my span." He turned and went on up the Sill Road, and then, without turning round, in the voice he used when he was about to say the true thing: "And if ever you find one, bring it to me before you bring it to a room. That's all. That's the whole of it."

Marek stood in the rain on the Sill Road with his brother going up it, and his chest hurt in a way that had nothing to do with the tar.

The rooms were over the lockkeeper's shop, the lamp was lit in the front one, and Tamsin had her feet under her with the seam on the ripper. Lio put her paper on the table and hung it over a chair back. Marek hung his coat on the back of his own door, and the folded page in the pocket stayed where it was, and his mother put a plate in front of him without asking, and he ate, because he was twenty-eight and there was bread and there was soup and he had not eaten since morning.

Lio got up on the chair so she could see his arm, which she did constantly and without apology, because she was interested in the state of the world.

"Will you push your sleeve up," she said.

"After I eat."

"Push it up."

He pushed his sleeve up, and she took his wrist in both hands and turned it over and looked at it the way a girl looks at a mark on a gate, and there were four shallow notches in the skin of the inside of his forearm, old and white and healed, and they were in a row at even intervals, and they were not the shape of anything that happened to a man by accident.

"Uncle Marek," she said, "why do you have four notches, when Dad's file has four months on it?"

Lio, he would think afterwards, when the whole business had gone into rooms and hearings and public registers, was the only person in Morrowfen who had ever put those two numbers side by side, and she had done it in one sentence at a table over soup, and nobody in the room had understood a thing.

His mother's ripper stopped.

"Go to bed," Tamsin said.

Lio looked at her grandmother. Tamsin did not look up. She drew the ripper through the seam, and the seam came apart, and she said again, in the flat voice she used when a thing was finished: "Lio. Go to bed."

Lio went. She took the paper off the chair as she went, and shut the door behind her with enormous care.
