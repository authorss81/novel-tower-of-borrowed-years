# Chapter 23 — Named Before the Sluice

Kern called a yard meeting for the second bell on the twenty-fourth, at the Gate yard, with all four yards on the boards, and the reason he gave for calling it was the same reason he gave for everything: *because the ward is going to be in a room next week and I am the one who goes.*

Two hundred and eleven people came. The rest were at the Tanner's, the Fuel basin, Ropewalk, or under a roof somewhere being dry, and Kern had a line about that too, and he gave it before he gave anything else.

"Two hundred and eleven out of four hundred in a yard this size, in weather like this, and I want the hundred and eighty-nine who are not standing here to understand that they are not a majority and they are not a minority either, they are a hundred and eighty-nine people who have gone back to work this morning, and that this yard's account tonight is the account of the yard and not of the people who happened to be standing in it when I opened my mouth."

He had a sheet of oiled paper. He had had it since the fifteenth and he had been writing on it in the evenings, and Marek had seen it in his hand at the hearing and had assumed it was a list of supplies, because that is what a water captain's sheet looks like in a clerk's eye.

"You have all come to be told the ward was saved," said Kern. "Wrong yard. I am going to read the twenty-fourth's account of the fourteenth of the rains and the first item is not the sluice, and anybody here who has a difficulty with the order of it can take it up with me after, and I will give you all afternoon."

He turned the sheet round against the tank.

*The account of the night of the fourteenth, in the words of Tallow Ward, kept by Aldis Kern, water-yard captain, sixteen years.*

*Rule of the account: for every thing the open Third Sluice did for this ward, the thing it cost this ward is named with it, in the same breath, in the order in which it happened. The Underbank is named before the sluice. This is the standing order of this account and it is not a matter of opinion.*

"Nobody has ever written it down," said Kern. "That is why I am standing here. Every man in this city has one half of that night and it is the half he was in the yard for, and my half is the half where nothing happened, and I want the other half on the same paper as mine, and I want it in that order, and I want it that way permanently, so that in six weeks when somebody in a Trust room says *the ward was saved*, there is a piece of paper in Morrowfen that says what it cost, and the two sentences are on the same line and cannot be separated without somebody lying on purpose."

He read the first item.

"Item one. The Underbank storehouse at the mouth of the passage, belonging to the yard, used for rope, tarpaulin and salvage, was under nine feet of water on the fourteenth night and came down. It came down at about the second bell of the night, from the roof first. The stock in it is gone. It belonged to Milan Vray and it was worth about four hundred and the yard owes him for it and the yard will pay him for it out of the yard purse at the first quarter, and I have written that down here so that nobody can tell Vray that the yard is waiting on the settlement to decide it."

Milan Vray, who was thirty-nine and rope-maker and had been standing at the back with his arms folded, said:

"Captain. The storehouse was condemned in the spring. I have been at this yard every month since asking to have it off the roll. It has got a slate on it. It is a condemned building and it took my tar and my rope and my salvage and a hundred and forty of my own money of stock that was in there because where else was I going to put it."

"Yes," said Kern. "That is true and I have written it true, and it goes in, in your words, on the line under the item." He wrote for a moment, with the whole yard watching a water captain write. "The storehouse was condemned in the spring and stood condemned through the rains. It is named because it fell."

"And if it had stood up you would not have named it."

"If it had stood up it would not have fallen and there would have been nothing to name. That is not a quibble, Vray, that is what a loss is. But you are not wrong about the slate, and I am not going to leave it out, and I am going to do the thing I do not like doing, which is to make this sheet bigger instead of arguing with you on it." He read out what he had written. "The yard's records show a condemned storehouse on the roll of ward property at the Underbank. The Trust's water office holds no record of the condemnation, because condemnation of ward property is not the water office's business and no water office in this city has ever held a record of anything that was not a pipe. So the night of the fourteenth goes into the record with one item that the office can hold and one item that it cannot, and the item it cannot hold is the one that was standing there legally and had a slate on it. That goes in as an item. That is now the thirty-ninth item and it is about the Trust, and I did not know I wanted it until you made me."

Vray stood there with his arms folded and said nothing, which was, in Kern's opinion, the correct outcome and not a defeat.

"Item two."

And Kern read it in a different voice, and the yard went completely quiet before he had finished the first clause, because they all knew what item two was and every one of them had been waiting nine days for somebody to say it.

"Vasil Ferun, thirteen, went into the Underbank passage at about the third bell of the night to bring out a chest of a neighbour's goods, which is not a thing a boy of thirteen is to be doing, and which I am going to name here because I am not going to have a night in this city where the boy comes out of the water and the account says nothing about where he was. He got the chest up the step. He got himself up the step. The floor came up after him, and his leg is broken at the shin in two places, and it will be mended and it will be walked on and he will be a man with a leg that was broken in a cellar in Tallow Ward while a valve a mile up the hill stood open against a sealed order, and I am going to say those two things in one sentence in front of four hundred people because they are one night."

He looked up.

"Vasil. Come up here."

The boy came up. He was thirteen and thin and he was on a stick and he was not a show, and he stood with his weight on the good leg and said nothing, and Kern waited, and the yard waited, and after a while Vasil Ferun said:

"I don't want it named."

"That is not what this is," said Kern, "and I am going to tell you why it is, and then you can go and sit down. It is not a matter of what happened to you. It is a matter of what there is going to be in this ward's account in ten years, when you are twenty-three and you have a trade and you come and ask this yard for something, and there is a queue. That is the day. On that day it will not matter a jot what you think tonight. On that day you will want there to be a night in this city's papers that a Tallow yard was under water, and it will be because I wrote it down when it was uncomfortable."

The boy said, "It was my fault for going in."

"Yes," said Kern. "And I have written that in as well, in your words, because it was, and because an account that leaves out the boy's fault is not an account, it is a handbill. Thirty-eight items and two of them are yours and one of them is about a slate on a condemned building. That is what an account is. Sit down."

He sat down.

"Item three," said Kern. "The sluice."

And then he read it, and it was exactly the paragraph the ward had heard him say on the causeway on the fifteenth, and the yard listened to all of it without moving, and it was the only item on the sheet that anybody in the yard had any affection for.

*The Third Sluice was held open by the senior Lantern Warden, Ilya Senn, from the second day of the rains against a sealed Crown Terrace order of the seventh. The order is a fact and the breach is a fact. The low kitchens at the Tallow Gate were supplied from that channel from the second night onward. Three hundred people were in them and about two hundred of them were children. The ward was supplied by hand from the second night, by a hundred and forty people, whose names were not taken by anybody and are not in any record in this city, including mine.*

"That is thirty-nine items with the first three of them as read," said Kern, "and I have forty in the sheet and I will read the rest at the second bell of the afternoon for the people who are at work, and the sheet stays on this board and anybody who wants to write a line on it can write a line on it, and if two of you write different lines about the same thing then both lines stand and neither of you is scratched out, because I am not the record and I have never been the record."

He put the pen down.

Then he said the thing Marek had come down the hill to hear and did not want.

"Clerk Vale, you have been standing at the end of my table for four days looking like a man with a form, and you have a form on you now and I can see the shape of it in your coat. So here it is, and I will wait for it. I want this account on a Trust paper. I want it in the investigation. I want the Deputy Steward to have it in her hand on the fourth of next month, and I want it to be in the record of the proceeding, and I want it to be the thing she has to answer."

"No," said Marek.

"Explain it to me, and then explain it to them, because you are the one who is going to have to explain it."

"A yard sheet certified by a Chamber clerk is a document that says the Trust's account of the ninth is incomplete," said Marek. "It goes in the file, and a file that says the account is incomplete is a file in which the settlement can be set aside, and a settlement that is set aside is a settlement the ward does not have to pay, and I would do it. I have been trying to get at that account since the twelfth and you have just given me a lawful way in and you do not want it."

"I do not want it."

"No, I did not think you did, and I would like you to tell me why, in front of these people, because I have been in four rooms this week where somebody in a good coat has told me the ward's own account of the fourteenth would be helpful."

Kern looked at him for a moment, and then he turned round to the yard.

"Because I am not asking the Trust to pay for this," he said. "I am asking the ward to say it. There is a difference and it is the whole of my life and there is not one clerk in this city who can hear it." He turned round to the yard. "If the sheet goes in as a document, then the first thing that happens is a room where somebody says the account is incomplete. And the second thing that happens is that the settlement is set aside, because a settlement on an incomplete account is not a lawful settlement. And the third thing that happens is that the ward's eleven households pay the whole thing instead of the excess, because if the account is wrong then the excess is wrong, and the Rezek household is in the first four and Rezek cannot hold a hammer to his chest. I want the ward to say what it lost. I do not want the ward to be able to say it did not owe it. I have been in this yard for sixteen years and I have signed for a hundred and fifty per cent of what a careful man would have signed for, and I did it every year, and I am not doing it again for a sentence on a piece of oiled paper."

Marek stood in the mud at the end of a trestle table with a form in his coat and understood that the man in front of him had just refused him a document in order to protect a person, and that the refusal was correct, and that he was the only man in Morrowfen who had ever been on the receiving end of it.

"Then what is it for," he said.

"It is for the yard," said Kern. "It lives on this board, and in eleven years somebody will come and ask the ward what the fourteenth of the rains cost, and there will be a piece of paper with the answer on it, in forty hands, with the Underbank at the top of it. That is the entire ambition. It is not a weapon. It is a memory that nobody can revise."

He came round the table and put the sheet into Marek's hands.

"Carry it," he said. "Do not copy it. Do not file it. Do not put it in that case of yours and do not put it in your coat with the rest. Carry it, and put it back on this board when you have finished whatever it is you are doing, and if you lose it I will not have a copy made, because the day this yard has a copy of a thing that is kept in forty hands is the day the thing belongs to somebody."

---

At the last of the light the yard was empty and the sheet was on the board and Marek came back for it, and Kern was standing at the tank with his coat open in the first mild evening there had been since the rains broke.

"Captain. One question and then I will let you go home."

"Ask."

"Your account has forty items and it is an account of a night. I have read it and it is the best document I have ever handled and it cannot go into a room." Marek held it by the corners. "It has no name on it. A Trust account of a night has a name on it — the man who kept the valve, the man who held the settlement, the man who certified the totals. You have written down that a rule was broken and you have not written down whose. Is that because you cannot write it, or because you will not?"

Kern was quiet for long enough that a carter went past the yard mouth.

"Both," he said. "Write it and it is a charge, and I will hand it to a Deputy Steward in a room with a door in it, and in that room the breach is the account and nothing else, and the three hundred people in the low kitchens are a fact that a clerk put in to soften it. Do not write it and the account is not evidence of anything, and it is a memory." He pulled his coat closed. "I have wanted the ward to have a memory for sixteen years. I am not going to trade it for a charge, and I would say the same on the fourteenth if the leg had not been broken. Give the sheet back when you are finished with whatever it is you are doing, clerk. And do not come up here and tell me what you have found out, because I have a yard to run and a hearing to walk into, and if you tell me the answer in a yard there will be two hundred people standing in the wet who have been told nothing by anybody and everything by you, and that is how a ward stops being a ward and becomes a mob with a grievance, which I have seen once, in my father's year, and it did not end well for anybody in it."

He went off up the hill, and Marek stood in the Gate yard in the first mild evening there had been, with an oiled sheet in his hands that could not be produced, and understood that when this ward's thanks finally came — and it would come, and it would come out of this yard — it would have to be carried to a man in a corridor by a person, because a ward cannot walk anywhere and cannot speak and cannot thank anybody, and there were four hundred of them and they would have to send one.
