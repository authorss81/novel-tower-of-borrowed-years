# Chapter 43 — What the Recital Leans On

The sixth bell was called late and the officer called it through the door, and the corridor made a noise about it, and then went quiet in the way a corridor goes quiet when four hundred people have understood that whatever is happening now is the thing they came for.

Bel Harrow had cleared the table. There was nothing on it but his own hand, a sheet, and a pen. The four figures — 640, 440, 279h 40p, 441, on Neris Kade's sheet, in the order they were called on the ninth — had gone to a second table against the wall, where anybody could walk over and look at them.

"Before the instrument," he said. "Mr Rann, you have been standing at the back of this room for eight hours and you have not asked to leave, and I have noticed, and I have decided not to make anything of it. Sit down if you want a chair. There is one and it has a leg."

"I will stand," said Hesper Rann. "I have read documents in rooms for twenty-two years and I have never once read one sitting down."

He came forward, and he did not go back to the wall, and nobody made him, and that was a small piece of administration that took four seconds and mattered for the rest of the afternoon.

"Mr Rann. I am going to ask you a question and I am going to say first, for the record, that I have no right to ask it, and that if you decline I shall write down that you declined and ask it again at the next sitting, which will be on a date I do not control either."

"Ask it."

"Your third clause recites a standing reserve. The fourth cites a schedule at its foot. Somewhere between the two there is a place where the reserve is kept, and it is not in the entry, because I have had the entry in front of me since the eighth bell and it is five clauses and a schedule that is not there.

"Did you read the page it rests on."

Hesper Rann looked at him for a moment with an expression that Marek Vale, standing at the back with his coat on, had seen four times in five weeks and had never once been able to explain to anybody.

"I read it," he said. "On the ninth, at about the fourth bell of the afternoon, in the vault, with a lamp I had to hold in my teeth because the case is a case and not a shelf and there is nowhere in that room to put a lamp down."

"And the first page."

"The first line of it is blank. There is a ruled line and the line is empty, and beneath the line there is a signature in a hand I have not seen in twenty-two years of signing my own name, and in the margin there is a remedy written in the same hand, which is a standing hold held by an office that has not been held since the third century." He said it in the voice supervisors use for a thing that is in the standing practice and will not be argued about. "I did not read it twice. I read the footnote, which is a reference, and the reference is a page number, and the page number is the first page, and I went and looked at it because a recital that resolves to a page is a recital that resolves to something, and it resolves to nothing, and I have known that since the ninth and I have had thirty-seven days to think about what to do about it."

"Did you do anything about it."

"I wrote the clause," said Hesper Rann.

---

He asked to read the entry himself and he gave his reason before anybody offered.

"I wrote it," he said, "and there is not one person in this room who knows what any of my sentences mean, and I am not going to have a Crown March representative with a bad hand read a document I have been signing for twenty-two years. I will read it as I wrote it, and if it does not say what you want it to say, that is what it says."

Harrow looked at Renata Vole, who did not object, and who had said on the sixteenth at the eighth bell that she would have every document in the file read aloud in this room in front of the people it was about, and who was not going to be the woman who stopped the first one.

"Mr Rann is not a witness," said Harrow, and wrote it. "Nobody in this room is sworn. What Mr Rann reads is not evidence and it binds him and nobody else, and it goes in the record as having been read."

"Read it," said Vole.

He read it standing up, holding it at chest height, in the voice of a man who has read the same page to himself for five weeks. It was shorter than anybody in that room expected, and that was the first thing that happened to the room, and it happened before a word of it was said. A ward settlement entry is a sheet of a clerk's language with a plate at the head of it, and it ran to about two hundred words, and by the end of it the people along the two side walls had understood that a thing which had ruined their month was a page they could have understood in an afternoon and had never once been shown.

The first clause named the instrument it answered. The Tallow Gate, the ward line, the low kitchens, thirty days' notice to the parties, and the date the current season's notice expired. He read the date the way a man reads a date he has read a hundred times. The twelfth of the rains.

The second clause recited the emergency, the flood, the shortage, and the schedule.

The third clause was the standing reserve.

He slowed down on it, not theatrically, but the way a man slows down on a sentence he has rewritten more than once.

"*Third. The standing reserve as recited at the foot of this assessment is an account of the water office, is held against the district account, and is honoured as certified, the district's excess of two hundred and some hundreds of pints a night in the period of the rains being carried against it in full; and the parties are noted that the ward's requirement for the peak night is its standing schedule and that the balance drawn at the ordinary wage is a lawful settlement and stands.*"

He stopped.

"That is a beautiful sentence," said Bel Harrow.

"Thank you," said Hesper Rann.

"It does not say what it means."

"No," said Hesper Rann. "That is what a recital is for."

---

Harrow wrote for half a minute and then asked the question that ended the third clause.

"In your office's language, what does a reserve recited in full against an account do."

"It is honoured as certified on the face of the finding," said Renata Vole. "It offsets the charge in full. That is the whole of a recited reserve and I have certified against it four times in nine years."

"Offsets which charge."

"The make-good carried on the district's irrigation account. Two hundred and some hundreds of pints a night, the whole of it, unapplied for the period of the rains."

"Which is the carry," said Harrow. "Forty thousand nine hundred, entered every year since the flood years, unapplied, held against a standing reserve this office has never located."

"And the reserve this clause recites is an account of the water office and is honoured as certified." Harrow put his pen down. "Deputy, I am going to say the next four sentences slowly. A recited reserve is only a fund if there is something to fund with it. You have just told me this one is honoured as certified and offsets in full against a make-good that is carried on an account nobody has ever applied. So when Mr Rann wrote *the standing reserve as recited at the foot of this assessment*, he was not writing a sum. He was writing a name, and putting it against a line on a page that has had forty thousand nine hundred on it, unapplied, since before the flood years, and the two of them together are the reason the ward's assessment looks like it does not have a hole in it.

"The ward does not appear to pay for the gate loan, because a thing that does not exist has been set against a thing that has never been applied. And that is the whole trick, and it is not clever, and it took about four minutes."

"That is a fair statement of the mechanism," said Renata Vole, "and I have certified against it four times in nine years and I did not know that was what I was doing until this afternoon."

"I believe you," said Harrow, "and it is in the record that I believe you, and I would like the record to be able to say that in four years when the person who needs it is not in the room."

---

"Then where is it," said Harrow. "Mr Rann, where is the standing reserve. Not where does it resolve. Where is it."

"It is not in the entry," said Hesper Rann. "The entry recites it. The reserve is kept in the oldest reference volume in the Season Vaults, and the reserve is at the first line of that volume, and the first line is a ruled line, and the line is empty."

"Mr Rann, the Chair is asking you to produce it."

"I cannot produce it," said Hesper Rann, "and I want the reason minuted properly, because I have been the only man in this city who has ever been asked to produce that line and I am not going to do it badly.

"The volume is in a case in a vault on the second floor, and the case is sealed, and the page at the head of it is the sealed founding page of this office, and there is a standing order on it which I have read perhaps nine times in twenty-two years, and it is in two parts. No clerk may erase the signature. And no clerk may copy the page.

"Not one copy. No clerk may copy that page. Not on a longhand sheet, not in a hand-book, not in a book of a clerk's own, at any hour, for any reason, including the reason that somebody in a room has asked for it. I have signed nine of those orders in nineteen years and I have watched four men in this building not be able to tell you what is on it, and not one of the four was a fool, and two of them were senior to me."

"Then the board cannot have it," said Bel Harrow.

"The board cannot have it. I cannot have it. I have not had it since the ninth, and a supervisor of twenty-two years who brought that page up the hill would be doing the single thing the order exists to prevent, and I understood the order on the ninth and not afterwards."

"Then what the Chair is holding," said Vole, "is a recital in a sealed instrument with no copy, against a line of forty thousand nine hundred that nobody has ever applied, and the room cannot look at either of them, and the room is being asked to accept a sum of money that no person in it has seen."

"Yes," said Hesper Rann.

"And you have known that since the ninth."

"Since the fourth bell of the afternoon on the ninth," said Hesper Rann, "with a lamp in my teeth, and about two bells after I had found four people in nine minutes between the Sill Row steps and the Fuel basin."

Nobody said anything for a while, and two hundred people in the corridor could hear that something had happened and not what.

---

"Then read me the footnote," said Harrow. "I am not asking you to copy the page. I am asking a man to read out a sentence from a piece of paper in a room with people in it, and if that is forbidden then this standing order of yours has a hole in it the size of a hearing and I should like to know that now."

Hesper Rann looked at Renata Vole.

"It is not the page," said Vole, after a moment. "A reference is a reference. Every finding in this city cites a page and a note, and neither of them is the page. Read the footnote."

"*The standing reserve is the first line of the oldest register and is not to be supplied otherwise.*"

"That is the whole of it?"

"That is the whole of it, and a full stop, and I have read it four times."

"And beneath the line."

"There is a signature," said Hesper Rann.

"Whose."

Hesper Rann stopped.

Marek Vale, at the back of a room built for thirty, with his coat on and no standing in it, watched a man of forty-six who had read a signature in a vault on the ninth of the rains with a lamp in his teeth decide whether to say it out loud in front of two hundred and eighty people.

"It is not a name," he said. "That is the first thing. It is a mark and a figure and a stroke, in the old hand, and it has been the same for as long as the page has been in the case, and there is no person in this city called it, and this office's standing orders refer to it as the founding hand and do not give it a name, and I have never met anybody who could tell me what the letters are."

"Read the marks," said Bel Harrow. "I have just ruled that a reference may be read. The signature is beneath the line the reference points to, and I am not asking for the page, I am asking for a thing nine people in this room have now told me they have all seen and none of whom can say what it is, and if the Chair's ruling is wrong then the Deputy will tell me and the Deputy has not said a word."

Renata Vole said nothing at all, and that was how it was allowed.

Hesper Rann looked at the table for a moment and then said it, and he said it the way a man says a thing he has carried for thirty-seven days and has not once been asked for:

"A, a stroke, and zero."

The room said nothing.

"A-0," said Bel Harrow. "In writing. Deputy, is that a name, a designation, a form number, an office, a hand, or a person."

"It is the founding hand," said Renata Vole. "That is how this office refers to it. It is a phrase and it is in the standing orders and it has been there longer than the standing orders."

"Is it a person."

"It is a signature." Renata Vole did not look up. "I cannot tell you what a signature is when I have not got the page, and I have answered that question this morning by saying I cannot, and I would ask the board to notice that I have said I cannot in front of a room full of people and not in a corridor, because that is a different thing and I have earned it today."

Sedge, a third-grade clerk of nine years on the intake floor who had refused all month to countersign anything with a suspended clerk's name on it, said, "There is nothing in the Chamber's book about it. I looked. Not to help anybody — it is the only archive in this building I can open without a seal and I wanted to know what a thing was."

Pella Rusk said, "Then what is it for."

It was the only question anybody in that room asked about the blank line, and it was asked by a schoolmistress, and it was the correct question, and there was no answer to it, and Harrow said so in one sentence and wrote it down, and the sentence is in the finding of the sixteenth of next month in the second-f hand of a Crown March representative who has never been a clerk of anything in his life.

"Nobody in this room can tell her," he said. "Not the Deputy, not the supervisor, not the man with a family copy of a hand-book on the Ropewalk. The reference resolves to a page, the page is sealed, the order forbids a copy, and the room is being asked to accept a reserve on the word of a man who has told us he did not look at it twice. I am not going to improve on that and I am not going to pretend this board understands it."

---

The last document of the afternoon was a schedule, and it took four minutes.

"Does this board need the labour-pledge schedule," said Harrow.

"No," said Sedge. "It is cited at the foot of the fourth clause. It was in the file on the ninth and it has been in every copy since. The ward's copy runs one, two, four and five, and the file's runs one, two, three, four and five, and the difference between them is recorded on the permanent record of the fifteenth of the rains, noted and not adjudicated, on the application of the clerk of the day, who is at the back of this room with his coat on."

Nobody turned round, and Harrow did not either, and Marek Vale understood that a man in a good coat had just spent something of his own to keep a fact in a record, and had done it without being asked, and had done it by a rule about procedure rather than by anything about him.

"Read the shape of it," said Harrow. "Not the names."

"Eleven lines," said Hesper Rann. "Four columns: household, days, surety, witness. Eleven sureties named. And the witness column is empty on all eleven lines."

"Read the witness column again."

"The witness column is empty on all eleven lines," said Hesper Rann.

Harrow counted them himself, from the page, in the room, in front of the seven, and it took about forty seconds, and nobody stopped him, and the count was eleven.

"Then that is the ward's complaint in one column," he said, "and I am not going to have this board spend an afternoon on it, because the Deputy set every line of it aside on the fifteenth and she was right to, and I would like it said that she was right to. Eleven sureties with no witness is a page nobody can rely on, including the eleven, and that includes the ward, and the ward has been posting against it since the ninth."

Pella Rusk had not moved from her chair along the side wall.

"Then a surety on that page," she said, "is a person who has not been asked to stand anything."

Nobody answered her, and the room went on for another hour and a half, and at the back of it Marek Vale stood with his hands at his sides and said nothing at all, which was the second time he had given that woman a technically accurate silence in a room with a door in it, and it was in front of more people than the first, and he had got considerably better at it.
