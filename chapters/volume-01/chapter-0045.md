# Chapter 45 — Does the Ward Have Water

The sixth bell was called and Harrow let the room run for an hour and a half, and none of it was procedure. It was the Chair asking questions and writing the answers down in a long hand that got worse as the light went, and the seven along the two side walls answering as persons on their own liability, and a table of five finding out what a board does with an afternoon when it has nothing to strike.

He went down the wall in the order it was sitting in, and he took about nine minutes a person, and the ninth minute of each one was the minute he stopped asking.

It began with Rezek, who put his hands out on his knees and would not open them.

"Sixteen days," said Bel Harrow. "Against sealed terms of fourteen. Granted at a counter-desk, on a rider, in about four minutes."

"Yes."

"Not for this board's reasons. I know why it was granted and it is not in front of me. Tell me what the rider was for."

"The wet." Rezek looked at his own knees the entire time he was answering, and nobody in that room could have said afterwards whether he had looked at the Chair once. "There was a wet-weather rider on the form and I signed under it because it was wet. The hands went on the twelfth and there is no line anywhere for the twelfth. There is no line anywhere for the twelfth."

"Somebody drew it for you."

"Grale drew it. Grale was the counter that day and Grale put my name to the day." He shut his hands, which he had not meant to do, and then opened them again on his knees because the Chair had not asked him to. "I am not blaming a man who did what I asked him to do at a counter-desk. I have said that in a yard and I am saying it in a room because this is the room it is going to be read in."

Harrow wrote for a while and read it back and Rezek said *that is my words*, and it was, and then the Chair moved on, and Rezek put his hands into his coat pockets, which took him longer than it should have.

Dobre was next, and gave three figures for a storehouse of condemned tar in a cellar and then said he could not say and had asked three people, and when Harrow asked him which of the three he believed he said that he believed the cellar and not the figures. And then Milan Vray was asked about a boy of thirteen with a shin in two pieces, and said the Underbank came first and the sluice came second, and Aldis Kern said it had better come first in whatever gets written down, and Harrow wrote it down with the Underbank first.

And at the back of the room, nine feet from the window, Hesper Rann stood with his hands at his sides and said nothing at all for an hour and a half, and not one of the seven looked at him, which was a decision about the yard made at once and without anybody putting it to a vote.

Marek Vale stood at the other end of the same wall and watched a man not be looked at, and understood that he was the only person in the room for whom this was difficult, and that he had no standing to be the only person in the room for whom anything was difficult.

---

The door opened at about half past the sixth.

Two men came in. They were not in uniform, because there is no such thing here, and they were not armed, and they were not old. They were wearing the plain good clothes of the Crown March's administrative staff and they had wet coats on and one of them had been walking fast.

Nobody at the table knew what they were. That was the fact of the afternoon, and Harrow watched all five of them work it out and get it wrong, and Renata Vole got there first, and got it wrong first, and said so.

"Crown March," said the man who had been walking fast. "We were sent for at the recess at the fourth bell. Are you the Chair."

"I am."

"Then I am obliged to say this in front of witnesses and I have a form for it but the form assumes a magistrate and there is no magistrate in this building that I can find, and the Chair is going to tell me whether that is a problem."

Bel Harrow put his pen down.

"It is a problem and it is mine and I am going to solve it in about ninety seconds and I would like the room to understand what I am about to do while I do it, because I am about to do it with the only power I have and I have never used it.

"I am a Crown March representative. I represent a procedure and not a constituency, and my whole office is that I can say a date out loud. My instrument carries five grounds for convening a board. Four of them are about figures. One of them is about a person, and it reads that a question of conduct excluded from a proceeding in which it ought to have been heard is a ground, and this morning I heard a supervisor of the settlement desk explain what a cause line is for, accurately, in front of the seven, and I asked him what action it was capable of, and he told me, and the Deputy told me, and the action has a boy's name at the end of it.

"That is the fifth ground. That is the whole of my office. I cannot order that man taken. I have no power to arrest anybody and I could not do it if I had one, and no board can, because a board of this kind has three things it cannot do and I have read them out in a yard and they are on the ninth's minutes.

"What I can do is hand him to the March. And a hand-over is a thing a Chair does, in a room, with a record, and if I do it here then it is in the record of a hearing in front of the people it is about, and the March can be told that the board heard the conduct before it was taken up, which is the whole of the fifth ground and the reason it is written the way it is."

He looked round the table.

"I am going to rule that this board has heard the conduct. Deputy, do you dissent."

Renata Vole had not moved. She had her hands flat on the table in front of her, one on each side of a closed bundle, and she looked at the two men in the doorway and then at Hesper Rann at the back of the wall and then at nothing at all for about four seconds.

"I have no dissent to make," she said. "I have not had one in the whole time this board has been in existence and I am not going to manufacture one in the last hour of a sitting because there are two men in my doorway. The finding of the thirtieth stands. The cause is entered. The collection is unblocked. Nothing this morning disturbs any of it and I am not going to help anybody say it does."

"That is not a dissent," said Harrow. "That is a witness statement and I am glad to have it."

"It is a Deputy Settlement Steward doing her job in front of a room and it is not a favour to anybody, and if it is put in the finding as a statement I shall object to the word *witness*, because a witness binds herself and I am an office."

"Noted," said Bel Harrow, "and the Chair rules that this board has heard the conduct of the supervisor of the settlement desk in the matter of the Tallow Ward settlement entry of the ninth of the rains, in that the entry carries an invented standing reserve and a material term not served on the parties, and in that a certification of cause was filed on the twelfth of this month capable of being acted on in a manner directed at a person in the counter-desk of this office, and I hand him to the March."

Hesper Rann came forward from the wall.

"Before I go anywhere," he said.

"Sit down, Mr Rann. This is not a room you leave in the middle of."

"I have said the two things I have to say and I am going to say them standing where I am, and then I will say one more, and I am aware that the Chair has said a man may answer and may decline, and I am answering."

He did not turn round, and the seven along the side walls did not turn round either, and the two men in the doorway waited, and Harrow waited.

"The entry is false-witnessed. A material term was not served on the parties and the reserve in the third clause does not exist. Both of those are correct and I am not going to have anything recorded that suggests otherwise, because somebody will read it in four years when the only people left who were in this room are the Chair and a boy of twenty, and the boy of twenty will not be in a position to correct it, and the Chair will be dead or in Crown March, and I have made enough of other people's arithmetic in my life.

"The second certification is a true sentence in a box on a form and I put it there on the twenty-first of the rains and filed it on the twelfth of this month and I knew what it would do to a man at a counter-desk when I wrote it, and I did it because I had decided who was paying the ward and I did not want it to be the four at the bottom of the roll. That is not an explanation. It is the arithmetic and I have never pretended it was anything else.

"And now the third thing, and I want the board to hear it and I want the Deputy to hear it, and I want somebody at the back of this room to hear it, and I have not looked round and I am not going to.

"I have wanted the ward's account to be taken from four named households and set against a schedule that has no witness on any of its eleven lines since the ninth of the rains. I am not withdrawing that. I have been in this room two days and I have seen a page read aloud with eleven blank witness cells on it and I have seen the Deputy set every one of those lines aside in a room in the rains and say that she was right to, and she was, and setting a page aside is not the same as un-posting a ward against it.

"If the Deputy's finding stands, then four households are going to be asked for two hundred and seventy-nine hundreds and forty pints of water at the ordinary wage, and they are going to be asked against that schedule, and a house that will not pay becomes a house that somebody else pays, and the Deputy has told this room in the first person that she will put that share in her own hand in a form in a room and that it will be correct. So it will happen. It is going to happen to the fuel basin or to Ropewalk or to the Tanner's yard or to the Gate yard, and four hundred and six people in this ward will be told it was their captain's water, and I have made sure of that."

He stopped, and the room had gone so quiet that the corridor could be heard through the door, which is a thing nobody had managed all day.

Then he put both hands flat on the table, which he had not done once in two days, and said:

"Does the ward have water."

Nobody answered for about four seconds, and in those four seconds Hesper Rann looked at the left-hand wall, and there were seven people on it, and one of them got up.

---

"I will answer it," said Nadja Ostrow, "because I have been carrying the answer to that question since the second of the rains and nobody has ever asked me for it and every person in this room has been too busy with a page.

"The Gate tank is full. The low kitchens have had water every day of the rains and they have had it because a hundred and forty people carried it by hand off the basin for twelve days and their names were never taken by anybody, including me, and about half of them are standing in this corridor at present because they heard there was a man being taken up here and they came to see whether it was one of the four.

"The Ropewalk standpipe is short and we have been boiling on a ration since the Wednesday and we will be on it again on the Saturday, and that is a fact about a standpipe and not about anybody.

"The water came off the basin by hand and down a channel above our meter and it was not lawfully the ward's, and this ward has said in a yard that it will pay for it at the ordinary wage and has not named the man who let it through, and we wrote that down on a board at the tank and it is still on the board.

"And I am not going to thank him in a room." She said it to the table and not to the man at the back of the wall, and she said it without any heat at all. "I said that in a yard on the twenty-ninth of the rains and I have not changed my mind and I am not going to change it now because there are two men in the doorway. A ward that has been given something by a man is a ward that has been given something, and I have two hundred children behind me every night of that and I am not going to stand in a room and hand him the frame for it on his way out. He never wanted it. I asked him in a yard on the ninth of this month whether he wanted to be thanked and he said he wanted to be left alone, and I have left him alone, and that is the whole of what I have got for him and it is not nothing."

Hesper Rann did not move and did not answer, and after about two seconds he nodded once, at nobody.

"The account was heard on the twenty-eighth of the rains," he said. "The terms this office offered me on it lapsed at the fourth bell of the fourth of this month and were not re-offered, and I want the March to have that, because it is the only thing I have ever been asked for by anybody in this room and I have been given it twice. Do what you like with me. I will give you the four entries and I will give you the plate and I will sit in a room for as long as you want and I will answer everything. I am not going to say I was wrong about the gate and I am not going to let anybody write it down for me either, and if it is in the finding I shall appeal it, and there will not be anything in the finding to appeal."

The man from the March took him out of the room at the second of the seventh bell, and it was not rough, and two hundred and eighty people in a corridor made a noise that was not a cheer, and one of the two hundred and eighty was Milan Vray, who had come up a hill in the rain twice and had said in a yard that he would rather have a roll in a yard than in a book, and who had watched a man walk out of a room he had stood outside of for two days.

Marek Vale was at the back of the room with his coat on and his hands at his sides and had not moved for two days, and he understood, standing there, at about the seventh bell of the evening, that a man in a good coat had sent two men down a stair at the fourth bell of the afternoon and that he had been in the room and had seen him do it and had not known what it was.

That was all he had for it, and it was the same shape as the six bells on the night of the fourteenth: a man seeing a thing happen in a room and being unable to read it, because he had no standing and no paper and no office. There was no form anywhere in the city for a claim by a man against a thing he had seen and could not read, and there had not been one for three hundred years either.
