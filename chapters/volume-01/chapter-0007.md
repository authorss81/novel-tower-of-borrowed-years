# Chapter 7 — Two More Days

The certification came up to his bench at the first light of the thirteenth day of the rains, and Marek read it twice, and then he did not sign it, and then he spent the day in a hole in the ground on the Tallow line with a Warden crew and four of Aldis Kern's men, which was not what a third-grade clerk did on a Wednesday, and which he had already decided to do before he had decided to do it.

The certification was a single sheet headed *Delivery schedule, Tallow Ward, season 412, day of peak* and it certified, for the fourteenth, the ward's lawful requirement at the standing schedule, and the standing schedule was forty pints a head for a worker and thirty for a person who does not work, and a child at the mother's discretion, and it certified that requirement as the whole of what the ward would be entitled to on the night of the highest water in nine years.

Which was a fair number. Which was, in fact, a generous number, and had been written by a woman in a chamber on the island who had taken the district's own form and filled it in honestly and had not once wondered why a ward that had been dry since the fourth day of the rains should be entitled to more water on the fourteenth than it had managed to take out of four yards since the second.

It would have to be signed by the third-grade clerk of the day for the district, and the clerk of the day for Tallow was Marek, and there was a query on it in the posting desk's hand, because Marek had been entered as a party on the tenth.

Sedge had come up at the second bell the night before, on his way home, and had stood at the end of Marek's bench with the certification in his hand and had said: "I can't put my name on this. I am not the Tallow clerk. If I certify a Tallow delivery I am certifying a district I have never audited and I will do it in the dark, and if it is wrong it is my hand and not a mistake of somebody's."

"You are a third-grade clerk."

"I am a third-grade clerk of the intake floor and I have been one for nine years, and there is a rule about the district and I have read the rule." He had put the sheet down. "That is your name on the desk query. It is a party. It wants a decision, and the decision is yes or no, and you are the only man in this hall who can write either of them."

He had gone. And Hesper had been in his doorway at half the second bell with his coat on, and had said nothing at all, which was the most eloquent thing he had ever done, and had gone back in.

So the certification was unsigned at the third bell and unsigned at the ninth, and the ward's lawful requirement for the peak was therefore a quantity that did not exist in any document, and the water that came into the yards on the fourteenth would be water that no office had authorized, and Marek had made it so by not writing two letters.

He took the ward's water account off the up-tray on his way out, because it was on his bench and because the figure in the middle of it was the figure he needed.

Tallow Ward, water, season 412, third week of the rains. The account recorded the junction's metered delivery against the four yard counters, and the second column of the third week was the number that had justified a ration, and it was enormous. It said the ward had taken four times its standing schedule in nine days, and every number in the column was a real number, delivered by a real meter, and it was the meter that was wrong.

He read the account for an hour with a cold cup of the hall's water beside his elbow and found it himself, because he could: the strainer at the second junction appeared in the account as a maintenance line, in the ward's own section, at a sum so small that it was clearly meant to be a rounding, and beside it, in the same line, the payee.

He put two fingers on the payee line and let the headache come up, because that was the thing now, that the price of looking was a fixed fee and he had begun, in a way he did not admire, to budget for it.

The page went flat.

There was no ink on the slate this time. There was a column and a figure in it, and the figure was the same figure, and the number beneath the number was the one he wanted and it was not a warning and it was not a verdict; it was a line in an account, exactly where it was, waiting for a man to read it, and it said:

*Junction second — strainer, screen and bypass, annual upkeep. Account 4-Crown, irrigation. Yard served: Crown Terrace north gardens, which draw from the Bell Weir main and have never drawn from the Tallow line.*

He took his fingers off it, and the account was an account, and he had a headache behind his right eye and a stripe of the desk that would not stay focused for about a minute and a half, and then a stretch of the afternoon he could not account for at all: the light going from the second window to the third and coming back low and gold, his own hand still on the page, and no hour in it.

He thought, quite clearly and with no pleasure at all: it had cost him nothing to find that. It was a payee line, in the account, in plain brown ink, in a column, at the fortieth row, and if he had been prepared to sit with the account for two hours in the morning instead of one in the afternoon he would have found it himself and had the same headache anyway. It had not said one word to him. It had only got to the fortieth row before he did.

Whatever this was, it did not tell him anything. It showed faster.

He copied the line out into his own book, in the ordinary way, with a note of the row and the page, and then he took the account up to Hesper's door and put it through the gap in the door without knocking, and said: "The strainer at the junction second is a Crown Terrace irrigation account. Row forty. If the Tower wants Tallow to be four times its schedule, that is the instrument. It is not ours and it is not the Wardens'."

Hesper took it, read row forty, and gave it back, and said: "Good. That is very good. That is a finding. Take it to the levy office at the third bell and the levy office will tell you it is a Crown Terrace matter, and the Crown Terrace matter will be referred to the Trust, and the Trust will say that a chamber clerk has no authority over a Crown Terrace irrigation account."

"Yes," Marek said.

"Yes. So do you see the shape of it?"

"I see the shape of it."

"Then do what a clerk does." Hesper shut the door. "File the finding. It will be in the record in ten days. The peak is tomorrow."


The second junction is at the bottom of a cutting off the levee, and to get to it you go down a dozen steps made of three different stones laid by three different generations of Wardens, and the bottom of the cutting is about four feet across and about a foot deep in black water, and in the middle of it there is a red iron main with a screen on the elbow and a strainer box beside it and a bypass wheel four feet up on a frame, set the wrong way round.

It was half past the first bell and it was dark and it was raining and Ilya's crew had brought lanterns and two picks and a length of chain, and four of Aldis Kern's men had come down the cutting on their own initiative, which Marek had not asked for and could not have afforded to ask for, and which he was going to be thinking about for a long time afterwards.

"The work is on a Crown Terrace account," Marek said.

"It is," said the old Warden with the picks, who was called Petross and was sixty-one and had been on the Tallow line since before Marek was born.

"Then you cannot do it."

"I cannot do it. I have said so to the water office four times and they have sent me four letters, all of which say the same thing in four different ways, which is that the work is on an irrigation account and the irrigation account is not my account." Petross got down off the step and went to work on the strainer box with a spanner and did not look up. "I have read the letters. They are very good letters."

"Then I am telling you to do it anyway."

"You are a clerk."

"I am a clerk," Marek agreed, "and I have no authority, and there is no work order in my coat, and if anybody asks you tomorrow why the strainer was cleared you will say a Warden's crew cleared it for a Warden's reasons and you will not say my name, and if you say my name I will deny that I said it, and you may rely on that because I have noticed that I am a very reliable liar and I would rather find that out now than in a hearing."

Petross laughed at that, once, and then he stopped laughing.

"You'll want a spanner," he said, "and you'll want to hold this while I turn it, and I am going to tell you that the last man who held it lost a knuckle. Not to the wheel. To the ring spanner. These are the mains of a flood and they are not gentle."

Marek held the ring spanner on the ring of the strainer housing while Petross broke the housing bolts, and the main jumped under his hands twice, and the water came up through the black floor of the cutting and over his boots, and it was cold in a way that is not like cold and that gets into the hands and stays there.

The screen came out at the eleventh hour of the morning. It was a foot and a half across and it was solid, and it was a mat of the things that the basin makes: peat fibre, tar skin, scale off a hundred coats, iron filings, the black slime of a Silt that has been carrying a city's waste since the spring, and Petross held it up on the chain in the lantern light and looked at it for a moment with an expression that Marek had seen once before, on a bridge, in a very different context.

"That is a third of a year of fuel line," he said. "And the next one is behind it, and the one behind that is a year."

It took until the second afternoon bell. The bypass was worse: the wheel was stiff, the spindle was seized, and the frame had to come off its bolts and be turned by four men on a chain, and Marek was the one holding the chain, and it took the skin off his forearm from wrist to elbow, and he did not mention it then or afterwards.

And then the wheel was on its stops and the frame was bolted back and the screen was in and Petross shut the box and put the cover on and said, "Now watch the slate."

Marda Senn was up on the levee with the slate and her hand on the top of the left-hand column, and the numbers had begun to come down.

Not all at once. In ones and twos, over about an hour, the left-hand column walked down the right-hand one, and the right-hand column held, because it was what the yards actually took and it could not be argued with, and where they met the difference column went out of use, and Marda Senn ruled a line through it with a steady hand, and put the date on the line, and said nothing, and four Kern men stood about watching a chalk board which was, in the whole of the ninth to the thirteenth day of the rains, the first good news any of them had been shown.

"The Gate tank fills by the fourth bell," Marda said. "It fills at half the rate it should and it fills. The Tanner's stops falling. Ropewalk gets water at the standpipe — about seventy pints an hour, which is nothing against the standing schedule and is the whole difference between a washday and no washday — and the low kitchens come off the basin by the fifteenth if Petross's screen holds."

"Two days," Marek said.

"Two days of what you have just done, and the peak is the day after tomorrow, and nothing in this city gives you the day after tomorrow. But yes. Two days. And not with a number on a page that is a third of a lie."

Kern found him at the top of the cutting.

"The yard is not going to be dry tomorrow," he said. "I want to say that first, because I have been the captain of this water for sixteen years and I have told this ward what was coming every day since the second, and I have been wrong every day since the second, and today I have been wrong in the direction of a man having water. I have not been wrong in that direction since the spring freshet." He put his hat in his hands. "That is yours. I have thought about it walking up the stairs, and I have decided that I am not going to thank you for it today, because I do not know yet who is paying for it, and I have a rule about thanking people in a flood."

"Someone is paying for it," Marek said.

"I know someone is paying for it. I have been the captain of this water for sixteen years. Somebody is always paying for it." He put the hat back on. "And the bill, clerk. The bill on the fourteenth. Does the fixing of your pipe change the bill on the fourteenth?"

"No," Marek said.

"No," Kern agreed. "No, it doesn't. It never does. That is the first thing they taught me and the only thing I have never got used to, and now go up to the island, because a woman on the third floor has been asking the desk where you are since the first bell, and when a Deputy Steward asks after you, clerk, it is not because she has forgotten."


The Trust's third-floor room was warm and had windows and had a view of the whole basin, and Renata Vole did not offer him a seat.

She was fifty-seven and she had come up out of a career in the water office and had the specific, dangerous calm of a person who can end a thing by saying four words and has spent thirty years learning which four.

"Sit down, Marek."

"Thank you."

"You have been in the ward since the first bell, in a cutting, with a chain. Your coat has tar in it. On the thirteenth day of a flood a third-grade clerk is not in a cutting. I will give you the two reasons and then the one that matters." She put a single sheet on the desk, face down. "The first is that a Chamber clerk has no authority over a Crown Terrace irrigation account and therefore no authority to order work, and if the screen had come out differently I should be writing to the Trust about a clerk who put a Warden's crew onto a Crown Terrace main during a flood."

"It came out as it came out."

"Yes. And the second reason is that Petross Oleksen is sixty-one and his hands are not what they were, and if the bypass had gone over on him he would have lost the arm, and there would have been an inquiry, and the inquiry would have found that a clerk of the Chamber of Accounts had gone out to a junction in a cutting and told a Warden to hold a ring spanner." She looked at him. "You are lucky. Luck is not a resource, Mr Vale, but I will allow that you have some."

Then she turned the sheet over.

It was not the certification. It was a recusal, drafted, dated the twelfth, waiting for his signature: *The Tallow Ward water assessment, season 412, was not assigned to M. Vale, third grade, and no part of it was at any time his to handle.*

"Rann's office is administering the assessment," Vole said. "You would be removed from the file, the certification, and the hearing, and the file would go to Sedge, and Sedge is a good clerk, and Sedge will certify what is put in front of him because that is what a good clerk does. The ward would be certified at the standing schedule tomorrow, and watered, and unlawful by about a third, and nobody in that yard would ever know. You would have made exactly the wrong choice in a way that cannot be looked into."

"And my record."

"Your record will say that the file was never yours. That is what the paper says. It is not a punishment, Mr Vale. Nobody will read it. It is the safest piece of paper I have ever drafted and I have drafted a great many."

She let him read it. It really was clean. It was the cleanest document he had been handed in four years, and it would do exactly what she said it would do, and it would do it with no marks on him at all.

"Why are you offering it to me on the day after the tenth?" Marek said.

"Because on the tenth you were a clerk who had written forty-one petitions in a book, and today you are a man who has had a Warden's crew in a cutting. The second kind of man is always offered something, because the first kind can be managed and the second kind has to be given something or he will go and do the other thing." She put her hands flat on the desk. "And because the file is not clean, and I have read it, and I have known since the ninth day that it was not clean, and I can hold a settlement through the peak with an unclean file in the building. I cannot hold it with you in it."

Marek looked at the recusal for a while.

"You knew it was not clean on the ninth."

"I knew a line had been written into an entry that did not come out of my office. That is a different sentence, and it is the true one, and I have never in thirty years walked into a room and told a clerk what I thought of a document. Hesper Rann has been a clerk in this building for twenty-two years and I will not put that on a piece of paper for a man I have never met."

She reached for the pen and then did not pick it up.

"The hearing on the twelfth is off," she said. "I have cancelled it. The twelfth was a hearing about water, and the water is a fact, and the fact arrived before anybody could sit on it, and I will not sit for two days to confirm a river." She put her thumb on the recusal sheet. "There will be a hearing on the fifteenth at the eighth bell, and I will preside at it myself, and everything in the room will be read out loud, and the ward's own copy and the file's copy will be compared in front of the ward. That is the date. Say no to me now if you are going to."

"So," she said. "Take it, or refuse it, and either way say it now, because I have a settlement to sign in an hour and I would like to know the name I am signing over."

Marek stood up.

"I'll refuse it," he said. "The file was mine from the sixth bell on the ninth and it will be mine at the hearing, and if it isn't then somebody drew it off me and it will be in a file, and I would like to be in the file when that happens."

Renata Vole looked at him for about four seconds, and then she put the pen down flat, and she said: "Then the certification does not go out tomorrow. The ward's lawful requirement for the peak is a quantity that does not exist, which means the water that arrives at that ward on the night of the fourteenth arrives without an authority behind it, and when the settlement falls due there will be no document to point at, Mr Vale, and no document to point at means the shortfall looks for a district, and the district is Tallow, and Tallow is four hundred people and one of them is you."

"I know what a shortfall looks for."

"You know it the way a man knows it who has read about it." She stood, which ended the interview. "Go home. Wash your arm. And be at the hearing on the fifteenth at the eighth bell with your file open, because I am going to preside at that hearing personally, and if you have spent this day building something I could not do myself I had better see it."


He got back to the copy room at the seventh bell, and there was a junior clerk at the end of his bench with a case under his arm and an air of enormous, agonising politeness.

"Mr Vale? I'm Kelb. Ondra Kelb. I'm on the counter-desk this week, for Tallow and the Tallow Gate." He was twenty and had cuffs he was clearly not used to and had been copying for nine days. "There's a case that wants the schedule read against the receipts and the schedule's in it now, sir, so I thought — the desk said to bring it up, and the desk said—"

"It's all right," Marek said. "Put it down. What's your name?"

"Ondra Kelb." He said it again, with the pride of a person who has said it four times today. "I countersigned the ward entry. On the ninth. I did the totals with a pencil because the pen hadn't come. Hesper said the figures were in, and I added them, and they were in." He stopped. "I heard about the cutting."

"That was a Warden called Petross."

"Yes, sir."

Marek looked at him, and there was a moment — a real one, a bad one — in which he considered saying something. *Do not countersign anything. Do not countersign anything at all, ever, not a total, not a sum, not a line, for as long as you live.* And it went through him and out of him in about a second and a half, and what came out instead was: "The totals were in. You were right about that."

Kelb's face did a small, entirely unexpected thing.

"Thank you, sir," he said, and put the case down, and went away up the stair with his cuffs showing.

Marek sat at his bench and looked at the ward file in its slot and the unsigned certification in the tray and the two days he had bought with a chain and a bleeding arm.

Then he went up to the roof stair, which was unlit and cold and where the noise of the Silt is loud enough to cover a man thinking, and he took out the book, and read in his own small hand the words a man cut into a page of the oldest ledger in the tower with the blunt of a knife, four hundred years ago, in a margin nobody was permitted to copy.

*No settlement shall be taken before the year is ended. The remedy is a standing hold. It is held by the clerk of record.*

He read it again.

He knew by then — he had known since the vault, and had not admitted it since — that the office of clerk of record had been retired in the third century, and that nobody held it, and that a clause naming a vacant office could not be used by anybody with standing, and that there was no office left in the building to hold a delay in.

Which meant that if a man in this tower took that duty on tonight, there would be exactly one place in Morrowfen for the delay to go.
