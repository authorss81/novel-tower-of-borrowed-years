# Chapter 14 — A Number of the Boy's

Ondra Kelb found out what his hand could still do on the sixteenth, in a laundry on the next street from Sill Row, while his mother wrung out a sheet and told him not to lift the basket.

He could lift the basket. That was the discovery, and it was not a good one. He got it off the bench with the right hand without thinking about it, the way you get a thing off a bench, and the basket came up and his wrist took it and his forearm took it and it did not hurt, and the sheet in it did not slop, and he stood in the middle of a wet room in a ward of the city holding forty pounds of wet linen in the hand that had lost the years of being able to do anything at all.

His mother watched him do it.

"Well," she said.

"Put it down."

"Put it down yourself."

He put it down. He put it down carefully, on the bench, square, the way you put a thing down when you have decided that the next thing that happens is going to be somebody else's decision.

"That was a basket," his mother said.

"Yes."

"You'd not have got that up in September."

"I don't know what I'd have got up in September."

"You'd not have got it up," said Milena Kelb, and wrung the sheet out harder than the sheet needed, and that was the whole of the conversation about it, and she went back to her board, and he sat down on the bench opposite with his right arm on his knee and looked at the inside of it for a long time.

The hand was warm. That was the second thing. It was the driest hand he had ever had in his life, and warm, and it sat perfectly still against his knee, not shaking, not twitching, and it would hold a cup at sixty. He had made himself test it before he came out of the clinic, because a boy of twenty does that alone, in a bed, at two in the morning, and he had held a cup of water in it for eleven minutes and set it down on a tray without spilling, and then he had tried to write his own name, and his name came out the way a nine-year-old's comes out when the nine-year-old's hand is stiff.

Nothing hurt. That was the third thing and the worst thing.

Sera Holt had told him the whole of it on the morning of the fifteenth with the door shut and nobody in the room but the two of them: that he would be able to work, and that he would not be able to do the thing he had trained for, and that he had two years of it in front of him in which to decide what the thing he had trained for was going to be instead, and that this was not a comfort and was not meant to be.

The thing he had trained for was *copies*.

He had been three weeks in the post. Before the post he had been a copy-room runner for nine months, and before that a chandler's boy, and before that a school. He could do a fair fast hand and he could keep four columns in his head and he could look at a column of figures for two hours and know whether the column was lying, and that was the whole of what he was, and the Tower had put him at a counter-desk at the fourth week because there were not enough third-grade clerks in Morrowfen to keep the counters manned, and because he was quick and quiet and did not ask questions, which was described in his assessment as *steady*.

He was out of the clinic on the afternoon of the fifteenth with a note for the Fund and a fortnight's pay in an envelope from a man in the hall who would not give his name, and he had walked from the Rainmarket side to a laundry on the next street from Sill Row in forty minutes with his hand in his pocket. He had eaten on the board and slept until the third bell and come back to it, and that was the sixteenth, and the district account had come out that morning with a figure on it that nobody in the street understood and everybody in the street had understood by the fourth bell.

The school house on Sill Row was shut for the day. That was not unusual; the ward school closes a week in the middle of a flood because the children are displaced and the stoves are wanted, and it had been shut for six days. But it was shut *cleanly* on the fifteenth and on the sixteenth there were two men in the doorway with a sheet of paper, and the sheet of paper was the district account, and the two men were not ward men and were not clerks either; they were the sort of young man that an office sends out when a line is going to be cut.

Ondra stood on the step and looked at the paper from the second row's window and could read the top of it upside down at that distance, which is a thing you can do.

*SUSPENSION OF ATTENDANCE. The winter term is closed from the—*

He went and found his sister at the tanner's, where she had been sent for the day, and told her in about nine words, and she said, "Which children," and he said he did not know, and she said, "Then it's all of us," and he said, "No," and she said, "It's all of us, Ondra, they've shut it, nobody's on it," and he said, "Four," because he had read the sheet from the window, and his sister of eleven said, "How do you know four," and he said, "There are four names on the front and four names on the back and the four on the front are off it for arrears and the four on the back are off it because the seat's gone," and she said, "Say their names," and he said them, because he could read them, and one of them was a boy called Tibb Vray who had given him a wax tablet in the spring and had wanted a proper one, and one of them was a girl of nine called Wren Ostrow whose mother cooked at the low kitchens and who was the only person in that room who looked unsurprised.

They walked up to the Fund's room together, over the chandler's shop, and got the shutter open, and that is where the second part of the sixteenth of the rains happened to Ondra Kelb, and it happened to about twelve other people as well.

---

The Fund's one room is eleven feet by nine, it smells of tallow and cold wax, and every inch of two walls is shelf. The shelves hold the discharge book, which is the only book the Fund has, and the book is nineteen years old in its binding and forty in its spine, and the pages in front are not a record of what happened to a clerk; they are a record of what the Fund was asked to pay.

Zorka Vendl was sixty-one and had kept it for nineteen of those years, and she was sitting at a table with a slate and a pen and a small brass weight on a stack of forms, and when two wet children came up the outside of a shop shutter she said, without looking up:

"Sit on the bench. Not the forms."

"I've the Fund note," Ondra said. "Sera Holt gave it to me yesterday. It's the clinic, six weeks of the cot, and two fingers of the bone-medicine, and—"

"I know what it is. The note tells me the price. It doesn't tell me the case." She wrote a number. "A case is a person and a date and an instrument. The person is you. The date is the fourteenth. Now sit down and tell me the instrument, and don't guess, because a wrong instrument is worse than no instrument. It comes back to this room in four years and nobody can make it go out again."

"I don't know the instrument."

"Then that's the first useful thing that's happened today." She put the pen down. "The form has a box. Every case on this board has a box filled in with a document. *Injury sustained in the course of duty.* That is a box, and it is the only box, and I have had it on this form for nineteen years, and I have never had a case where the box was the true sentence. It is a box for saying where you were, not what happened, and every clerk who comes up here can fill it in in four seconds and none of them ever want to, because what happened is the part that makes you stop writing."

"That's twice tonight somebody's said the same thing to me," said Ondra.

"Which is a thing about this room and not about you," said Zorka Vendl. "Go on."

The board filled up after the first hour. That was the thing he had not been ready for: he had expected a room with a sad woman in it, and what he got was a queue. The counter-desk juniors came up in ones and twos all afternoon, because there is no wall in it a man can stand his back against, and there is nothing a junior of the third week can do with a morning except go and see what the Fund's room looks like.

By the light going he had counted eleven. Two of them had been at the counter-desk on the ninth. One was three weeks in the post on the ninth, like Kelb; the other had gone to the washroom at the noon bell and come back to find the entry already countersigned, and had thought nothing of it, and had said so twice, to two different people, in the tone of a man explaining an administrative gap.

None of them asked him about his hand. That was the second surprise, and it was worse than the first. What they asked was about the countersignature, in detail, and they wanted to know whether the figures had to be wrong. One of them, a boy of nineteen in a wet coat, said, "I have always understood that a countersignature means somebody checked it," and Zorka Vendl said, "It means he was there," and he said, "That's the same," and she said, "It is not the same, and the difference is the whole reason that boy on that bench has a hand like that and you have not, and you may never know how close you came, and that is the correct outcome, and it is not a comfort, and I am not going to give you one."

The one who had gone to the washroom on the ninth was Emil Skar, six weeks in the post, and he had a pale face and had been sent up by the counter-clerk who had him on the desk that week, and he stood at the end of the table and read the form over Zorka Vendl's shoulder without asking, which was a thing nobody else in the room had done.

"Ma'am," he said. "This box. *Injury sustained in the course of duty.* Suppose a thing happens in the night watch, and the man does not come in until the morning, and nobody finds out about it until the day after that. Which day does the duty start on — the day the watch began, or the morning?"

Zorka Vendl looked at him for a while.

"That," she said, "is the first question anybody has asked me in this room in six years, and the answer is the day the watch began, and you may put it in the box, and nobody has ever put it in."

"And that is a Monday," said Skar, to himself, in the voice of a young man doing an addition. Then he looked at Ondra Kelb, who had been sitting on the bench for two hours with his hand in his lap, and his face did something, and he said, "I'm sorry," in a voice with no particular weight in it, and then, immediately, honestly, and in a way that was harder to sit with: "What happens next in a case like this. After you've put the form in. What actually happens."

"Nothing," said Zorka Vendl. "The clinic gets paid. That is what happens next. Six weeks of the cot and the medicine and the woman who comes in, and a hand-book when you want one, and the Fund's levy is a twentieth of a hundredth of the hall's pay, so it comes out of every clerk in the Chamber at a rate you would not believe, and the levy pays it, and that is the end of it."

"And the years?"

"The years are not in the form," said Zorka Vendl.

The room was quiet for a moment, and the tallow smell came up off the floor.

"Go and look at the shelf," she said. "Board three. From the top."

He got up and looked. The discharge book was not in columns, exactly. It had the year, and a name, and a case number, and a total in the fourth column that was what the Fund had paid, and a mark in the fifth column that meant one of three things, and there were forty-one marks in the book in nineteen years.

Twenty-four of them were a single stroke, which meant a case closed and paid and the person went back to the desk.

Thirteen were two strokes. Ondra counted them twice, because he was a clerk and he counted things.

"That's a hand," he said.

"Two strokes is a hand," said Zorka Vendl. "Or an eye, or a season's recovery, or a hip, and the Fund does not ask which, because the Fund could not afford to know which, and because a clerk who has lost something does not have to sit in a chair and be asked what it was in order to be paid for a doctor. Four of them are three strokes."

"What's three strokes."

"Three strokes is a clerk who did not come back to the hall," said Zorka Vendl. "I have four of them in nineteen years and I can tell you the years and I cannot tell you a single thing about any of the four, and two of them were not in the hall before me, and one of them is the brother of the woman who sat in that chair this morning, and I found him because she asked me to find him, and I could not."

She picked up the pen again.

"That is the whole of the fund," she said. "It turns a person into a case number, competently, on a Wednesday, in that size of room, and it is the only thing in this building that turns an injury into a claim at all, and every single year since the flood years it has run at a deficit, and it will run at a deficit next year, and it is the only organisation in the Tally Halls that anybody has ever thanked me for."

---

Marek came up the outside of the chandler's shop at the last of the light with the Tallow file under his arm and did not go in for four minutes, because he could hear there were a dozen people in there and he needed to know that first.

He went in.

The room was full. Eleven juniors in wet counter coats standing against the shelves, every one of them between nineteen and twenty-three, the secretary at her table, the boy on the bench, and his sister on the floor with her back to the stove.

"Sit down," said Zorka Vendl. "Not on the forms."

"My name is Marek Vale. I'm the clerk of the day for the ward named on the roll. I'm not from the Fund and I'm not on your board and I have not come about a payment."

"Then you've come about a case number, because it is the only thing in this room that isn't a person." She turned the slate round. "What instrument."

Marek took the file out from under his arm and opened it on the table beside her slate, and put his finger on the foot of the entry, and said:

"The ward settlement entry. Countersigned at the counter-desk on the ninth day of the rains, in pencil, by Ondra Kelb, of this hall, third week in the post, on the totals. That is the instrument. And the entry was signed as supervisor by Hesper Rann, and the ward settlement was certified as posted on the eleventh by a one-page certificate of posting that is a different paper and is not the instrument, and I want all three of those names in one sentence on your board, because at the moment your board has two and the folder has two and neither of the two is a document anybody can be paid against."

Zorka Vendl did not move for about four seconds. Then she put her hand out for the entry and read the whole of the foot of it, and read it again, and read the pencil, and then she said something Marek had not expected her to say, which was:

"Third week." She looked up. "You put that in because it is the answer to the question of whether he was careless."

"Yes."

"Nobody has put that in. In nineteen years I have had two cases where the box said *careless* and in both of them the man was six months in the post and the desk was short, and I have never once had a clerk of the day come up here and put a junior's date in the post in the same sentence as a document." She wrote. "Ward settlement entry. Countersigned at the counter-desk, ninth day, pencil, on the totals. K. Kelb, third week."

"Is that the right heading?"

"That is the right heading, and it is the first time in nineteen years that heading has been long enough to be useful, and I am going to keep it long." She wrote the case number and gave it to him to read back. "Clerk Vale. I am going to say three things and you may do exactly what you like with them.

"One. The Fund will pay the clinic and it will pay for a hand-book and it will not pay for the years and it has no box for the years, and I have never argued about that in nineteen years because there is nothing to argue with.

"Two. The Fund's levy is paid by the clerks of this hall, and the levy is a rate on the hall, and a rate is not a fund, and if this hall is ever asked to make good a shortfall on this case there will be a meeting in that hall about it, and there are forty-one men and women in that hall and eleven of them are juniors, and the meeting will be fair, and it will be very fair, and everybody in it will be able to say truthfully that they did not know."

Eleven juniors looked at the floor in a way that suggested they had been about that meeting all afternoon.

"Three," said Zorka Vendl. "The certificate of posting. You have told me the name of the supervisor, the name of the junior, and the date on the counter-desk. You have not told me the name of the clerk of the day who countersigned the posting certificate on the eleventh, and it is in the folder, and it is a separate paper, and you have just spent four minutes making sure I know that."

"I have."

"That was the cleverest thing anybody has done in this room in nineteen years," said Zorka Vendl, "and I would like you to know that it is also the cruellest, and that you did not do it for me."

Marek stood at the table in nine feet by eleven of cold-wax air and did not say anything, because she was right, and because the answer to that was a sentence about procedure, and there is no sentence about procedure that is true.

He did not offer money. He did not offer to help with the hand. He did not explain the mechanism, and this cost him the most, and he understood as he stood there that it was going to cost him the most in about three weeks, when a boy in a room with a door would ask him why, in a voice much like his own, and he would have to find a way to say *because a delay was stored in a yearline and a false-witnessed instrument walked its chain to a living certifier* in a sentence that a mother and a Fund secretary and a room of counter-clerks can all receive, and there is no such sentence, and the tower has never needed one.

Zorka Vendl capped her pen.

"Clerk Vale," she said. "The junior at your elbow. If you are going to stand in that room, then you may as well be in the room, and I would like you to say to him now, in front of these people, whether the figures he added up were right, because he is going to be asked that for the rest of his working life by people who were not in this room, and he needs to have heard it from somebody who was."

Marek looked at the boy on the bench, who had not moved and had not looked up and had a hand lying open in his lap like a thing that had been put down and not picked up.

"They were right," Marek said. "The column was correct and the totals were correct and the page is in the record. I have said so on a record this afternoon and it is signed by a Deputy Settlement Steward and it is in the file and it will be there after every one of us is dead."

Kelb did not look up.

"Sir." It was one of the juniors by the shelf, and he was about twenty-one and he had a clerk's coat on with the badge not yet removed from the counter rotation. "Sir, will it happen to us?"

The room went entirely quiet.

It was a one-sentence answer. Marek had it, assembled, in the correct order, in the flat voice: *yes, and I did it, and it happened because the document you countersigned carried a term you were not shown, and if I tell you that you will not believe me, and if you do not believe me you will not be careful, and if you are not careful the next one will be you, and I cannot prove one word of it to you because the proof is in a page that no clerk in this city is permitted to copy.*

He opened his mouth and nothing came out of it, and it was visible, and a room of counter-clerks and a Fund secretary and a mother and two children watched a man fail to produce a sentence, in a room where a boy had produced one at four in the morning on a stone stair.

Zorka Vendl picked up her slate.

"Master Rill," she said, not unkindly, "the clerk has been asked a question that is not a matter for this room, and I am going to take it off him, because he has not the standing to answer it and you have not the standing to put it. What I can tell you is the answer to the question you should have asked, which is what you are going to do about it. Write it down. Every one of you. What a counter-signature is. Not what it means — what it *is*. Four lines. Take them to the desk and say them out loud to the next junior who comes on, and do it in the next four days, before somebody comes on who has not heard."

"Says who?"

"Eleven of you, on a form I will sign, in a room I do not have to explain myself in." She looked round. "That is a mutual. That is what a mutual is. You have never once asked it to do anything and it is going to make you do something in the next four days and you are not going to enjoy it."

Kelb spoke, still to his knees.

"Two hundred and seventy-nine hundreds and forty pints," he said.

"Say what?"

"The figure on the ward's copy at the foot of the roll." He said it without any expression at all, in the flat administrative voice of a young man who has been given a fact and is now arranging it, and Marek had heard that voice on a stair four nights ago and it went through him like cold water. "It is two hundred and seventy-nine hundreds and forty pints of excess in one night against a lawful hundred and sixty, and I added the column up twice on the ninth with a pencil because the pen had not come, and the column is right. So you can write that on your board, and I have written it in your book, and it is the only number anybody in this room is going to be able to check, and if one day somebody comes up here and says the column was careless, you send them to the Tallow file and to Renata Vole's seal and you make them stand there while it is read out."

Eleven juniors looked at him.

"Thank you," said Zorka Vendl, and wrote it down, and did not say anything else for about a minute, which for Zorka Vendl was a great deal.
