# Chapter 40 — The Yard Is Not an Address

They tried to sit in the Gate yard on the thirteenth and it failed in four minutes, and it failed the way everything fails, which is by somebody saying a word that turns out to mean more than anybody in the room had thought it meant.

"Article one of the ward's terms," said Harrow. "That the day is told in advance, and that the notice of the day is not left at an office. Nothing in article one says where the room is. Do you want it here?"

"I want it here," said Aldis Kern.

"Then I have to tell you that a sitting has to be at an address." He was standing at the tank steps, in the rain, in a good coat that had cost him more in four days than the coat Marek had bought. "A board of this kind does not exist in the March either, and where it does exist it sits at a place with a door and a seal, because a seal is how a person knows that the room on a particular day is the room the board is in, and there is no seal on a water yard and there is not going to be one, and I could ask the Deputy for one and she would give it to me and it would be a lie."

"Then what is in the seal."

"The date, the hour, and the terms." He took a folded sheet out of the case. "This is the notice. It is Trust paper, it has the Chamber's seal because the Chamber is a party, and it has three lines on the back that we agreed on Friday in a room on Bell Stair, and a fourth we agreed this afternoon, and the fourth is in a different hand and I am not going to pretend it is the same document. The ward's attendance is not a matter of grace. The record is read to the yard afterwards by a person who is not a clerk. A member who dissents may have the grounds written in their own words and kept. And no collection is entered while this board is open."

"That is five lines."

"There are four and one of them is in the margin and I have not got the hang of your margins yet and I intend to."

---

The other four attempts took the rest of the day and are worth having in the order they failed.

The Trust moved that the cause entered on the twelfth be struck from the desk's book, on the ground that a cause is a statement by an officer about his own arithmetic and the officer has been examined. It failed, and Renata Vole gave the answer herself, in one sentence, and gave it so kindly that Harrow wrote it down in his own hand and read it back to make sure of it: *this board has no power to strike an entry out of a desk's book, and it is not going to be pretended into one, and what the board can do is hear the cause, and I am willing to have it heard, in front of the parties, on Thursday, and I would be obliged if the board did not waste the morning on a motion it cannot carry.*

The board then tried to find a figure. That was Sedge's motion and it was his instinct and it was the correct instinct and it was hopeless, because the finding certifies four hundred and forty and the ward's roll says two hundred and seventy-nine hundreds and forty pints of excess and the file says six hundred and forty and the examiner's record of the twenty-eighth says four hundred and forty-one, and the four are all right, and the room could not choose between them and would not have been allowed to.

"Record all four," said Neris Kade, who had been given the second seat and a sheet of paper by the Chair and had not asked for either, because a person who puts the bodies in has to be in the room. She is not a member and cannot vote and did not pretend otherwise; what the sheet is for is holding the four numbers as they are called out, in the order they were called, which is the one thing at that table nobody else was doing. The board agreed it in four minutes. "Record all four and adjudicate none of them. That will be the first act of this board and it is the whole of the moral content of a board in its first sitting, and I would like it minuted that I said so and that I am not being dramatic, and I am, but the other thing is also true."

Harrow minuted it.

And the fifth attempt was the one that succeeded, and it succeeded by about nine words, and the nine words were his own, and he said afterwards that he had been carrying them since the Monday and had not known until Tuesday whether he would be allowed to use them.

"No collection is entered while this board is open," he said. "A board is open from the moment the notice of the day is served to the moment the finding is made in this room. That is the term and it is the only term in this document that stops anything, and I want the room to notice what it is worth. It is four days. It is a queue behind a door. The collection enters the day after the finding whether the finding says anything or not, and the four of them at the bottom of that roll will be asked for two hundred and seventy-nine hundreds and forty pints of water at the ordinary wage in the second week of next month, whatever we decide, and if we decide nothing, which is possible, they will be asked for it on the ordinary course and faster."

"Then it is worth nothing," said Aldis Kern.

"It is worth four days," said Harrow, "and in this city that is not nothing, and I am not going to let anybody in this room say the word *nothing* again about something I have just got, because I have written it in a column of my own once this week and it is very tiring."

Nobody moved against it. Neither Renata Vole nor Hesper Rann moved against it, and Hesper Rann's not moving was what told Marek Vale, standing at the back of the yard under the tank steps' overhang with his coat on and nothing in his hands, exactly what the term was worth and exactly who had written the form in the drawer on the twenty-first of the rains.

---

They set the hearing for the sixteenth, at the eighth bell, with four days' notice, which is less than the Charter's minimum for a proceeding of consequence and more than the Charter allows where a settlement is already certified, and Harrow put the date in himself and served the notice himself because there was no clerk available to serve it and no office in this city that would let one.

"The parties are four," he said. "The office. The ward, by its captain, which is a person. The Fund, which is a person with a room over a chandler's shop. And the Chamber, which is a body and has nobody in it today that I can serve, and I am going to walk up the hill and give the notice to the woman on the posting desk in person, and she is going to read it out, because the ward's term says the notice is not left at an office, and if the notice is not left at an office then it has to be carried somewhere, and there is only one somewhere in this city that a ward can be in."

"It is the Gate tank," said Kern.

"It is the Gate tank," said Bel Harrow, "and I have to tell you that a ward cannot be served, and that a notice of a hearing served at a tank is not a service, it is a thing a man in a good coat does in front of four hundred people. And that it is the best available arrangement in this city and that the reason it is the best available arrangement is that somebody refused to build anything better, and she is sitting in this room and she is not going to thank me for saying it out loud."

---

The notice was carried down the hill on the fourteenth, in the rain, by the clerk of the day for the sitting of the twelfth.

There was no argument about who carried it. A notice of a hearing is served by the clerk who held the sitting it is served out of, and that clerk was a person and had standing and could hold a seal, and there were four other third-grade clerks in the hall and any one of them would have done, and the four of them had looked at each other over a tray at about the eighth bell and had come to the same conclusion at the same moment, and none of them said a word about it, and Ondra Kelb picked it up.

He walked down the causeway with the notice in a folded sheet under his coat against the water and the string round his wrist, and the yard saw him coming from about forty yards, because the yard had watched him go up on Monday and had not managed one single thing about what happened up there, and there had been three hundred and forty people on that stair.

He got the notice out of the sheet and could not get the string off his wrist, and the string was the office's and it was waxed, and the right hand would not do it, and he stood at the tank for four seconds with his wrist up and his left hand working at a knot he could not see, and Milan Vray came up the steps and untied it for him and gave the string back and did not say one word about it, and neither did anybody else in that yard, and that was the correct decision by about forty people simultaneously.

"Emil," said Kelb, reading. He was reading it to the yard because the yard's term says the day is told and not left at an office. "A hearing of the emergency appeals board, the second-floor room, Bell Stair, the sixteenth of this month, the eighth bell, notice of four days. The board will sit and take the account of the ward and will record it. The ward may attend and its attendance is not a matter of grace. The record will be read in this yard afterwards, out loud, by a person who is not a clerk. And no collection is entered while the board is open."

"Four days," said Vray.

"Four days."

Somebody at the back of the yard said, "That is the whole of it, then," and Aldis Kern did not turn round, and did not answer, and did not need to, because there were three boards on the wall behind him: the yard roll of the pail water in forty hands, the oiled sheet of the night of the fourteenth with the Underbank at the top of it, and the two sheets of twenty-nine answers with three no's on them, and a wet new notice going up above all three, held by a nail, in the rain.

Across the channel above the yard the Third Sluice stood shut with a lamp hooked to the rail and nothing on the platform, and had stood shut since the third of the rains, and the town main it had been holding up was thinner than the yard had been told and had been thinner for a fortnight, and the Ropewalk standpipe would be short by the end of the week unless somebody boiled on a ration again on the Wednesday, which they would do, and did, and had been told to do by a man who wanted nothing for it.

Marek Vale stood at the edge of the yard and did not go up, and was not asked, and had the four things in his coat that were worth nothing, and the fifth thing in a house book up the hill that was worth less, and had watched a boy of twenty carry a piece of paper a mile and a half in the rain with the wrong hand, and had worked out, standing in the wet, what the next four days were made of.

The ward had a room. The room had a chair in it, and a Crown March representative who would serve the minutes in his own hand because no office would let a clerk do it, and a table of five with a Trust officer at it who would not concede she had been wrong, and a Chamber clerk who would have served the record if the room had permitted it, and a Fund secretary who had been asked twice and had declined twice and was still in the room, and an examiner at the second seat with a sheet she could not vote on, holding four numbers in the order they were called, and a term about dissent that three people had voted in and one had refused on the record.

On Thursday morning four hundred people would be in a corridor and a stair in the rain, because a ward is not a person and a room holds thirty, and the first thing that any of them said in that room would have to be said by a person with a name in a box, and the most the machinery had produced in a month was four days in which nobody's name went on an invoice.

For four years Marek had believed that a document was the only thing in this city that moved anything.

The notice was on the board at the Gate tank, wet, in the rain, and it was going to be there on Thursday morning, and on Thursday morning the room was going to fill with people who had been sent out of it.
