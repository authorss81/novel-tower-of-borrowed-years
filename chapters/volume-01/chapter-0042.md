# Chapter 42 — The Order Things Happened In

The recess was twenty minutes and the corridor did not empty in it, and at the end of it Renata Vole asked for the length of it to be called through the door, because four hundred people had come up a hill in the rain to be near a room and were owed a figure.

"Twenty minutes," said the hearing officer. "There will be a further recess at the sixth bell and I do not know how long, and if I am wrong about the sixth I will say so from this door when it is called."

Somebody at the back of the corridor said *thank you*, and the officer shut the door, and it was the only thing anybody in that corridor had been given in a day.

Bel Harrow stood in front of the table with his coat off, which it had not been since the eighth bell, and did not sit down.

"Before we go on. Nobody is sworn — I said that at the eighth bell and I meant it. So the seven accounts you heard are seven people telling you what happened to them, in their own names, on their own liability, and they bind nobody but themselves. What we have is seven accounts and a bundle, and they do not fit together, and I have spent the morning finding out that they do not fit together."

He put two fingers on the bundle at the foot of the table.

"The office keeps its papers in the order of what is owed. A clause, a recital, a figure, a schedule cited at the foot of a clause. Take two pages out of that bundle and lay them side by side and they sit together, because the office decided they sit together. Your captain keeps his in the order of what happened. A date, a number of pails, a hundred and forty people, a boy going into a cellar. And that order cannot be filed, because a thing that happened to a man on the fourteenth is between a thing that happened to him on the ninth and a thing that happened to him on the fifteenth, and that is not a structure of anything.

"So when I asked at the ninth bell for the ward's account in the order the ward keeps it, the Captain could not give it to me, and he was not being difficult. He has a roll of the pail water on a board in the Gate yard in forty hands, an oiled sheet of forty items under nine feet of water on the same board, two long sheets with twenty-nine answers and three refusals, and nineteen answer sheets on a nail with their strings on. Every one of those is a record. Not one of them can be carried into a room. I have been trying for two days to think of a way to carry one in and I cannot, now that I have said it out loud.

"Which means this room has seven people in it, and that is every part of the standing Tallow Ward has in the proceeding that is going to decide its bill."

Nobody said anything, and two hundred and eighty people in a corridor said nothing at the same moment, which is a thing that happens in a building like this and which nobody has ever measured. Sixty of the three hundred and forty had gone down the hill in the recess, to get food into the yard before the light went, and were on the stair again by the third bell.

"Now the question the room does not want. Deputy, I am about to put a figure on the table, and I want it on the record that I got it from a woman nobody in this building has asked for anything in a month."

---

Pella Rusk had not stood up.

"I told a yard on Saturday that the only place that number can be said properly is a room, in front of the people it is about. So I am going to say it once, in this room, and not twice, and I am not going to be the one who carries it about afterwards."

"Fourteen places," she said. "Ninety-one children on my register, and eleven chairs stacked on the back row because the term shut again on the sixth. That is the ward's school, valued by the water office at the close. The office values a school as a fixed thing, because a school is a term and a term does not vary. It has been fourteen since before I came here nine years ago. Nobody cut it. It is what my school is worth.

"And I am finished being the woman who says that number. If this board wants it, use it. And if anybody wants me to say it again in the yard at the end of this, I will not, and that is not a performance."

Harrow wrote it down, read it back, and got it right, and then did a thing that took four seconds and which nobody at that table expected.

"Mrs Rusk. If the finding is read in the Gate yard afterwards by a person who is not a clerk, and that number is not in it, then everyone who heard it here has heard a number in a room that is not in the record, and in nine months it will be a rumour, because you have just told us that is what happens to numbers. Do you want it in the finding."

"With the ninety-one in it," said Pella Rusk. "A number without the ninety-one is a lie told in the other direction."

"Noted," said Bel Harrow, and wrote *fourteen places against ninety-one children*, "and if the Deputy tells me it is no matter for this board I shall read it in anyway and let her object on the record, which she will enjoy."

"I shall object on the record," said Renata Vole, "and I shall not enjoy it, and I shall object after it is written."

---

Then the Chair moved the chairs, which was the only piece of theatre anybody did all day. He had the seven taken out of the front four rows and set along the two side walls facing each other, so the table could see all of them and they could see the table. It took four minutes and a good deal of noise, and Bohdan Rezek had to be got up out of a low chair by two men, and he laughed at it and said he had been getting up out of chairs for thirty years.

The room was then a table of five with seven people down the sides of it, and Harrow looked at the arrangement and said, "That is what the room is for. It is not for us."

Then he asked the question.

"Deputy. The ward's levy is drawn out of the district account. That is your office's position and it has been your office's position since the fifteenth. The account is over-drawn at two hundred and forty per cent as presented and five hundred and eighty before a carry nobody has ever applied, and it is cut at the close of every month by a written order, and the cut comes off the bottom of the page.

"What is at the bottom of the page."

"A standing supply," said Renata Vole, without looking up. "The ward clinic's coal and the ward's night lamp."

"And under that."

"The school."

"And what is a school worth on that page."

"It is worth a term," said Vole. "The office values it as a term, and a term does not vary with the winter. I have not got the figure in front of me and I am not going to guess at a figure in a room."

"It is fourteen places," said Pella Rusk.

"Thank you," said Renata Vole, and said nothing else, and Harrow wrote down that she had said thank you.

"Then the board has a problem and the problem has a shape. A day of levy is a day drawn out of the district account. The account is over-drawn. The account is cut at the close and the cut comes off the bottom, and the bottom is a clinic's coal and a lamp, and then a schoolroom with eleven chairs stacked on the back row.

"So every day this board asks the ward to take is a day that comes off the bottom of that page, and the bottom of that page is a school. Deputy, is there any arrangement of this office by which that is not true."

"There is no arrangement of this office by which that is not true," said Renata Vole, "and this office has known it for as long as the school has been on the account, and this office has never once had to say it in a room with the ward in it, because this office has never had to ask four hundred people to take days. I have said it. I would like it recorded that I said it in the first person, and that I am not going to be told by any member of this table that I have enjoyed it."

---

Nadja Ostrow stood up and did not use the chair.

"You asked me at the bottom of the hill whether I would take a public levy, and I said no to the days and yes to the work, and you wrote it down in my words, and nobody has come back to it. I want the room to know I noticed, because it is the only decent thing anybody has done for me since the ninth."

"Mrs Ostrow—"

"You have now got a number in the room. Fourteen. That is what my daughter's place is worth on the page you cut. And a day of mine is a day off that same page. So a man in a good coat has come up my lane twice and asked me to take money out of my own daughter's seat, and the first time he did not know it, and the second time he did, and this time he has got the number in a room and said it out loud, which I am told is what I have been asking for. I would like to say that I have got it. I would also like to say that a number being in a room does not put it back."

There was no sound at all from the corridor, which two hundred people do not manage by accident.

"So I will say the rest and sit down. I will take the work. I will not take the days. Not this month, not in March, not in any month while that page has my daughter's arithmetic on it. And if the answer to that is that the ward's bill cannot be paid in days, then the ward's bill cannot be paid in days, and I would like somebody in this room to write that down instead of asking me a third time."

Harrow wrote it down. He wrote for about eleven seconds and read it back, and she made him do the lane name again because he had got it wrong, and he did it again.

Then Aldis Kern stood up, and he took longer getting up than the others, and he did not sit down again.

"And before the board goes looking for a shape it likes, there is one more thing on the table and it is mine and it is not comfortable.

"Since the fifth of this month the yard has gone out into nineteen lanes and asked at forty-one doors, one household at a time, two of them to every door, and got twenty-nine answers and three refusals, and the refusals are on a board in the yard in the words the households used.

"That is the consent this board has. Twenty-nine households out of four hundred and six. Eleven lines on a schedule nobody was served with. And this morning the office has been talking, quite reasonably, about a public levy on the ward — and a public levy on the ward is a levy on four hundred and six people, and three hundred and seventy-seven of them have not been asked one question by anybody.

"I am not saying the yard will refuse. The yard has never refused me a real question. I am saying that if this board builds a levy on four hundred names, then the first name on it that cannot pay becomes a second day, and then a third, and the whole of it comes apart in a fortnight in a yard with eleven chairs on the back row of a school. I have been captain of this water sixteen years and I have signed for a hundred and fifty per cent of what a careful man would have signed for, every one of them.

"Twenty-nine doors. That is what I have. And the difference between that and four hundred is not a formality and I will not have it treated as one in a room."

He sat down.

The room was quiet long enough that a chair moved somewhere behind the door.

"That is the second objection of the day and neither of them is mine," said Harrow. "The first was the Deputy's, and she was right, and the answer to hers is that this board cannot make anybody take a day. The second is the Captain's, and he is right, and the answer to his is that this board cannot go out into nineteen lanes and knock, and I do not intend to pretend a board sitting on Bell Stair can do what four hundred people did in three days with charcoal and a bit of string.

"So the board has two answers to make, and one of them has to be made this afternoon, and I am going to make the second one myself. The Chair may make his own proposals. This is the first I have made in five years, and I have thought about it for four days, and I want it in the minutes that I did not think of it before the eleventh of this month.

"The ward's bill is two hundred and seventy-nine hundreds and forty pints at the ordinary wage. It may not be paid in days drawn out of the district account, and I have given that reason in the Deputy's own mouth and I am not going to improve on it. It may not be levied on four hundred people who have not been asked. Therefore it is to be paid in work, named, at the ordinary wage, in the ward's own yards, on a schedule written out and open on a board; and a household that will not do it is not to have another household's share put on it, and the Deputy has told this room already that she will put such a share in her own hand, in a form, in a room, and that it will be correct and she will not enjoy it.

"That is not a conversion and I am not going to call it one. A conversion means a thing has been changed into another thing, and nothing has been changed into anything. It is a bill moved onto an instrument that already exists in this city, and I have found it, and it was on the desk of a district accountant in a frame turned to the wall, and I did not get it from him and he is not in this room."

Nobody looked at the back, where Grale was standing.

"Deputy, does the district account carry anything that has never been applied."

"It carries a carry of forty thousand nine hundred," said Renata Vole, looking up for the first time since the school had been named. "Entered every year since the flood years. Unapplied, unclaimable, held against a standing reserve this office has never located and has never been asked to locate, because no form anywhere asks whether the fund exists. A carry is a line of charges the office has entered against itself and cannot apply, because it cannot name a district to apply them against. That is the whole of what a carry is, and I have said more about it in this room than I have said in nine years."

"Then you can name a district," said Bel Harrow, "and so can this board, and a board is a body, and nobody has ever done it because a carry is a question the office asks itself once a year and does not answer."

He put his pen down.

"I am not making that order today and I am not proposing it, and I do not know whether it is lawful, because the man in Crown March who would have to sign it is on a road between here and the coast. What I am telling this room is that the ward's bill is going to be paid, and that the instrument it is going to be paid out of is the one line in that account that has sat there for three hundred years unasked, and that when the Chair says that he is aware he is proposing to spend a thing nobody in this room can prove exists. I do not know what is on that line, and neither does she, and neither does the accountant whose account it is, and I am going to find out on Friday, and I am not going to pretend in the meantime that I know, because a board that starts by pretending it knows is a board that ends by not checking."

He turned round to the room and not to the table.

"I am going to move that the instrument be called at the sixth bell, in full, before any part of the finding is settled, and that is a request and not an order, and every person in this room is going to hear the whole of it, including the parts that are about me. Mr Rann is the supervisor of the settlement desk. The entry in that bundle is his, and he signed it on the ninth of the rains, and I am going to ask him a question in a moment that I have not put to him before and that I have no right to put to him, and I am going to rule that the Chair is asking it, and that a party may decline to answer, and that the declining is written down as a declining and nothing is made of it. Mr Rann, are you content with those terms."

"They are the terms I invented on the ninth," said Hesper Rann, "and yes."
