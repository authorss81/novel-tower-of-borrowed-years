# Chapter 31 — A Room Is Not a Decision

The notice of the recount went onto the posting board on the first of next month at the first bell, with a drawing pin and a wet sky, and it named a sitting on the twelfth, twelve days' notice, and the parties, which were the office of the Deputy Settlement Steward, the supervisor of the settlement desk, and the assessment of the water of Tallow Ward season four hundred and twelve.

Then it said, on the last line, in the same hand: *served at the Gate tank, Tallow Ward, at the eighth bell.*

Marek read it twice with a queue behind him and understood the last line in four seconds. There was no box on any form in the building for the parties to a ward finding, and a ward is served where it is, so a notice to a ward was a sheet of paper nailed to a wall in the rain with a clerk on a stool in front of four hundred people who had already been served the same finding once.

Ostrowsky was on the step with the pad under his arm.

"It goes to eleven households and to a tank," he said. "I have been the levy clerk of this hall nineteen years and I have never once written *served at the Gate tank* in a line of my own. The form has a box for every thing in the world except the thing this notice is about. Go to the yard, clerk. You will want to be in it before the queue."

---

He got there at the noon bell and the yard was full, which was the first thing he noticed: two hundred and sixty people in a wet yard is a crowd, and four hundred is a public, and the Gate yard had been a public every day for a month and had somehow never once been a room.

The second thing was a handcart at the mouth of the yard with its shafts in the mud, a good cart, belonging to somebody who had not expected mud. On the shaft end was a leather case. Beside it stood a man of about forty in a coat none of them had seen, with a badge on the breast that four people at the front read and one of them said out loud, and the yard did not know what to do with the word.

"Captain," the man said. "I am going to be stupid at this and I would rather say so at the start. Bel Harrow, a Crown March representative. I have never been to a flood and I have been in this city three hours, and I should like to be a person in your yard for two hours."

Aldis Kern had a whistle round his neck he had not used since the fourteenth.

"Representative of what."

"That is the whole trouble. Of the procedure." Harrow turned his hand over. There was a brown ink stain across two fingers and a small cheap book in it. "I can make a room sit. I cannot stop a collection, I cannot pay anybody, I cannot touch your water account, and I cannot make four people's arithmetic agree with one another. When a district has been settled in a hurry for thirty years, Crown March sends the one department that can say a date out loud. We are not clever. We are the calendar."

Nadja Ostrow said, from the second row, "Are you a collector."

"No, ma'am."

"People come in good coats."

"I know. I am not going to be able to fix that by being pleasant, because being pleasant is what people in good coats are for."

The yard laughed, and it was a bad laugh and a real one, and something in the yard decided that the afternoon was going to be worth having.

---

What he wanted, in the end, was a petition.

He said it at the second bell, from the tank steps, because there was nowhere else to stand. "I have a form. A petition for a public board, two pages. The first is a schedule of grounds and the second is the petition. I cannot do anything without the second, and the second has to come from a body and not a person, because one person is a complaint and a body is a claim, and a claim is what requires a room."

Kern held up three hundred and eleven signatures on the back of a fuel-yard tally. "It went into a file as a document received and not acted upon. I could not tell you what that meant and I have decided it meant no."

"It meant no. May I?"

He turned it over, read a name or two under his breath, and gave it back.

"That is the best paper I have been handed in eleven years and I am going to have to ask you to write me another one. Your petition asks for four decisions, and four decisions can be refused in nine minutes with every word correct. What I need is a room, and there is no form anywhere in your country for a body asking for a room, because a body asking for a room is a body that has understood something about itself that no form has ever had a box for."

He stopped, which was his mistake, because a yard will finish a sentence for you if you leave it, and forty voices said *yes* at once and stopped, because a yard is not a room and nobody there knew how to make one.

"Five grounds on the first page, and four of them are about arithmetic and you have a great deal of arithmetic." He went through them on the steps in the rain. A settlement certified during a declared emergency. A party not served with a material term — and he added, without being asked, that he was not permitted to say which term, would be asked about it in a room on the ninth, and would not be able to answer. A document in the case with no copy. An account bearing figures that do not agree. A question of conduct excluded from a proceeding in which it ought to have been heard.

He put the form down on the tank.

"Four of those are about figures and one is about a person, and the procedure does not care which is worse. It cares which is argued. That is the part I came here to be embarrassed about, and I will be embarrassed out loud, because it is worse than being embarrassed quietly."

"Then say it," said Kern.

"I have to teach you to complain in the grammar of an office. Everything you have said to me since the second of the rains is true and none of it is a ground. A valve was open, a storehouse came down, a boy of thirteen has a leg. Those are events. A ground is a statement about a document, and a document is the only thing a procedure can be fed. I cannot take a valve to a board. I can take *an entry whose fourth clause recites an account no desk in this city can audit, and an assessment of which one page was never served on the parties*." He looked at Marek in the crowd and then deliberately away again, because he had noticed a clerk, and a clerk was a party, and he had not asked. "And I cannot take it out of what you have told me. I can only take it out of what one of you can put on a page in the shape the form wants, and the hardest sentence in this city begins *the document says* and ends with a thing a body did."

"So we have to write it," said Ostrow.

"You have to write it, and it will be worse than what you have written."

---

They wrote it all afternoon in pencil, in eleven hands, and it was the worst piece of paperwork produced in Tallow Ward in twenty years and the first thing the ward had ever produced that was shaped like a request. Harrow sat on the tank step and asked the order of events four times in four separate groups, got four different orders, and wrote all four down, and then said out loud that the fourth was the one he had been told was correct and that he had no way of knowing.

At the fourth bell he asked the question he had been waiting to ask, and he asked it to the yard and not to Kern, because he had worked out inside an hour that a yard answers a yard.

"What do you want decided."

There were eleven answers, two of them *everything*, one of them a name, and then somebody at the back said, slowly, in a voice that made the yard go quiet for the second time that day:

"Nothing. We want a room."

Harrow got up off the step. It was a woman of about sixty at the back with her hood down who had said it, and nobody in the front rows turned round to look at her, which told Marek more about her standing in that yard than four days of standing in it had.

"Then I have to tell you what a room costs, and I will do it in the open air, because you should not be told this in a corridor. Your bill is not suspended and it stands. Four of eleven households are payable this season, named in a finding with a seal on it, and the finding is lawful, and nobody in Crown March has the power to make a lawful finding unlawful.

"However. The fourth clause of that finding says the collection may not be entered before a cause is entered in the box at the head of the form. Your bill comes due on the twelfth, at a levy-office sitting I have no standing in and cannot enter, because a recount is a supervisor's step and a representative is not a supervisor. On the twelfth a reason will be written in that box, and then the collection may be entered, in the ordinary course, by people doing their work properly, and there is nothing in this city that stops it after that except a room.

"So a room does not stop your bill. A room is four days after your bill, and it is a room where somebody has to answer a question out loud in front of you. That is what you would be buying."

"Then put it in a calendar," said Kern, and looked as surprised about it as anybody.

Harrow wrote for nine minutes and read their second page back to the yard, which was not a petition.

*We the households of Tallow Ward ask that a public board be appointed, to sit at a place, on a day, with a chair. We do not ask it to decide anything.*

*1. That a ward may be in the room, that the people who live in it may sit in it, and that the ward's attendance is not a matter of grace.*
*2. That a person may be asked a question and may answer or not answer, and that not answering is written down as not answering, and nothing is made of it.*
*3. That anything read into that room may be read aloud to the people it is about, in the same words, before it is read to anybody else.*
*4. That the room keeps a record, and that the record is read to the ward afterwards, in the yard, out loud, by a person who is not a clerk.*
*5. That a person who disagrees with what the board decides may have the disagreement written down in their own words, and kept, and the keeping is the board's duty and not the person's.*
*6. That the ward is told the day in advance, and that the notice of the day is not left at an office.*

"That is not a petition," he said.

"No," said Kern.

"It is a terms sheet. I have to take it to Crown March as a request to sit, and Crown March will send it back with a question on it, and the question will be what the ward wants decided, and I am going to have to write the word *nothing* in that column." He put the pencil down. "It will be read in four rooms by people who look at the word and not at the six lines under it."

"Do it," said Nadja Ostrow.

"I will, at the first thing on Friday, badly and slowly. And Captain, I am going to want to come back to this yard nine times before the end of the month and I will not be good at it, and I am telling you now so that you can decide how much room to make me."

---

He was in Renata Vole's office at the fourth bell, and she had the form before he had finished sitting down, and she refused it in procedure with a schedule of reasons, reading each off the page and giving him the page number for it, which is a thing she does.

"One. A finding certified by this office on the fifteenth is not appealable, and a returnpath requires a living holder of the obligation, and the holder of your ward's obligation is a schedule with eleven names on it and no witness against any of them, and this office set all eleven aside in a room with your captain in it, which was correct and has put that instrument out of anybody's hands. There is nothing to appeal. And you are not appealing it. If you were, I would tell you the same thing.

"Two. The matter was opened on the ninth to the fifteenth and enlarged on the thirtieth without asking any party whether he agreed to it. I put that in my own finding because it is true, and I am not going to have you find it at a table and say I hid it.

"Three. A Crown March representative has standing in a Crown interest, and no Crown interest is pleaded. The Crown Terrace account in this matter is a district irrigation account. The word Crown is a district and a terrace, and this office is not going to let a Crown March officer into a district water account on the strength of a name."

"That is three reasons and they are all correct," said Harrow.

"They are correct, they are in writing, and I have given you the page for each, which I do for two people a year."

"Then I will not argue with one of them. I will say the thing that is not a reason. You have told me there is no appeal and I have not appealed. You have told me it is a district matter, and I have already written to Rainmarket to ask whether the district irrigation account is Crown money or district money, and I shall be told it is district money, and I shall be grateful." He put his hands flat on the table. "What I am asking for is a room. I am not asking this office to be right about anything. I have read one finding and it is better work than most of what my own office produces, and I should like to say that to the person who wrote it, in a room with the people it is about in it, and I am not going to be able to."

Renata Vole looked at him slightly longer than she looked at most people.

"Will you sit on the board."

"My instrument requires a March representative to preside at the first sitting, and after that the board chooses its own chair. I have put a sentence in the terms asking it not to choose me, and if it chooses me I will accept, because a calendar is worth more than my pride."

"You are a Crown March officer," said Renata Vole, "and you want to chair a board about a district water account in a city you have never been flooded in."

"There is nobody else in this room who can make a thing sit, and I have stopped pretending that is a compliment to the office. It is a fact about the size of it. We are the calendar."

She squared the form, and Marek — who had spent forty minutes in the corridor outside the third floor with no way to be useful to anybody — would find out three days later, from a clerk who had stood in the same corridor for the same forty minutes, that she had said one more thing before Harrow left the building.

*That she would schedule it; and that he should understand what that meant, because she was scheduling a room, a room was a thing she had never scheduled, and she would like it noted that she did not want to and could not find a reason to refuse that was better than the reason to agree.*

---

The notice went up on the posting board on the second at the first bell, under the recount notice, and the yard read it standing on a tank in the rain, because that is where a ward is told things.

*Notice of a first sitting of an emergency appeals board, at the second-floor hearing room, Bell Stair, on the ninth day of this month, at the eighth bell, notice being given seven days before; the parties being the office of the Deputy Settlement Steward, the ward of Tallow Ward by its captain, the Benevolent Fund of the Counter-Clerks, and the Chamber of Accounts.*

*Councilor Bel Harrow of the Crown March will preside at the first sitting.*

It carried no grounds and no terms and did not say what the board would do, and a woman at the front read it twice, then turned round and asked him in front of two hundred and thirty people what it did for her. Harrow told her the truth, which is that it put a date in a calendar, that the date was the ninth, and that after the ninth he did not know what happened.

And Marek Vale, standing at the end of the row with a book in his coat that nobody in the yard knew about and four things in it, understood the shape of the next eleven days at about the fourth bell, and it was this.

The bill came due on the twelfth. The board sat on the ninth. The cause was written on the twelfth by a man in a room he had no standing in. And on the second of this month his own name was going to come off a column in a desk's book, and the only thing left to him was to stand in a yard in the rain, where a man with a leather case had told four hundred people the size of what he had brought and had been believed, and it had bought them a date.

A date was not a decision. A date was the whole of what a procedure could do for anybody who had not already decided, and Marek had spent four years believing that a thing in a calendar was the same thing as a thing in a hand. It was not. He watched the yard go back to work on a wet morning with a date in its head, and the water still cost what it cost, and the four households still stood at the bottom of a roll, and nothing that had been promised was going to stop a clerk writing a reason in a box.
