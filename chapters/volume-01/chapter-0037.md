# Chapter 37 — A Thing With No Copy

The ward had been arguing about the cut order for three weeks and had never once tried the obvious thing, which was to ask for a copy, and on the tenth of next month Pella Rusk filled in the form.

It is a short form. It is the same form anybody in Morrowfen uses to ask for a copy of any record held by the Trust, and it has a box for the record and a box for the applicant's connection to it, and she filled in the record as *the order in a frame on the wall of the levy office, by which the district account is held to its presented figure*, which took her a long time to write because she had to be exact and she could not be exact, because there is no name on it. There is a date in the corner of it in Grale's hand and the office stamp, and the stamp is the only name it has.

She went to the Threshold at the first bell on the Saturday with three of the mothers, and Vray, and about forty people from the Gate who had heard on the Wednesday night that the schoolmistress was going to ask for the page.

The clerk at the intake floor read the form and then read the record box twice, and then he did the thing that a man in a queue behind you can hear perfectly well, which was to go quiet for four seconds.

"You are asking for a copy of a working instruction of the water office," he said.

"Yes."

"There is no such record."

"That is not what I asked. I asked whether there is a copy of it. There is a difference, and the difference is on this form, and you will find it in the fifth line, where it asks *what is your connection to the record*."

"Madam, I am not going to be—"

"Then say it in those words so that the person behind me has it, because there are forty of us and I am not the one who needs it."

The clerk, who was about twenty-three and had been on the floor for two months, said it in those words. *There is no such record in the possession of this office.* And then, being a decent clerk, he told her why, which nobody in the ward had known and which is the whole of the chapter.

"A copy of a cut order would be a second cut order. The water office is permitted one."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"That is what you have just said," said Milan Vray, "and I want you to understand that I have had a piece of paper nailed to a wall in my life and I have been angry about it for eleven years, and I never once thought of asking whether anybody else had one. You are telling me there is one. You are telling me we could all have had it."

"You are being extremely clever and I do not like it," said the clerk, "and it is also true, and I have to tell you that I have read that rule and I have never thought about it either, and I have been in this hall for two months."

---

They went back down the hill and up the causeway to the levy office at about the third bell, because Neris Kade had said one sentence in the corridor at the Threshold and the sentence had been *ask for the account*, and nobody had understood her and she had not explained, because explaining it is not her office.

The district account is a record. It is a record of a district. The parties to a district account are its ratepayers, and a ratepayer in Morrowfen may read a rate, and Rusk had been a ratepayer of this district for nine years and had never once asked, because it had never occurred to her that a woman who teaches in a ward is a ratepayer of a district account, and because the water office strikes a line without writing to the people the line is about, and a person who has never been written to does not know that a paper exists with their street on it.

Grale read the school's line out of the book standing up, with his finger on it, in about four seconds, and would not read anything else, and stood there while the yard did the rest.

The line is a sum. That is the finding of the morning and it took about eleven minutes for four people who had never seen a district account to arrive at it together in the middle of a wet yard.

"So the school is that much," said Rusk. "Not ninety-one children. That much. A sum."

"A sum, valued by the office, at the close," said Grale, "and the office values a school as a fixed thing because a school is a term and a term does not vary, and a yard repair varies with the winter, and a lamp varies with the season, and there is a line near the bottom of that page which does not vary at all, and the office does not know a single name on it, and I have cut that order four times in nineteen years."

"And what does that much buy," said Rezek, from the back, in a fuel-yard coat, with both hands in the front of it, because it was the ninth and he could not get them shut and had stopped trying about a week ago. "Mrs Rusk. What does that much buy, in children."

Rusk had the answer before he finished the sentence, and she did it in the yard, out loud, with a slate, in about two minutes, and she got it wrong twice and right the third time, and the yard let her.

"It buys places," she said. "That is the word the office uses and the office is right. A place is not a child. A place is a term, and the office values a term, and the number of children a ward can hold in a school is whatever the number of terms it can pay for comes to, and I have never once been told what that number is, and it is on a page in a building, and I have been teaching here nine years and I did not know until the second of the rains that my school was the cheapest line on it."

"Say the number," said Vray.

"The number is not a fact about a school," said Rusk. "The number is a fact about an account. And if I say it in a yard it is a number somebody repeats in a room with the yard left off it, and I will not do it, and I said on the thirtieth of the rains that I would not do it and I have not."

"Then I will," said Rezek.

---

Rezek could not write. He had been a fuel-yard foreman for twenty-two years and his assessment when he came in was *steady*, and the steadiness was a fast left hand and no right hand at all after the ninth, and he had spent three weeks being handed a slate and a charcoal by people who had begun, in the way of a yard, to be proud of him for it.

He did not write the number.

What he did was stand in the middle of a wet yard with his hands out in front of him, the way he had stood on the thirtieth of the rains in front of two hundred people with a clerk on a stool, and say:

"I have not held a cup since the ninth day of the rains. Three weeks. I have had a cloth round a beaker and a woman at the fuel-yard has been pouring it for me and she is fifty-one years old and she is not my wife. Nobody cut me. I want that said in this yard before anything else is said in this yard. Nobody in a good coat cut my hand. I have been a foreman for twenty-two years and I have taken a barrow off that basin with these hands every one of those years, and on the ninth of the rains I had been given a draw that was sixteen days against a sealed fourteen under a wet-weather rider, and I put my hand out in this yard on the thirtieth and it did not do what hands are for, in front of strangers, and a yard-man had to explain my hands to a clerk."

He put them down at his sides.

"So do not tell me about people who are cut. People are not cut. **Lines are cut.** And a line is cut by a man with a frame in a room, on the last working day of a month, and there is nobody in the room, and there is no notice, and there is no form for what the room does when it takes the money out of the wall and puts it in the place of the wall."

---

Pella Rusk said the number at the fourth bell, and she said it once.

She had it on the slate, and she had had it since the third bell, and she stood in the middle of the yard with a schoolmistress's slate held up the way she held it up in front of ninety-one children and did not raise her voice, and she said the school's line on the district account as a sum, and then she said what the office's own valuation of a place makes of that sum, which is the number of children the ward can hold in a school, and then she put the slate down.

Fourteen, and the yard heard it, and about a third of the yard said *fourteen* at once and two people said *what* and one woman at the back said it in a whisper, and then there was a silence of a length that Aldis Kern later described as about a quarter of a bell and that everybody who was in it remembered for the rest of their lives.

"Ninety-one," said Vray.

"Ninety-one," said Rusk. "I have ninety-one on a register and eleven chairs stacked on the last row because the term is shut again from the sixth, and my school is worth fourteen of them, and that is not a figure anybody cut. That is a figure that has been true since before I came here, and nobody knew, and I am the schoolmistress, and I have been in nine years of accounts and I have never been asked for a number, and I am not going to be asked for it again in a yard, because a number said in a yard is a number somebody repeats in a room with the yard left off it, and I said that on the thirtieth and I have not broken it."

"You have just broken it," said Neris Kade.

"I have just said it in a yard in the presence of a hundred and forty people and I am not going to say it twice and I am not going to say it in a corridor, and the only place it can be said properly is a room, with a chair in it, in front of the people it is about, and there is one of those on the sixteenth of this month, and the notice is nailed to a board at the bottom of this hill and it says *the day is not to be left at an office*." Rusk picked the slate up. "So that is where it goes. Not to me. I have been the one who says it for nine years and I am finished."

Marek stood at the end of the yard with the rain coming off the eaves and the two boards behind him, and understood the whole of the two weeks in about nine seconds and understood none of it in any way that would let him do a single thing about it.

He was the man in the building where the number had come from. He had gone and got it, on the thirtieth, in five days with two clerks and a field examiner who was not his, and he had put it into a finding under a seal, and it had cost nothing to put there and had cost two households their week and four hundred people their certainty, and it had produced a finding that said the ward drank four hundred and forty against a lawful hundred and sixty and was right about it. He could not make the number say anything else. He could not get the frame off the wall. He could not be in the room, and he could not be asked a question in it, and there was a woman on the second floor of the hall who had a case number in a book and a form with one box in it and a rule about witnesses that a suspended clerk had just made possible, and none of it touched the wall, and none of it touched the number fourteen, and the number fourteen was going to be said out loud in a room on the sixteenth by a schoolmistress, and he was not going to be in the room.

He had a second reason to be in a room now and it was not his and it was not a form. Two boards on a wall in a yard, in forty hands, in the rain, and neither of them could be carried anywhere.

---

The argument went on until the light went and it was not an argument anybody in the yard lost, because it was an argument nobody in the yard had been asked to have, and the two things that came out of it were both true and neither was a document.

The first was a sentence Pella Rusk said and which Grale, a district accountant of fifty-one, allowed to stand.

"A thing that cannot be given a copy is a thing that can be changed with nobody present," she said. "I have thought about that for three weeks and I want it written in a room, and I cannot write it in a room, and the reason I cannot is on a wall in a frame with a stamp on it and no copy in the city. So I am going to say it in a yard and I am going to say it every week until somebody is angry enough to write it down, and that is not a procedure, and I do not have anything better."

"Grale," said Neris Kade. "Will you say the line's position in a room."

"No."

"Why not."

"Because it is the ninth line, and the eighth, and the third, and I have cut it four times, and I have taken a rate off a school four times and told myself each time that the school was the cheapest line on the page, and that was true every time." Grale had his hand flat on the frame, which was still turned to the wall, and he did not turn it round. "And because I have a horror of something I cannot prove, and because the day I prove it, I have to stop. So I will not say it in a room, and I would be obliged if nobody in this yard decided that I was a coward, because I would have to say so, and I have not got the energy for it."

"Then say what you will say."

"I will say that it gets cheaper as it goes down, that it has got cheaper since before I came, that somebody worked that out about sixty years ago and has had the order out of order ever since, and that I have a suspicion about who and cannot prove one word of it." He took his hand off the frame. "That is all I have got and it is worth nothing and I have given it to you anyway, because you have come and asked me in a yard in the rain and nobody has done that in eleven years."

And the second thing that came out of the yard was nineteen people writing down a fifth answer to the column with nothing at the head of it, in nineteen hands, on the board at the Gate, under the yard roll and the oiled sheet and the two sheets of answers, and the heading was still blank, and there was going to be a vote about whether to fill it and the vote was going to be against.

---

Neris Kade caught Marek at the edge of the yard at the last of the light, with the rain starting again, and she did not ask him anything, which she had told him on the sixth she was going to do, and she told him why, and it was not warmth and it was not forgiveness and it was the closest thing either of them had said to anything.

"I have stopped asking you things," she said, "and you owe me a fact from the nineteenth of the rains and I have not asked for it once this week, and I want you to notice that I have not, and I want you to notice that I am not going to tell you what I am going to do about it either, because if I tell you, then from today on every time I fail to ask you it is a kindness, and I am not being kind to you. I am being careful with a record, and there is a difference, and the difference is that a kindness can be taken back."

"Kade."

"You can watch. You are welcome to watch, and it is more than the Fund's room would give you, and you are getting it because nobody in this yard believes for one minute that four hundred people in twenty lanes in three days had anything to do with a suspended clerk." She put her slate under her arm. "That is the last comfortable thing I am going to say to you this month and I would like it noted that I said it in a yard and not in a corridor."
