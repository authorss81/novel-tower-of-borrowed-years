# Chapter 8 — Six Bells

The copy room emptied at the sixth bell, the way it emptied every night, and by the time the lamps were turned down along the north wall Marek Vale had been sitting alone at the end of his bench for an hour with a spoiled requisition in his hand, a pen he had not dipped, and a case with a Tallow Ward label on it that he had taken out of the rack and put on his knees.

The requisition was a used one. A fuel-yard request for forty elvers of chain, sent up in the third week of a flood by somebody who had wanted the ink of it to be black, and returned because the yard had managed, and spoiled in the ordinary way, and destined for the scrap basket at the closing of the day. He had taken it out of the basket at the seventh bell and put his own mark on the back of it, which is not done, and he was aware of that, and he did it anyway.

He wrote out the terms in the order the Tower's forms keep them.

*Need.* One settlement: the Tallow Ward water assessment, season 412, posted on the eleventh day of the rains, due on the fourteenth day of the rains at the peak.

*Pledge.* None offered. The remedy takes no pledge. It is not a loan.

*Scope.* One settlement, across the boundary named in the assessment, which is the ward line at the Tallow Gate. The remedy is not to be extended to any other settlement, boundary, or contract, and takes nothing else with it when it ends.

*Witness.* None. There is no office to witness it. The clause does not provide for one.

*Settlement.* The day is moved to the end of the year. Every term of the contract stands. The collection is not stayed.

He read the last line of the clause again. The year. The remedy moved a day to the end of a year and a year was a year, and there was no office here to move it into, and he had worked out already what that meant and had not yet let himself finish the sum.

He read it back and it was a correct set of terms and it was the worst piece of work he had ever done, because he had written *Witness: none* in a form that had five parts, and had written it in a steady hand, and had known while he wrote it that the reason there was no witness was that there was nobody in the city with standing to give one.

Then he touched the requisition with two fingers, because that is what you do, and nothing whatever happened.

He sat with that for a while. No pain. No page. Nothing in his eye, nothing behind his temple, no flat quiet, nothing at all. He waited for his hand to shake, and it did not, and he found that he was disappointed, and then found that he was frightened of being disappointed, because a remedy that does nothing on a test page is either a dead remedy or a remedy that keeps its account for later.

He did not try it on a real instrument. He turned the idea over for an hour in a cold room and did not try it, and the reason was not caution. The reason was that he had worked out, somewhere between the seventh bell and the middle of the night, that he was not going to survive the not-trying.

Then Hesper came in.

He came in the way he came into everything, from his own door, and he did not turn on a lamp, and he went straight to the posting board and stood in front of it and looked at it, and Marek watched him look at it from the end of the bench, and the hall was so quiet that Marek could hear the Silt against the pilings four storeys down.

The board was otherwise empty. One settlement left on it for the week and it was the Tallow line, and the Tallow line said the fourteenth, and the hour column said the peak, because Hesper Rann, when he wrote a posting, wrote the hour and not the word, and the word had been *peak* in the ward's copy and the hour had been the fourth bell in his, and the fourth bell was three bells off and would come up somewhere in the middle of the night.

Hesper put his finger on it. He did not touch the board; he held his finger a knuckle's off the wood, in the air, over the Tallow line, close enough to read and too far to smudge, which is a thing a supervisor does with a line he has already settled.

"Sedge has gone home," he said.

"He's been gone since the second."

"Yes. And I have signed for the ward myself, which I have not done in nineteen years, and which I would not do for a warden who put his house back on the line." He took his hand away. "The ward is short at the peak and short by more than the yards can argue about. I have it in the ward's own count and it is in Marda Senn's count and it is in the yard counters, and every one of those three is a set of people who have no obligation to the tower and every one of those three says the same thing, which is the first time in my life that the ward and the tower have agreed about anything."

Hesper turned round. In the dark of the unlit hall his face was not readable, and Marek was glad of that, and understood twenty minutes later how completely he had been relying on it.

"You have been at that case all evening."

"It is a case with a schedule in it."

"It is." A pause. "It is also the case that you are a party to, and that you refused to be removed from at the fourth bell today, and that a Deputy Steward who has never in thirty years drafted a recusal put on your desk and then took it away again. Do you know what that is, Marek? That is a woman who has run out of instruments. She has nothing left to do with you. Everything after this is a person being reasonable at you."

"I know what it is."

"Do you." Hesper went to the door and stopped. "Then I will say the one thing I have to say and I will go to bed. The remedy in the margin of the oldest volume has a name. It has had a name for three hundred years and the name is not a power, and the office it names was retired in the third century and there has not been a clerk of record in this city since the two of us were nobody. If a man in a building takes that duty on tonight, there is no office to put the delay in. It goes into him. All of it, and a clerk of record would have had it in an office where a committee could argue about it, and there is nobody in this city who can be sent for. And it does not stop with him. A remedy does not change who owes what. Whatever the ward has been spared tonight is still owed at the sixth bell, and it will be collected out of whatever the Tower can reach, and a man who has read a chain knows that the Tower is not a patient thing."

He said that last part quite pleasantly, and then he said, before Marek could answer it: "I am telling you this because I would like you to have heard it from a man who signs things for a living rather than from the margin of a book. I am not telling you not to. I am telling you the shape of it. Go to bed."

He went out. The door came to, and Marek sat at the end of the bench with the requisition in front of him and the case on his knees and a nosebleed starting, and it had not started a moment before.


The peak came up the river before the first bell and did not stop.

That is a thing about the Silt in the ninth year of a bad decade: it does not rise and fall like a smaller river. It comes up the channel in one long shoulder and goes on standing, and the water in the low ground stops moving and starts pressing, and the Tallow Gate took the first of it in the small hours with a bang that could be heard four storeys up in a stone hall, and then took it, and kept taking it, and the whole building felt it in the floor.

Marek put the case open on the bench.

The entry lay on the top, with the recital and the fair fast hand and the pencil countersignature in the corner with the junior's name under it, and underneath it the labour schedule he had filed himself on the eleventh day with a docket note that was true, and under that the receipts, and under that the case lining.

He put his left hand flat on the entry, over the fourth clause, and it was the hand he was reading with, and the page was not paper and the clause was not a clause, and the numbers in the margin were not numbers.

> One settlement. Six bells. The delay is yours.

The pain came in behind his right eye like a driven nail and he made a sound he did not choose to make, and then everything became extremely clear.

Six bells was the whole of a watch. The tower kept two counts and would not mix them — eight for the day, from the first at the opening of the desks to the eighth in the morning after, and six for the night, from the last of the light to the light again — and the fourth of the night was where a river came up, and the sixth of the night was where a watch stopped being a watch. A remedy that put a settlement by the year had that one length to put it in. He had not chosen it. He had only found out what it was.

The hall did not go quiet. It went *specific*. The lamps along the north wall took on a hard edge, and the Tally Stair four storeys up took on a hard edge, and the ceiling beams took on a hard edge, and the edges did not move; the edges were the only thing in the building that would not move, and he understood that this was not a punishment and not a gift, and that it was simply the arrangement he had made, and that the arrangement was that for six bells he would have to be quite clear.

*One settlement. Six bells. The delay is yours.*

The first bell had gone. He had not felt it go. There was no chime in it, and the roof stair was a hundred feet away, and it was the second bell before he knew any time had passed at all, and by then the pain had come up out of the base of his skull and into the whole of the right side of his face and there was blood on his upper lip and on the back of his left hand and he had no idea which of the two had happened first.

The third bell. He counted them the way he counted columns in an account, which is to say he counted them the way a machine counts, and each one arrived as a fact with a distance attached: *the third is now, and the fourth is the peak, and the settlement is at the fourth, and the ward is at the fourth.* And between the third and the fourth he had a stretch of time that was entirely his own, and he used it, because he was a clerk and he had a bench and he did the only thing there was to do with it.

He read the file. All of it. Every leaf, from the fair fast hand at the top to the wax stitch at the bottom, twice, and where a line was long he followed it with his finger, and he wrote nothing down, because there was no point in writing anything down.

He had a bench and an open case and the length of one bell, and he spent it making a map of a document he already knew, because a man who is going to be asked what is in a file in a room ought to know what is in the file, and because he could not think of anything else, and because the alternative was to sit at the end of a bench and wait with his hand on a page.

Somewhere around the fourth bell the peak came through the gate and the sound of it changed and Marek felt the floor go, not much, a hand's breadth, and a pane of glass in the window at the end of the hall dropped out of its putty and fell on the flagstones and did not break, because the flagstones are four feet thick and everything you drop in the Tally Halls survives.

The fourth bell was the peak. The fourth bell was the hour in Hesper's posting, and the settlement was due, and he was holding it, and the ward's water did not improve by one single pint.

That was the thing. That was the whole of what he had done, and he made himself say it, out loud, in an unlit room, to nobody, because a man who has done a thing that costs something is entitled to know what he bought with it.

Not one pint. The Tallow Gate had shut and the low kitchens were on the basin and Ilya Senn's four men were moving water by hand and the Gate tank was filling at half the rate Petross Oleksen had promised and there was a storehouse in the Underbank that was going to be a storehouse of water by morning, and none of that was because of him. What was because of him was that at the sixth bell, in this room, a number was not going to be written on a board.

He had bought six bells in an empty building. In exchange, four hundred people were going to wake up on the fifteenth of the rains having drunk water that no office had authorized, from a valve a man had broken seven days earlier against a sealed order, on the strength of a remedy that had not been in use for three hundred years, applied by a third-grade clerk with no standing, no witness, and no authority of any kind, to a settlement on a piece of paper he had not been given the right to hold.

Somewhere in the middle of the fifth bell he found that he had stopped being able to feel his left hand, and the page had gone from not-paper to something he could not describe, and the entries in the file had stopped being entries and had become a single vertical line of marks going up the page and off the top, and the line was not asking him anything.

By the fifth his nose had stopped bleeding and started again twice, and the hall had gone very far away, and he could not see the ends of things, and there was a taste of iron and a taste of the lamp oil and a third taste he had no name for.

And then the time went.

Not quickly. That was the thing he would have wanted to remember afterwards, and it was the thing he could not: it did not rush, it did not swim, it simply stopped arriving. He had been in the copy room for a length of time that he could not state, and he had been there for a length of time that he could not state, and both of those were true, and between them was a stretch he had no access to at all, and the stretch began somewhere around the fifth bell and it had a beginning, because he could remember the fifth bell going up the stair, and he could remember almost nothing after it.

He worked out afterwards, from the lamp oil in the reservoir, that something had happened in that stretch. He never found out what.

He sat at the end of his bench with his hand on the page and did not take it off, because taking it off was a decision and he was not making decisions in that condition; the entry had gone back to being a page some time in the middle of the lost stretch and had stayed a page, and his hand was still flat on the fourth clause, and his head was a tunnel with a wall at each end and a shape swimming about four inches in front of his eyes, and his mouth was full of iron, and his hand on the bench was shaking so hard that the pen had rolled off it and gone on rolling and had stopped against the skirting.

He wiped his face on his sleeve, and then he sat still for as long as he could, and then he looked at the Tally Stair.

The copy room door stood open. The lamp on the stair landing had been turned down and there was a voice on the stairs, and it was the posting desk's night woman, and she was saying something about copying, and the something was: *— suspended, so it cannot be taken, and the ward's got a claim on a copy before the morning, and the ward's got a hearing tomorrow, and if I put a suspension notice on a ward's levy without a copy of it, somebody in that room is going to tell me I never gave them anything —*

*— Tower won't take it. That's the rule. You can't copy a held settlement, it's not a state that's got a copy, they never made it to be a state —*

*— then it's by hand. That's by hand. It's all by hand now. That's the whole levy by hand on the Stair, eleven pages and the schedule, and I've got the duty clerk and I've got nobody else, so —*

Marek listened to it from the end of the bench with blood on his sleeve and a tunnel round the edge of his head.

Then the stair door opened the rest of the way, and the posting desk's woman came in with the notice-board spike in her hand, and said, "Mr Vale. Are you the Tallow clerk."

"Yes."

"Then I need a hand on this and I need it now, because the Tower won't take it and the ward's entitled." She was holding a board and a sheet and a bucket of the copyroom's heavy ink, and she had the air of a woman who has been doing this job for twenty-two years and has stopped expecting anybody to help. "Who can write the ward's levy out longhand? It's eleven pages and the schedule and the roll. It has to be somebody who can read a hand, because the original's a hand and the copy has to be the same hand, that's the rule for a copy. You did the roll at the Threshold. Can you do eleven pages in what's left of the night?"

"I have to be at the hearing at the eighth bell tomorrow."

"I know you have. I'm not asking you about tomorrow." She was already turning to go. "Kelb's on till the fourth of the morning. Kelb's the fastest hand in this building and he is twenty years old and he will do it for the honour. I'll send up for him."

And Marek Vale sat at the end of his bench with a tunnel round the edge of his head, and was aware, in the way you are aware of weather, that he had not been entirely present for some part of the last hour and could not have told anybody what had happened in it, and that somewhere inside that hour a woman had said a boy's name out loud in a room with a man in it.

He could have said the boy's name. It was two feet in front of him. He had four years in the hall and the best eye in the building for a hand on a page and the whole of the ward's levy in his head, and the copy would have gone into Kelb's hand as a gift instead of a punishment, and there was time, and he was a clerk, and it was a small thing.

He said nothing.

The posting desk went out. He heard her boots go up the stair, and stop, and come back down, and go up again, and there was a young voice on the landing, surprised and pleased, saying: *— the whole levy? In longhand? On the Stair? With the witness column, or—*

*— with the witness column.*

*— right. Right. I'll want a clean sheet, the lamp to be turned—*

*— I'll want the lamp turned down, sir, your eyes—*

And Marek sat in the unlit copy room with the case open in front of him and the Tallow line on the posting board, and the fifth bell gone, and the sixth not come yet, and no idea at all what the hour between them had contained.
