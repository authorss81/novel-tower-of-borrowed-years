# Chapter 50 — The Line He Leaves Open

The copy room on the last evening of the rains had the lamps going along the north wall and the rain not doing anything, as it had not done anything for most of a month, and Marek Vale had the oldest reference volume out of the case and shut on the table in front of him, and he was not the clerk of the day for anything, and there was nobody in the building who would come and close it.

He had a sheet of the public register ruled and a pen and about four hours, and he had told himself, on the stair, in the way he told himself things now, that this was a hand-copy of a single page, and that he could do a page in an hour, and that it would take four, because it was the first page and because he was going to do it properly and there is no form for doing it properly except the one he was about to break.

The standing order on the sealed founding page is in two parts and he had it by heart, which is not a thing you are supposed to be able to say. No clerk may erase the signature. And no clerk may copy the page.

He copied the page.

It is a short page. It has a head-line, the office's oldest hand, and beneath the head-line a single ruled line with nothing at the end of it, and beneath the line a signature, and in the outer margin four words about a remedy held by an office that has not been held since the third century. He wrote the head-line first, in his own hand, and then he came to the line.

He put the nib on the ruled line and he sat there, and the lamps along the north wall went on doing their business, and he understood for the first time in five weeks what Hesper Rann had been doing on the ninth of the rains with a lamp in his teeth: that a man looks at a line that has got nothing in it and has to decide, in a room, on his own, whether to put something there, and that the whole of the tower's arithmetic came out of a man putting a number in a line he had not read.

He wrote a figure. He wrote the first stroke of a four and then he stopped and took the pen off the page and put the cap on it, and he sat with his hands on the table for about four minutes.

Then he took the cap off again, and he did not blot it, because a blot lifts the grain and that paper is three hundred years old, and he went along the four he had started once with the blade of the small knife that lives in the drawer of every desk in that room, and stopped where the stroke stopped, so that the line was blank again and clean, and the page said what it has said for three hundred years, and that is the whole of what he did with it, and nobody in the building saw him do it.

Then he wrote the signature underneath, and this is where the standing order stops mattering.

A clerk is taught, in the first three weeks of the post, that when a document carries a signature that is not the clerk's, the signature is written out in full — every letter, every figure, every stroke, in the order it is in the original — and beside it, in the clerk's own hand, the words *written in full*, and the date, and the clerk's own name and no office. It is not a forgery and it is not a copy of a signature. It is the only way anybody in this building is permitted to put one hand next to another, and the reason for it is that a shorthand mark on a page is a thing a person can put on a page.

So he wrote it out, letter by letter, and then he wrote the two marks, and then the figure, and then the stroke.

A, a stroke, and zero. A-0, written out in full and letter by letter, because that is what a clerk does with a hand that is not the clerk's.

*Written in full. The eighteenth day of next month. M. Vale, clerk, no office.*

And then he read the line back and did not change one word of it, because the clerk of record has not been held since the third century and there is nobody in Bell Stair who can be it and there is no form for appointing one, and a man in a hall with no office of his own is the truest thing available and it is also the only one that is not a lie.

The Trust's sealed notice on the Tally Hall door says the public copy is to be replaced, that the founding hand is not to be reproduced in it, and that the work is to be carried out by the officer of the record and by no other hand. There is no officer of the record. There has not been one for three centuries. The Deputy's office is not the record and the Chamber is not the record and a suspended clerk is nobody's clerk, and the notice has therefore been on that door for fourteen days with nobody able to carry it out, and the public register has an empty page in it where the oldest page should be, and an empty page with nothing under it is not a blank line with a signature on it. It is a blank page, and anybody at all may write in it, and in about nine months somebody will, in the ordinary way, for a good reason, with no idea whatever.

That is what Marek Vale understood on the stair, and it is the only thing he has understood about the ninth of the rains that the ninth of the rains was about.

He copied the page and he wrote the signature in full and he entered it in the public register on the table by the window, where the register lies open all day and anybody who comes up the hill can read it, and the lamps along the north wall were on the whole time, and at the sixth bell of the night he shut the case and put it back and it took him longer to get the case shut than it had to do the page.

---

The girl came up at the fifth bell because her mother was at the seamstress's and there was nowhere for her to be, and the duty clerk on the posting desk let her in, because the duty clerk has let children into that room for twenty-two years and has never once written down the fact that she does it.

She is Lio, she is nine, and she is off the school roll and the term shut on the sixth and nobody in Tallow Ward knows when it opens, and she came and sat on the bench at the copy room's end with a slate and did not ask him anything for about twenty minutes, which is the single most restful thing that has happened to Marek Vale in a month.

Then she said, "You have written the empty one."

"I have."

"You did not put a number in it."

"No."

"Why."

And he could have said four things. He could have said it was a standing order. He could have said it was three hundred years old. He could have said a man in a good coat had spent a year and a half of wages trying to make a blank line say what it did not say. He could have said that her grandmother's household is the seventh of eleven on a roll in a file in Bell Stair, and he did not say any of the four, and what he said was the only true thing he had, and he said it to a nine-year-old on a bench in a copy room with the lamps going.

"Somebody has to write down what the tower owes," he said. "That is the whole of it. Every register in this building is a list of what has been given and what has been taken, and the taking is always written down before anybody has looked at what the giving was, and that is the mistake and it has been the mistake for a very long time, and a man who writes a number in an empty line so that the page balances has not fixed it. He has only moved the emptiness.

"I do not know whose year the empty one is. I have been asked that by better people than you and I have had a month to work it out and I cannot, and I am not going to invent one, because a man who invents a year is doing in one stroke the thing that whole district account has been doing for three hundred years, and it is much easier and it is more expensive and nobody ever notices until the invoice."

She thought about that for a while, and then she said, "Whose year is it."

"I do not know," said Marek Vale. "Nobody does. The office has been empty for three hundred years and there is no form for filling it and there is nobody who can appoint one, and the Trust's notice on the door downstairs has been asking for me to do a job that only that person can do, and I have been standing under that notice for fourteen days and I am not going to go and ask anybody why, because the answer would be that nobody knows, and I would have to carry that about as well."

"Then whose year is the empty one, and who is going to write the next page?"

"I am," said Marek Vale, "and the line is not going to be closed while I am the clerk of record."

He said it, and he heard himself say it, and he did not take it back, and there was nobody in that room to take it back in front of, and it was the last thing he said that evening, and it is in nobody's record, because he had no office to file a record in, and because the bench at the end of the copy room is not a table and a slate is not a document and a nine-year-old is not a party to anything.

He had not said it in a room in Bell Stair. He had not said it to Harrow or to the Deputy or to a board of five in a yard. He had said it to his brother's daughter, on a bench, at the sixth bell of the last evening of the rains, in a building he was not clerk of anything in, and it was a thing a clerk says about himself.

---

The lamps were lit along the Tallow Gate that night and the four yards were running on the public levy and the schedule on the board at the tank had four columns in it and about two hundred names, and the Ropewalk standpipe was short again and had been boiled on the ration for a fortnight, and a rope-maker's condemned storehouse was still under nine feet of water in a cellar in the Tanner's yard with the pump on it and the water office holding no record of condemned ward property because condemnation is not its business.

The public register was on the table by the window with a new page in it. It said what the oldest page says, and then it said a signature in full, and then it said *written in full*, and a date, and a name with no office after it, and anybody who came up the hill that week could stand at that table and read it, and about forty people did, and there is no register of who they were because there is no form for a person who reads a page.

The Trust's sealed notice stayed nailed to the Tally Hall door, and it was not taken down, and it was not signed by anybody who will admit to having read it.
