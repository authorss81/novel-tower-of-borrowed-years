# Chapter 6 — The Standing Reserve

There were three things wrong with the water, and Marek spent the whole of the twelfth day finding out that he had come down to look for the wrong one.

He went down at the last light with the Tallow case in a leather sleeve under his arm, because he was a clerk who did not trust a file he had not walked, and because the standing reserve was a recital in that file, and because a recital is a promise about money and money has to be found somewhere.

The promise was in the fourth clause, in the same fair, fast, confident hand as the labour schedule, and it read: *the whole of the sum is offset against the standing reserve of the water office, and no household named in this assessment is drawn upon for the season.*

That was the sentence. Twenty-two words, and it meant that the four yards paying this season would pay nothing, and that the seven not paying this season would be asked for nothing at all, ever, and that the ward's bill was therefore a document with a good deal of white space on it and a signature at the bottom.

He wanted to find the reserve.

The Gate yard was on the top of the ward where the levee met the low kitchens, and the tank there was half. Marek had seen it dry on the ninth day at dusk. He stood and looked at it for a while in the rain and then did the thing he had been wanting to do since he was seventeen and had learned that numbers can be made to agree by people who are not liars, only busy.

He walked the run.

The trunk main came up out of the low ground at the second junction, painted red at the top, and it had a meter on it, and beside the meter there was a standpipe and a board. The board was slate, and on the slate there were numbers in chalk in a small upright hand, forty-one days of them, in columns, and the last nine days were in a different colour because they were in a different month of the rains.

He stood in front of it for a long time.

"That is a private board," said a voice behind him.

Marek turned round. A woman of perhaps fifty, in a Warden's coat with the sleeves turned back, a slate dust on one forearm, standing in the pump-house door with a lamp in her hand.

"Warden's clerk," she said. "Marda Senn. And you are the Chamber clerk who wrote our forty-one petitions in a book in the order they arrived."

"Yes."

"Marek Vale. Sill Row. Seventh of eleven." She said it exactly as it was written on the roll, without any weight on it, and then she moved past him to the board and set the lamp on the sill. "You have come about the reserve."

"I have."

"Then you have come about the wrong thing, and I am going to tell you about the right one, and then you may decide what you think of me." She put her finger on the top of the left-hand column. "That is the delivery from the junction, in hundreds of pints, chalked every hour since the second day of the rains by a Warden whose name is Ilya Senn, because there was a season twenty-two years ago when the junction meters disagreed with the yards by a third and a man named Vollner was dismissed for it and the truth of that was never established. That is the right-hand column. That is what the four yards actually took, at the yard counters, from four sets of yard clerks who are not paid by the Wardens and who keep their own books because they have never once in my life been trusted with a number by anybody in a building."

She put her hand on the two columns.

"Read the ninth day of the rains. Nine is on the right. It is on the left. The left says the junction delivered eleven hundred and forty. The yards took seven hundred and ninety. I have chalked the difference every hour for nine days and it is never less than a third and on the sixth and the eighth it is more than a half."

Marek looked at the two numbers for a long time.

"Third and a half," he said.

"Close to forty per cent, if you like it round."

"The meter is over-reading."

"The meter is over-reading," Marda Senn said, "and it has been over-reading since the freshet, and I have said so in writing to the Warden's office twice and to the water office once, and the water office is Crown Terrace, and the Crown Terrace water office does not read Warden's memoranda, it reads the Wardens' *counts*, and the counts go to the account, and it is the account, not my slate, that says Tallow Ward is drawing four times its standing schedule. Four times, clerk. My chalk says two and three-quarter times at the junction, on a week when the low kitchens have been boiling their pans on a ration."

"Four times."

"In the ninth day of a flood, in a ward where the low kitchens have been boiling their pans on a ration since the fourth day, and the Gate tank was dry on the ninth and is half now, and the Tanner's is low, and Ropewalk has been dry since the sixth." She took her hand off the board. "Your assessment says the ward is drawing four times its standing schedule, and your assessment is therefore justified, and the justification is a number in your own file, and the number is a lie, and it is not a lie anybody made. It is a stuck strainer at the second junction and a bypass somebody set the wrong way round when they reset it after the freshet, and it is a lie that has been standing in the rain for nine days being *true-looking*."

Marek had the case in his hands. He put it down on the pump-house step, and took out the entry, and opened it to the fourth clause, and set it on the board's ledge beside the slate.

Then he read the reserve recital out loud in the rain, which he had never done in his life, to a Warden's clerk with slate dust on her arm.

Marda Senn listened to the whole of it with her head slightly down, and when it was finished she said, "No."

"That is not a—"

"I know what that is. It is a note that says the bill is paid." She lifted the lamp off the sill and held it up so that they both had light on the page. "Look at the top of the sheet, clerk, where the page numbers are. One, two, four, five. You noticed that on the tenth and you wrote it in a book, and the ward wrote it on the ward's copy, and it went up the hill and nowhere. Now look at the *reserve*. The reserve is a recital in the entry. A recital is not a page. But look here—"

She put her finger on the margin of the entry, beside the fourth clause, where in the fair hand there was a small bracketed mark and a figure: *(3)*.

"—there is a figure in the margin and I cannot read what it points at, because it points at a page number and a page number in a file of this size means nothing to a woman with a lamp. What I will tell you is what it is not. It is not the schedule. The schedule is the thing they mean to pay with, and this is the thing they say they will not have to pay with, and nobody in the history of this basin has ever said the second thing and meant it." She straightened up. "Whatever that figure points at is where the reserve is, and it is not in your hand, and it is not in mine, and it is not in that file of yours unless you can get into the vaults tonight."

"I have the file."

"Then you have a schedule and I have a question," she said, "and neither of those is an answer."

"I am not allowed to take a schedule out of a certified case," Marek said.

"Then take a lamp and read it where it sits, and I will stand here in the rain, because I have spent two days watching a lie go into an account and out of an account and into your file and out of your file, and I would very much like to know whether the account is the only place it has been."

So they went up.

It was half past the last light and the causeway was empty and loud, and at the sluice end of the ward line there was a man in a Warden's coat standing on the platform of the Third Sluice with a hand-wheel in both hands, doing nothing at all, which was the most conspicuous way of doing nothing that Marek had ever seen.

"That is Ilya," Marda Senn said, "and you will want to be careful, and I will tell you why in a moment, and I want you to hear the whole of it and then I want you to do your job."

Ilya Senn was forty-one and looked fifty and had a lantern hooked to his belt and a folded paper in the inside of his coat that he took out and handed to Marek without being asked, which told Marek that Marda Senn had told him somebody was coming up the hill who would be holding a file.

"Deputy Steward's order," Ilya said. "Third Sluice to be closed at the seventh bell and held through the peak for the Crown Terrace intake. Signed, sealed, dated the seventh. Read it and then tell me what your ward's water is doing."

Marek read it. It was exactly what it said it was. The order was in order. The valve was open, and had been open for five days, and there was a chalk mark on the frame with a date, and the date was the seventh.

"Your order is the seventh," Marek said.

"My order is the seventh," Ilya agreed. "And the sluice has been open since the second, and I have been on this platform since the second, and I have four men with me and we have been moving the low kitchens by hand off the basin, and the reason I have not closed it is that if I close it on the seventh at the seventh bell, the low kitchens at the Tallow Gate have no water at all, and there are three hundred people in them, and two hundred of them are children, and I have a count of them in my coat here."

He tapped the coat pocket that had held the order.

"You are going to ask me whether I have authority to do this," he said. "I have none. There is a Crown Terrace intake at the head of this channel, full of water that came out of the Tallow basin, and I have been taking it out of the channel and putting it into the ward since the second day of the rains, in full view of a Crown Terrace water-clerk, who has written me four letters, which I have kept. One of them is the reason I know the order will be followed by an inspection rather than a conversation. So: no. Write it down. Somebody has to own it. If a thing is written nowhere, then in six weeks a man will stand on this platform and say that nobody ever told him about a valve, and he will be right, and there will be three hundred people in the low kitchens with an empty bucket."

Marek had the file under his arm and the order in his hand and there was a thing he had come down here to do, and for a moment he could not remember what it was, and then he remembered, and it was to file an objection, and an objection was a piece of paper.

He put the order back into Ilya's hand. "I will record that the Third Sluice has been open against an order of the seventh, with the sluice operator named."

"That is correct."

"That will go on your record."

"Yes," Ilya said. "Write it on your own arm if you cannot find the paper. It is not a complaint. It is a fact and it is mine and I have earned it. If you put it in a file and it is read in March by a man who was not here, it will be the only reason there is one."

They walked back up together through the rain, Marda Senn a little ahead with the lamp, Ilya behind with his hands in his pockets, and Marek in the middle with a case, and it occurred to him somewhere on the Ropewalk that he had just been given, by two people who owed him nothing, the two facts that made his file legible, and that neither of them had asked him for anything, and that he was going to go and do something neither of them had asked him for.

On the causeway he stopped and said, "How long has the ward been fed off the basin?"

"Since the second," said Ilya.

"And the certification goes live at first light tomorrow and it will certify the ward's delivery at the standing schedule, which is a third more than the yards can take, which the file says is a third less than they are taking."

"Yes," said Ilya. "Which is why your file is right and your ward is thirsty, and both of those are true, and the second of them is the reason the first one is available."

Then they were on the island.

The Season Vaults were at the back of the tower, down a stair that got colder as it went, and the reference case was in the third alcove and it was a plain deal case with a hasp and a lead seal that had been broken and re-broken so many times that the lead had a lip like a mouth.

The vault clerk of the night was a young man with a cold and a key on a string and no interest whatever, and Marek stood in front of the case with the sleeve under his arm and the order on the seventh in his pocket and thought: *a page. It is a page and I can look at it in this building and be certain of the thing that is on it.*

The volume came out heavy and dull and the boards were gone and it was a sewn ledger with a limp sheepskin cover and it was, he understood as soon as it was in his hands, the oldest reference volume in the tower, which was the oldest surviving thing in Morrowfen that anybody was permitted to touch.

The first page was blank.

Not empty. Blank, in the way a page is blank when a line has been ruled and then left: there was a rule at the top in faded brown, and the rule ran the full width, and beneath the rule there was the space where an opening entry went, and the space was the space for a first entry, and there was nothing in it at all.

Beneath the blank, in a hand he had only ever seen on one other document in his life, which was the Tower's own founding schedule, there was a signature: **A-0**.

The two characters were cut rather than written, with the blunt of a knife and a steady hand, and they were the oldest ink in the room, and every clerk in Morrowfen could read them and not one of them could tell you who they belonged to. The instruction was at the foot of the page, in a later hand, in the flat bureaucratic register of a standing Trust order: *no clerk may erase this signature, and no clerk may copy this page.*

And the same standing order was what had kept the page untouched in practice for three hundred years, which was a thing Marek noticed standing there in a cold vault with a lamp, and which was the first genuinely frightening thing he had encountered since the ninth day.

In the margin, in the old hand, in ink the colour of weak tea, was a remedy.

He read it four times.

*No settlement shall be taken before the year is ended. The remedy is a standing hold. It is held by the clerk of record. It changes the day and nothing else. Every term of the contract stands and the collection is not stayed. It is not a release and it is not a forgiveness and it does not touch the pledge. It moves the day to the end of the year and the party who takes it holds the delay in his own yearline, because there is no other place in this building to hold a delay.*

*Clerk of record. The office is retired. It is vacant. It has been vacant since the third century of the Reckoning.*

Marek stood with his hand on the page.

The ache came up in his temple and went into the eye on the same side, and he saw the page go flat and quiet the way a page does, and the old ink came up out of the sheepskin like weather coming up out of the ground, and what he got was four words and then nothing at all, and this time the nothing was the answer.

> The page agrees with itself.

The vault came back. The young man with the key was still there. The lamp was still in Marek's own hand because he had taken it out of the alcove without deciding to, and his hand was shaking, and the headache was behind his eye and down the side of his face, and the page was a page.

He read the reserve line by hand, in the fourth clause, in the margin, in the citation at the foot of the entry, which was a superscript in the levy office's brown: *held against the standing reserve, fn. 1.* And footnote one was on the same page, in the same small hand, and it said, in full:

*Held against the standing reserve. The standing reserve is the first entry of this volume.*

And the first entry of this volume was the blank.

There was no reserve. There was a recital and a footnote and a rule that sent you to the oldest page in the tower, and on the oldest page in the tower there was a line for the first entry, and the first entry had never been written, and beneath the line a man whose name nobody knew had signed his initials in a knife-cut and then, in the margin, written down the one thing he had thought of, which was a way of stopping a settlement, and then somebody had put a Trust order on the page and gone away, and for three hundred years every levy in the city had been able to lean on that blank line the way a hand leans on a wall.

Marek stood in the vault with the finding in his hands, and it was a page, and it was nothing.

The objection he had come down to file would have gone to the posting desk and would have been read by a woman copying since the first bell, and it would have said that the standing reserve is not a sum, and the posting desk would have referred it to the levy office, and the levy office would have said that the reserve is an accounting matter and not a settlement matter, and it would have taken some weeks, and in some weeks the ward would be thirsty and the peak would come and go.

So he copied it.

He copied the footnote, and then, because he had already read it, because a footnote without the page it points at is a sentence, he copied the margin as well, in his own small hand, on the back page of his own book, and he did it standing up in a cold vault with a lamp in his teeth, and it took eleven minutes, and it is the only time in his life that he broke a written instruction of the Yearspire Trust, and he did it for the worst possible reason, which was that he could not think of anybody who would arrive in time.

He put the volume back. He put the case back and the hasp down and told the vault clerk he had read the reference volume, which was a permitted thing to do and the only thing he was ever going to say about it, and he went up the cold stair and out into the rain on Stillstep Island at about the eleventh bell, and the wind took the lamp out of his hand and put it out.

Hesper Rann was on the causeway steps with his coat open and no umbrella, in a rain that had been falling for nine days, and he had been there long enough to be soaked through.

"Twelve hours," Hesper said, without preamble. "I have been standing on this stone for twelve hours waiting for you to come up this stair, and I would like that entered somewhere, because nobody in this building has ever waited for anybody for twelve hours and I am forty-six and my knees are a matter of public record."

"You could have written a note."

"I could have. I did not want to write a note about where you had been." He came up the last three steps. "Listen. There is a certification going live at first light and Vole's office wants your name on it, and I have put you down for it. Certify it, and the ward's delivery is certified at the standing schedule tomorrow, and the settlement draws the difference on the fourteenth, and the settlement is due, and it is drawn from eleven households — and there is a copy of the paper in that case which says no household named in this assessment is drawn upon for the season."

He said that last part lightly. He said it the way a man says a thing he has read out before and knows to be true.

Marek stood on the causeway steps in the rain and worked it out, because there was not enough in him any more to be angry with.

Hesper's lie had not manufactured a shortage. The ward was short, the river was up, the tank on the Gate had been dry, and there were three hundred people in the low kitchens being carried off a basin by hand by a man who was breaking a sealed order five days into it. The lie had done one thing: it had made the ward's bill look empty.

And the reason the ward's bill had to look empty was in the entry itself, in the first line, in the same fair hand, which is the line where a ward entry names the instrument it is answering.

The instrument was a gate.

The Tallow Gate was a floodgate and it was closed and it was supposed to be shut on the fourteenth night, and a shut gate is a settlement, and a settlement needs a pledge, and the pledge had to be a ward, and the ward's pledge was the eleven households, and the ward had to be told and given thirty days, and the thirty days ran out on the twelfth. The need in the entry was the ward's water. The thing behind the need was the gate.

That was the clock. There was a clock on it the whole time. There had been a clock on it from the sixth bell of the ninth day, when a senior clerk had taken an unassigned file off a bench because the gate loan had to be sealed inside the notice period or the gate could not be shut on the fourteenth, and the gate not shut on the fourteenth meant the low kitchens and the Fuel basin under eleven feet of the Silt by the morning of the fifteenth.

And the lie was not *safety*. If the reserve had been real, the ward would have paid nothing and the gate would still have been shut and the reserve would have been somebody's future somewhere. The lie moved the cost off the page where the ward would have refused it and put it in a column on a page the ward had never been given, and it bought a gate.

It bought the ward its own floodgate, and it charged the ward for it in a currency the ward would not see, and it did it in the last two days of a notice period, in the rain, with four hundred people in the aisle at the Threshold, and Hesper Rann had done it with one invented line in a fair hand and a boy's pencil at a counter-desk.

Marek understood, standing on the steps, that he was not going to be able to write an objection that said Hesper Rann was a liar. He was not going to be able to say it to anybody, ever, in any room, without somebody standing up and saying: whose gate?

It was the first time in his life that understanding something had made him worse.

"I will need to see the certification before I sign it," he said.

"You will see it at the first bell, and you will sign it or you will not, and if you will not then I will take your name off it and put Sedge's on and Sedge will sign it in the dark and the ward will get its water and be short, and you will be a third-grade clerk who watched a ward go short on a Tuesday and had a file under his arm." Hesper put his hands in his pockets. "And Marek. Do not go down there again tonight. Go home. You are chasing something in the vaults and there is nothing in the vaults. There has never been anything in the vaults. The vaults are where they keep the paper that the tower is proud of."

Then he went down the steps, and Marek went up them, and the rain came sideways off the parapet, and it was at the top of the stair, with the lamp out and the noise of the water going over the causeway beneath him, that Marek noticed that he had lost a piece of the last quarter of an hour.

He could not get it back. It was not there, the way a tooth is not there. He had gone out of the vault and up the stair and been in the rain and had a lamp in his hand that he did not remember taking, and there was a stretch of the causeway, of the wind, of his own boots on wet stone, of nothing, and the whole of the missing piece was gone the way his midday bread had gone on the tenth, except that the bread had been an ordinary thing and this was an hour of his own life with a man in it.

He stood on the causeway in the rain and worked out that Hesper had said something to him on the steps, and that the something was gone, and that Hesper had known he would not be able to remember it, and that Hesper had therefore said it.

Then he went home through the Standing Market with his hand shut over the book in his coat, and the book had a footnote and a margin in it that no living clerk was permitted to have, and a posting on the board that he had signed with his own name at the first light of the eleventh day, and a boy of twenty at a counter-desk whose pencil had been the last mark on the totals of an entry that had been true in every sentence and false in everything it left out.
