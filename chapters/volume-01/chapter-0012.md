# Chapter 12 — What a Ward Is

Nadja Ostrow was standing at the clinic door with a folded apron over her arm and had been standing there for the length of time it takes to decide whether to go in, which is a thing Marek had watched about four hundred women do in eleven days and had never got used to.

She was thirty-eight and she cooked at the low kitchens at the Tallow Gate, and she was one of the eleven households on the roll, which is a piece of paper she had read in a queue like everybody else and had understood precisely as much of as anybody else in the ward understood of it.

"You're the one that wrote the book," she said.

"I wrote the book."

"Then you're the one that's on it." She did not make it an accusation. She said it the way a person states the weather. "I've got the Ropewalk line, the cook's line, and it's me on it because I'm the one they can put a name to, and I've been standing here since the fourth bell working out whether I go in that door to say it."

"Do you want to say it to me or to somebody who can do anything about it?"

"I don't know. That's the honest answer and I can't get past it. So I'll say it to you, because you're the one on the list as well, and you can carry two."

"Go on."

"What I want to know is whether I owe it." She shifted the apron. "Nine days of me boiling and washing and hauling water for three hundred people out of a basement, and there's a line in a book that says Ropewalk, cook, and a number of days against it, and a page I never got that's got the days written out. That's a wage. A wage is work done now for money now. Those are days in the future. I have four children and a man with a chest on him and I have not got four years to hand anybody in the spring, and I didn't know about that page, and I heard about it in a yard at six this morning, and the thing I heard had already been round the ward twice by the time it got to me, and not one word of it was about me."

"Yes," Marek said. "You owe it, as the paper is written."

"Then say it back in words, in front of that door, so I can hear it."

He said it back in words, in front of that door, standing in the wet, for about forty seconds, and she listened the whole way through with the folded apron getting wet in her hand, and then she said "Right," and went and got on with her morning.

That was the whole of Nadja Ostrow's business at the clinic door, and Marek thought about it for the next two hours while he walked up the Basin road behind a crowd of forty people who had made up their minds about something.

The ward came at him in pieces. That is what a ward is; it is not a district on a map, it is forty kitchen yards and one gate and one standpipe, and it arrives at you as four separate smells.

Reeking of the tanneries first, then wet ash from the fuel yards, then the low kitchens' fat and salt, and under all of it the cold green smell of standing water that has been in cellars too long. The road up Sill Row had four inches of mud on it and a channel down the middle that somebody had cut on the sixth and that the yard captains had argued about on the seventh and had then dug out again on the ninth because arguing had stopped being useful. The Gate tank was full. That was the first thing you saw and it was the reason the yard was quiet; a full tank in the fifteenth of a flood is a thing that stops an argument halfway through.

Aldis Kern had his three captains in the Gate yard with the tank's hatch up and a lamp hooked to the frame, and the yard was packed.

He had a sheet of paper in his hand and he read it out loud.

"Loud and slow, and don't let anybody talk over the end of a line," he said to the yard before he started, and then he started, and he read the way a man reads who has had sixteen years to be unfair and has spent them being exact.

"First list. What we got on the fourteenth night. Ninety-one people out of the low kitchens and dry beds in the school house for a day and a half, which is not a triumph, it is a Monday. No child drowned. No yard lost. Petross's screen holding and the Gate tank filling at half a rate and it filled. A hundred and forty-odd of us carried water off the basin by hand in the low kitchens from the second night onward, which I am putting down here because nobody else is going to put it down. That is the first list."

He turned the sheet over.

"Second list. What we did not get. Nothing off the bill. Not one pint of that water has gone against this ward's account, and it does not now, and it will not, and I'll have the account in front of that room in a fortnight. The account is *worse* for us than it was on Friday, and I want everybody here to hear me say that out loud, because a yard that only knows the first list is a yard that gets robbed twice. The screen is holding so the meters are honest now, and the honest number is two and three-quarter times our standing schedule, and the paper used to say four, and four was the lie and two and three-quarter is the truth, and I do not have a single thing to say to anybody that makes that better."

"Rezek's hands," somebody said.

"Rezek's hands," Kern agreed. "I will come to Rezek's hands. Here's the other of it. There's a school on Sill Row and the district account pays for it and the district account is what this levy is drawn out of, at the ordinary wage, by the day. The account is overdrawn. It is three and a half times overdrawn, and at the end of the month the office draws the days up, and when the office draws days up it does not come to a yard and ask. It takes the cheapest line in the account. That's the lamp oil on the Basin road. That's the school."

The yard was very quiet.

"I am not going to tell four hundred people in a yard that the ward's water bill is why the school is shut in the winter term," Aldis Kern said, "because I have not seen that in a document yet and I don't say it without a document. But I have got a number in my head of how many places a district account carries and I have got a number in my head of how many places the ward school carries, and I am telling you the second number is the small one. And when the office draws the days up, it will not come here first. It will go to the top of Sill Row first. I'd rather say it to you now so that when it comes you will know somebody said it a month early."

Nobody said anything for a while. Somewhere down at the tank a man was hammering a hoop and the sound was the only thing in the yard.

Marek put the levy down on the tank's stone kerb, unrolled it, and put his hand flat on the top of it, and four hundred people watched a clerk do that.

"Now," he said.

He said it in the flat voice. He had used the flat voice on the Chair step at four in the morning with a boy and he had used it in a waiting room with a woman an hour ago, and it was the only voice he had that did not put anything extra on a sentence, and the yard understood it in about four seconds and stopped shuffling, which was the best possible thing that could have happened to him.

"You've been told the water came because a clerk held something up for six hours," he said. "That's not what happened and I am the one who did the holding, so you should get it from me before you get it from a yard captain.

"Holding up a settlement does not make water. It stops a collection going out the door. It buys time. It bought us six bells of time and not a drop of it, and if you have been thanking the clerk for the water, stop, because the water did not come from me.

"The water came out of a valve. The Third Sluice. A Lantern Warden named Ilya Senn has had that valve open since the second day of the rains and he has been doing it on his own name and his own authority, which he has, and against a sealed Crown Terrace order that is dated the seventh and that he has broken every hour since, in front of a Crown Terrace water-clerk who has written him four letters, and he has kept all four letters in his coat where anybody can get at them. He is doing it because if he shuts it, the intake at the head of that channel fills up and the low kitchens at this gate have nothing to put in a pot. That is his arithmetic and he has said it out loud in a hundred yards and I have written it down in my own book and it is going in the record whatever happens to me.

"Which means this ward has had a floodgate for a fortnight because a Warden broke an order and stayed on a platform, and every hour of that gate is an hour somebody is going to write down as sabotage in a Deputy Steward's line. In about a fortnight the Trust is going to call that man a saboteur, because a Trust cannot have a ward that got saved by an act of disobedience, it can only have a ward that got saved by a decision, and it will have made the decision itself.

"So here is the account, and I have read both sides of it, and the second side is longer.

"This ward owes nobody for last night. Not a day of it, not a pint of it, not one hour of anybody's future. Nobody in Bell Stair can bill us for the hold, because there is no form for a hold; I have been told so by the best person in this city for that particular sentence. That is a piece of luck and I am sorry for it, because I would rather you owed it and paid it than owed it to a man who is going to be ruined for feeding you.

"And this ward owes a debt to that Warden, and he has not asked for it, and it is not a debt that can be paid in a letter or a signature or a line in a book, and the ward has not agreed what it is yet, and I am not going to agree it for you either. Today is the fifteenth and there is a hearing at the eighth bell and the Trust's third floor is going to compare your copy of this roll with the file's, and I am going to be in that room, and the only thing I am asking of you in it is that somebody in this yard says out loud where the water came from. That's all. Say that sentence in that room. Everything else in this is arithmetic and you can out-arithmetic anybody, because you have been doing it on a wet chalkboard for nine days while we wrote the number up wrong on purpose."

He had the yard for about six seconds.

Then a voice near the back said, clear and hard and not raised: "Which clerk?"

Marek had been waiting for it since the fourth bell of the fourteenth and it had arrived at the sixth of the fifteenth, and it was not the voice he had expected.

"Which clerk held it up."

"I did," Marek said. "There is a notice on the posting board at the Threshold with my name on the line and the hour on it, under the words *no office of record*, and I took the duty in an empty building with no authority and no witness and nobody asked me to. I have said so out loud twice since last night. You may say it out loud. It is the only thing in this business I have never tried to keep quiet."

"And the boy at the counter," said the voice. "The one in the clinic."

"The boy at the counter was given a document with a column of totals on it and a pencil, and he added the column up, and the column was right, and he signed it, and that is a fact and it is a fact in his favour, and the clerks of this building have been telling the ward for two days that he was careless with a number, and he was not. He was three weeks in the post. There were not enough third-grade clerks in Morrowfen on the ninth for anybody else to be at that desk. If you want somebody to be angry at, the honest place to be angry at is the office of the man who wrote the column, and I am not going to stand in a yard and hand you a boy of twenty to be angry at instead, because there is a room on the island this morning where the whole of it is written down, and in that room his name is the one on the instrument, and my name is on a different paper, and if somebody confuses those two papers it will be because somebody in this tower wanted them confused."

Somebody said, "That's the tower's business, that."

"It is the ward's business too," Marek said, "the moment you have to be in that room, and I would rather you walked in knowing it than walked in angry and found out at the eighth bell."

He was aware that he had said too much and in the wrong order, and he also knew — standing there with his hand flat on the roll on the tank kerb — that he was going to do it again tomorrow, and that this was the second time in six days he had given something away in a public room because holding it had become a thing he was ashamed of, and there was no procedure anywhere in the building for the difference between a thing worth keeping and a thing that has to be said.

Neris Kade came through the yard.

She was on her way somewhere else. She had a satchel and a folded waterproof and the look of a woman with four hours of examining in her and a list that was not finished, and she crossed the yard from the school house end toward the Basin road, and as she passed within four feet of him she looked down at his forearm, where the sleeve had ridden again, and did not stop.

"That is a yearline mark," she said.

He did not answer. She was already four steps past him, and she did not look back, and a yard of four hundred people had seen a clerk's forearm get read in public by the Rainmarket examiner in one sentence and had understood every word of it, and Marek thought — much later, in a stair, days afterward — that of all the things that happened to him on the fifteenth, that was the one that took longest to sit down.

Afterward he went up Sill Row to the school house, because he had promised a wall.

There were ninety-one children in it and the room smelled of wet coats and the paraffin stove had been turned too high by somebody sensible. Kelb's sister was in the second row and had a shawl over her head and was staring at the door, and when Marek came in and put himself against the wall by the stove where a grown man can stand and be looked at without being touched, she looked at him for about two minutes without moving her head, and then she got up and came and stood beside him, and looked at the door.

"You're going to tell me," she said, in a voice pitched for somebody two rows away.

"Not today."

"You said last night you were going to say things."

"I said that to your mother." He kept his eye on the room. "I am going to tell you on the nineteenth, because that is the day she has written in her book, and because I would rather you had three days of being told wrong by a yard and one hour of being told right by a clerk. On the nineteenth, and not before, and I will be here at the second bell."

"You can't put a date on it."

"I can put a date on it," Marek said, "because I am a clerk, and the only thing I have ever been able to do is put a date on it, and it turns out that is worth something."

She stood beside him for the rest of the morning. It cost him nothing to be a wall. It was, in the whole of the fifteenth, the only thing he did that did not cost anybody else anything.

Then the eighth bell went, out in the street and not in the school house, and the ward came up out of four yards and a hundred yards and eleven kitchen yards and a standpipe and began to walk to the causeway in the rain, four hundred people, in carts and on foot, with a rolled sheet of oiled paper under Aldis Kern's arm and a printed roll that four different hands had copied, and a boy of nine at the front of it carrying a lantern because somebody had given her the lantern and she had decided that carrying it was the job.
