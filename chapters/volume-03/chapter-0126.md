# Chapter 126 — The Season He Gave Back

The second sowing on the low ground went in late in the third month because the first one was late, and it came up thin, and on the seventeenth of the growth month a man could stand at the end of a drill row on the low-ground road with a hoe in his hands and see in about four seconds that there was not going to be a crop off this ground this year.

Marek Vale came down the low-ground road at about the fifth bell with a coat and a lamp he had not lit and a name in his head that was a household four miles and eleven switchbacks above a stone bridge, and he got about two hundred yards past the chaff stall, which was still shut four days running, and then he went into a field and tried.

---

He had it worked out by then, and the working-out had taken four days and most of the four days had been spent standing in wet ground.

The four words were the whole of it. *A name, and then the season you have got.* Not *this season, this lot, here.* A name, and the thing the name has. And a thing can only be in one place, and a season is a thing, and a season is in the terraces now, in nine drill rows above a drain, in a store with a lock, in about four hundred and some people's ground, and he was not going to be able to point it at the low ground without taking it off them.

He thought about that for a long time on a bench in a yard in the third week, and he thought about it badly, and the way he thought about it badly was that he began with *the basin is owed this*, which is a true sentence, and ended with *therefore the basin may have it*, which is not a sentence anybody may conclude from the first one.

He put his hand on the headland at the end of the drill row and said a name at it.

He did not say a woman's name, because he did not have one. He said the name of the road, which is what a man says when he has nothing to say a person's name with, and he asked for the season that this road was owed, in this sowing, here, for this district.

Nothing came up. The drill row stayed thin. The hoe stayed in his hand and the ground stayed what it was and about four men in the next field over watched a man in a good coat put a hand on a headland and take it off again and go and stand in a ditch.

He did it four times over an hour and a half and the fourth time he said it properly, out loud, in the plainest words he had, with his hand flat on the headland and his face turned away from the four men, and the fifth time he stopped saying anything at all and just stood there with his hand on it, and the field did not do a single thing.

And then, at about the half-hour, the ditch he was standing in went out from under him.

---

Not the ground. That is the part he could not explain to anybody afterwards, including himself, and he went over it for a year.

The ditch did not move. He did not put his hand on anything. He was standing in a ditch on the low ground with his back to a hedge and his hands at his sides, and the low ground went out of his reach.

What happened was that he could no longer remember how to do a thing he had watched about nine hundred people do, and it was not a thing of his own.

He tried it on the hoe. He had used a hoe. He knew what a hoe was. He could not get the timing of it — not the swing, which is easy, but the *taking up the next handful*, which is a thing that is in a body's hands and not in a body's head, and it had gone, and he stood in a ditch on the low ground and worked out that it was not his timing that had gone, because he had never had any, and that what he had lost belonged to somebody who has lifted sacks or hoed or drawn a drill or dug since she was seven, and that she was in this field, or in a field like it, four miles up a road.

And it had happened that afternoon, in the ditch, in front of a woman with a satchel. On the ninth of the growth month what he had lost was his own: his own boiler, his own door, his own ledger, the four habits of a man who has lived in one room for ten years. That had been a bill. **This was not a bill.** Nobody had charged him a season of her hands. He had walked into a ditch on the low ground and come out holding the use of somebody else's working life, and the reason was that he had tried to spend a thing that was not his on a thing that had already been spent, and the price of that does not go on a bill and does not go to anybody either, and by the time he understood it in the ditch it had been in him for about nine seconds and there was no office in this kingdom that could have stopped it and no form in this kingdom that could have said so afterwards.

He tried four more things in about a minute and a half. A gate latch. The weight of a bucket. The way a woman turns a sack off her hip. The sound a scythe makes when it is going well.

All four of them were gone and all four of them were somebody else's, and what he had in place of them was a hole shaped exactly like a habit, and the habit was not his and would not have been his.

He got himself out of the ditch and stood on the headland and understood, with the clarity that comes at about the third hour of the night, that this is what attaching means, and that it is not a punishment, and that nobody in this kingdom has a form for it because it is not a thing that happens to anybody who is owed something.

It is a thing that happens to a man who spends a season he does not have on a person he has not asked.

---

Neris Kade was forty feet away with a satchel of returns over her shoulder and a chaff stall's shutter up behind her and about nine people in a lane, and she had been on that low ground since the second bell and had watched all of it, and she came over and did not ask him what the matter was.

"You have spent a season," she said.

"Yes."

"And on the ninth of the growth month you spent one on a holding on the second terrace, and you did it at the third bell before light with a lamp you did not light, and the rows came up on the twelfth, and there are about nine people up there who have told that story eleven times this month and told it to each other and got the same eleven things out of it every time, and every one of those eleven things is wrong." She shifted the satchel. "I have been up there twice this week. I did not go to find out. I go there. It is my ground."

"Yes."

"And on the seventeenth, at about the half-hour, in a ditch, you tried to give one back to this ground, and it did not take, and something came off you and went into you, and you have not been able to hold a hoe since and neither can I tell you what a hoe is supposed to feel like, and I have watched you try three things since I got here and you are going to try a fourth and I would like you not to, because it does not get better and because the lane has nine people in it and two of them are mine."

Marek took his hand off the bucket.

---

"Say the true part," said Neris Kade, "and do not put it in a room afterwards, and do not tell me it was necessary, because I have watched necessary men this year and they are the reason that clause is nailed to a wall in a shed four miles from here."

Marek said the true part.

It took him about two minutes and he did not do it well. It was that the counterweight had to land on somebody and had been landed on nobody for two years because nobody could be named, and that a season is a thing that can only be in one place, and that he had chosen the place, and that the whole of what he had over a class was that he had converted it into a woman with five children, and that he had done it without asking the class and without asking the woman and had told both of them afterwards by pointing a hoe at their own ground.

"And it is the only difference I have," he said. "The paper does not choose. I chose. That is the whole of the argument and it is seven words long and I have had nine days to find an eighth and there is not one."

"No," said Neris Kade. "There is not a tenth. And I am going to say the other half and then neither of us is going to be thanked and neither of us is going to be right, and we are going to stand in a lane and not build anything."

"Say it."

"You have done the one thing in this basin that there is no form for, and you have done it with a part of you that no office in this kingdom can be shown and no register can be entered and no witness can be paid, and that is the first honest sentence anybody has said about it and I am not giving it to you as a comfort." She did not raise her voice and about nine people in a lane found they had better get on with what they were doing. "The class was the only honest thing on that paper. The class was the one part of it nobody had signed away. And you have taken a class of four hundred and some people and made one of them pay, in a field, in a month, without a word to her, and the reason you did it is that she was the only one you could get your hand on. That is not a difference from the paper. That is the paper, with a name at the front of it, and the name is yours and it is not hers and she has not seen it."

---

"And the other half," she said, "which is mine, and I am not going to pretend it is small.

"This ground is not coming up, and the stall behind me has been shut four days running, and by the fourth month about nine households in this city are going to be hungry who were not going to be hungry in the second month, and there is no office in this basin that can send a person up a road to ask a household whether it can spare anything, and there is no form in which a person may be told that a district is short, and there is no form in which a district may be told that a *field* was paid for a loan it did not know it was on. And you have just spent a season failing to fix it in a ditch, and you are the only person in this basin who has ever tried to fix anything of this shape at all, and I am not going to be the woman who takes that off you, and if I were you I would not let me."

"Then what is to be done."

"Nothing is to be done tonight," said Neris Kade. "That is not me refusing. There is no form for it and there is no party for it and there are no first names in it. I have a satchel of returns that go nowhere and a piece of paper with four figures on it that nobody has ever called a figure out in a room, and I am going to take the satchel down to the seed house and read somebody else's letter out to sixty-one people, and I am going to be at the low ground on the twenty-second if you are going to be anywhere at all."

She said the last part flatly, and it was not warmth and it was not an invitation and it was not a thank you, and she did not say his name, and she had not said his name since the tenth month of last year.

---

Marek Vale stood on the low ground at about the seventh bell with his hands in his coat and went and found, in a lane, the only trade in this basin where a man can be asked to write a thing down without a form and without a fee, and there is not one, and everybody knows there is not one, and about nine people a month find it anyway.

A letter had gone to a harbour nine days up the coast on the seventh of the second month. He had paid a pound for it and sealed it and put it on a shelf above a chandler's shop, and the man on that shelf had said the coastal post goes out on the tide, and the tide was on the ninth of the month, and a letter put there on the seventh would be in somebody's hand in about six weeks.

That was the third month. It was the seventeenth of the growth month. It had been about thirteen weeks, and there was nothing on the shelf.

The letter asked one question and it was going to be answered with one of three words, and Marek had said in a lane in the third month that he would write it in about a fortnight when he had somebody else's paper he could not get into a room, and it had gone in the second month, before he had a piece of paper of any kind, and he has never been able to work out whether that was the one sensible thing he did this year or the first one that was purely a way of not doing it.

He stood in a lane and worked out the rest of it. A letter would tell him whether one man's name is on a list in a town he had never been to. It would not tell him what a hand did in a cellar four miles from a river. And not one thing in this basin that was going to happen between tonight and the fourth month of next year turned on the answer. And a thing not coming back at all is not a no, and a man who has just come out of a ditch holding somebody else's hands is in no condition to be told by a stranger in a foreign calendar what a mark is for.

He did not go. He went down to a yard at the end of the lane where about nine people were mending a bar in a cut, and he put his shoulder against the end of it, and it did what it did, and nobody thanked him for it, and he could not have told them why he was there.
