# Chapter 129 — A Brother, a Case, and a Refusal

Tomas Vale had gone down to the low ground on the sixth of the harvest month to fetch a bar he had lent, and had left his case in a lean-to at the top of the fourth switchback, and Marek Vale came up the road with it over his shoulder because it was a day's walk and there was nobody else on it, and met his brother coming back up about two hundred yards below the lean-to, and neither of them had expected the other.

The case is a bridgewright's case and it is thirty-one years old and it has a handle wrapped in tarred twine and it has three pegs in it and a chalk line in it and a key in it that opens nothing anybody has ever owned.

Marek put it down in the lane and did not put it down for about four seconds, and then said:

"Before."

Tomas Vale stopped. He is thirty-four and he has been up this road thirty-four years and he said one word, which was "What," and it was not a question, and then he waited, and he did not say it again.

---

There is a thing about Tomas Vale and it is the only thing about him that anybody in four terraces will tell you, and it is that he has never once interrupted his brother.

Marek told him in a lane on the sixth of the harvest month at about the fourth bell, standing on the outside of a bend with about nine feet of drop below the wall, and he told it in the order it happened and he did not editorialise and he did not say sorry once, and it took about four minutes.

He told him about the ninth of the growth month, and the third bell, and nine drill rows on the second terrace, and a woman he was not permitted to name. He told him that he had put a season of the basin's into a household's field and had got the four days of it right and had not known for four days that he had got it right. He told him that on the seventeenth he had tried to put one back into the low ground in a ditch and had failed and that what had come off him in the ditch had been somebody else's hands, and that he could not lift a hoe in the right order and had about nine months of a season of a woman's work in him and did not know whose.

He told him that a physician had come up eleven switchbacks on the nineteenth and had looked at him for four minutes and had written one line and a mark she had invented at the left-hand margin of a clinic sheet, and had taken it away with her, and had told him she would not say whose it was, and that he had not asked her twice.

And he told him about the second, the third, the fourth and the fifth of the harvest month, and about nine men and a channel, and about a hand on a bank.

---

Tomas Vale stood on a lane above a stone bridge with a bar over his shoulder and listened to the whole of it without once saying anything, and at the end of it he said:

"Right."

And then he took the bar off his shoulder and put it down in the lane, which he has not done in the time Marek has been able to remember, and said:

"That is what I asked for."

"I know what you asked for."

"You do not," said Tomas Vale. "On the second of the second month I sat at my own table and I said I wanted to be told when I am being used, in the room, before, and not a month afterwards, and you stood in that kitchen and gave me four sentences about the volume of work. That was five months ago. And you have just done it in four minutes on a bend on the sixth of the harvest month, before, in the open, with a bar on the ground and nobody in the lane but me, and it has cost you about nine seconds, and I have been waiting five months for nine seconds."

He picked the bar up again.

"So that is the first thing and I am not going to make a speech about it and I am not going to say that it makes it right, and I want to hear the word right come out of your mouth so that I can tell you it was not there."

"It was not there."

"Good," said Tomas Vale. "Now I am going to say three things and you are not going to write one of them down and I am not going to ask you to."

---

"The wall on the second terrace is going. Not this year. It is going in about nine years and there is a weep in it that I have found and I have told nobody and I told you in a lane in the growth month and you did not write it down and I have thought about that every day since and I have decided it was correct, and I am telling you it was correct so that you do not go up a hill and change it.

"Because the moment that goes on a piece of paper it goes up a road in about nine months in somebody else's hand with a head at the top of it. And there is no form in this kingdom in which a weep in a wall can be entered, and I have been a bridgewright for nineteen years and I have watched about nine men try, and it is always the same, and the paper always outlives the men who are on it. So it is a thing in a margin on a board in a loft with no date on it and no name at the foot of it but mine. And if I am not here in nine years then that board is the only place in four terraces where the jobs are, and it is not a record, and it is the only thing I have ever made that was worth anything, and you did not write it down and that is the whole of what I have to say about you."

"I am not going to write it down."

"I know. It is why I told you and it is why I will tell you the next two things, and if that is a system then it is the only one either of us has got."

"The second thing is that the store on the fourth terrace is not open and has not been open since the second month, and the key is in a coat, and the keeper of it is going to be dead in the winter, and there is no form on which anybody may be applied for." He said it flatly, the way a man reads a culvert's invert. "I have not asked him for it. I am not going to ask him for it. If you have got the key in your hand then you have got it and you have not asked him and he has not given it to you, and both of those are correct, and I have thought about the pair of them for two days and I would like to know what a man does with a key in his hand in a valley with no form in it, and I am asking you on a bend, and I do not expect an answer and I am not going to be angry about it."

---

"And the third thing," said Tomas Vale, "is the wall on this road."

He stepped out into the lane and put a hand on the parapet above nine feet of drop and did not look down it.

"Line four," he said. "Four miles of road, eleven stakes and a rope, a culvert with its inlet four feet below its invert, and about nine hundred yards of it that I have stood on and would not let a cart over, and I have not put one of those things on a form and I am not going to, and I have told you and you have written nothing, and in about nine years there is going to be a bad winter and somebody is going to have to make a decision about this road with about nine people standing on it, and if there is a board in a loft then I will be forty-three and I will be standing on the road.

"And if instead there is a form in a building a quarter of a mile from the ground, then in nine years a man will read it and decide about the road from a desk, and there will be no ninth-year anybody to correct him, and my daughter will be eighteen and she will not be able to tell which nine hundred yards of this road are the ones I would not let a cart over, and there will not be one living person in this basin who can tell her except me."

Marek said, "That is not a fair account of how forms work."

"No," said Tomas Vale. "It is a fair account of how they survive. That is the whole of what I know and I have been on this road thirty-four years and I have never once known a form to come to the road."

He picked the case up off the wall and gave it to Marek.

"Now carry that back down and do not say one word about it."

---

Marek Vale carried a thirty-one-year-old case with a wrapped handle down eleven switchbacks on the sixth of the harvest month and put it in a lean-to at the bottom of a lane in Tallow Ward, and then went and stood at the counter of a shed eleven feet by nine for about a quarter of an hour without saying anything at all, and then said it.

"I want to enter a line about my own paper."

There were about twenty people in the shed, which holds twenty-two, and about ninety in the lane, and it was the seventh of the harvest month and the rota had a day on it and nothing at the head of it, and Sanne Droll put the chalk down.

"Have you got a form."

"No."

"Have you got a paper."

"No."

"Then there is no line," said Sanne Droll, "and what you have brought here is not a line and it is not a paper and there is no rule in this shed about it, because every rule in this shed is about a piece of paper that a person has brought in about themselves, and you have not brought a piece of paper, and I have been here thirty-nine years and I am not going to start inventing a class for a man in a wet coat."

"Suppose I write it on a sheet of my own."

"Then I ask you the four questions," said Droll, "and I ask them at the counter and not at the door, and the first is *is that your line*, and the second is *is that the person behind it*, and the third is *is it your words*, and there is a fourth one that nobody has ever heard me ask and it is the one that matters this morning, and it is *is there a person behind it who is in this room.*"

"And the answer to the fourth one is no," said Marek.

"Then it does not go in," said Sanne Droll. "Not because of anything to do with who you are. Because the person behind it is four hundred and some people on four terraces and there are about twenty of us in this shed and not one of them is on four terraces, and there is no form in this kingdom in which a line may be entered about people who are not in the room, and that rule was made in this yard in the ninth month and I helped make it and I would make it again tomorrow."

Marek stood in the middle of a shed with a bench against the wall and did not say anything for about a minute.

"That is the first line anybody has ever refused me on the yard's own rule," he said.

"Yes," said Sanne Droll. "And I would like it noticed that the yard refused it and not that anybody refused you, and I would like it noticed that you did not put your name to it and I would like it noticed that I am not going to say a word about why not, because if I say why not then I am a person who knows, and I am a person who keeps this counter and I have been here thirty-nine years and I have never once known anything."

---

Then Petar Duss came in about half an hour later, at about the sixth bell, with a rope round his wrist and a folded paper in the inside of his coat that he has not let out of his coat since the growth month, and he has been in this shed four times and every one of the four times he has asked somebody to write down what he said, and every one of the four times he has been told no.

He said it in the doorway, before he had said anything else, which is what he does.

"I want a line about my own paper and I am the person behind it. I paid four shillings for it in the growth month to a man in two rooms over a chandler's shop on the low-ground road, and I never saw him put it on, and there is a mark on the corner of it in a calendar that nobody in this basin can read, and I have said on a bridge and on a stair and in this lane that I knew the road was opened on purpose, and I have asked about nine people to write it down and about nine of them said no."

"Have you got the paper," said Sanne Droll.

"I have got the paper."

"Then give it to me and I will ask you the four questions and if the answers are right the line goes in."

Duss did not move.

"Second question," said Droll. "*Is that the person behind it.* You are the party. You are the man who signed it and you are the man who paid four shillings and you are the man who stood on a bridge. And the mark in the corner of it has a person's name in it, in nine letters, the smallest thing on the paper, and that person is a man in two rooms over a shop on the low-ground road who has said out loud in his own rooms that he does not know what the marks are and has not known for nine years, and he is not in this room, and I am not going to write to him and I would not know what to say if I did."

---

Petar Duss stood in a shed eleven feet by nine and understood what had just been done to him, and it took him about four seconds, and what came out of him then was the loudest thing said in Tallow Ward in about four months.

"Nine months," he said. "Nine months I have stood on a bridge and on a stair and in this lane telling anybody who would listen that I am the man behind my own paper, and I have said it out loud in front of nine people every time on purpose so that it would be in a room, and I have asked about nine people to write it down and about nine of them said no, and the whole of my wanting has been that one day somebody in a room would say *yes, that man is the person behind it*."

"And I have just said yes," said Sanne Droll, "and it is not a comfort, and I am not sorry about it, and I would have said it to a man with a good coat in the same voice. The person behind your paper is a man who will not say who he is, and the paper carries him in a corner in nine letters, and that is not a thing anybody in this room can weigh."

"And that is a new one," said Petar Duss.

"It is a new one," said Sanne Droll. "Thirty-nine years at this counter and I have never had to make it, and I am making it on a paper with a mark in the corner on it and I do not know that I am right, and I am not going to pretend to you that I have checked. What I have checked is that the mark is the only part of that sheet with a person in it and the person is not in the room, and everything else on it is a form and a figure, and a form and a figure cannot be refused because a form and a figure cannot be anybody's."

---

"Then write that down," said Petar Duss. "Write down that a paper with a mark in the corner of it cannot be entered in this yard, and put it on that board, because I have got four names on a Crown March form in a lane in this city and in about nine months a man in a good coat is going to stand in that lane and say I had a contract and that this yard took it, and there is a mark on it and I cannot give you the man, and he is going to be right about both halves and I am going to be standing in a lane holding nothing."

Sanne Droll put the chalk on the counter.

"No," she said.

"No?"

"No. Because a refusal written on a board is a list, and a list needs a head at the top of it, and the head of it would be *refused*, and there is a column on that wall with nothing at the head of it that nineteen hands in this yard declined to name and that a vote in about four minutes did not fill and did not rule out, and if I put *refused* at the top of a column then that wall becomes a roll of people I have turned away, and in about nine months a man in a good coat can produce this yard's own door."

"Then what do I do with it," said Duss.

"The only thing this yard does," said Sanne Droll, "is read it out."

---

Teodor Lask is nineteen and he is the reader on the days the rota says and he read somebody's second line out loud twice at the ordinary speed every morning of the tenth month of MR 413, and on the seventh of the harvest month, at about half past the sixth bell, with about thirty people in a shed and about ninety in a lane and a bench against the wall, he read out a refusal twice at the ordinary speed.

He read it exactly as Sanne Droll had said it, in a boy's flat voice, and he did not put anything at the head of it and he did not put anything at the foot of it, and he did not say whose refusal it was, and the two men it belonged to stood about eleven feet apart in a shed that holds twenty-two and neither of them said one word to the other about it.

Petar Duss heard his own paper refused in a room in Tallow Ward, twice, at the ordinary speed, by a boy of nineteen, and said thank you, which nobody in that shed expected, and which was not for the boy.

At the fourth bell the next morning there were about eighty people in the lane and a reader at the ordinary speed and a shed that did what it does, and there were now two men in this city who could not get a line in about a thing that was their own. Neither of them was on a form. There is no form in this kingdom on which either of them could have been named, and a boy of nineteen read both of their refusals out twice at the ordinary speed and neither of them asked him to.
