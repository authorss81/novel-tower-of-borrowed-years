# Chapter 117 — The Press and the Proof

The chandler's yard in Bell Stair has a printer's shop standing open on it with a taking press in it and a roller and a box of dampening, and the shop had been shut since before the flood year, and the yard let it to anybody who asked and the only thing anybody had ever asked for it in eleven years was a place to leave a barrow.

Marek Vale asked for it on the ninth of the seed month and the chandler said yes, and charged a day's rent for it in a yard where nothing was ever charged, and said that he was not doing it out of the way and that a man had to pay something or the arrangement was a favour, and a favour in a yard is a thing that gets asked about later.

Sedge came down the hill with the form in a flat case. Ondra Kelb came with him because a duplicate has to be taken by two people and because about nine other people in this city had asked Kelb to do a duplicate in the last month and he had done all nine.

---

The cellar under the yard is eleven steps down and there are nine crates in it and two hundred and eleven sheets of a Crown March seasonal form in eight of them and about forty standing on edge in the ninth with the boards off.

Vesna Duss said three things and then went and stood on the bottom step with a lamp.

She said the cellar was hers and the shop floor was not, and the press was not, and the yard was not. She said that if a man went to work on a sheet down there with a wet roller then in about nine months there would be a run of damp in the corner of her cellar and two hundred and eleven sheets of a form in it, and that paper is dry down there and dry is the only thing she has ever wanted out of that room. And she said: *I am not going to be thanked for telling you and I am not going to be the woman in a yard with a cellar of Crown March forms, and I said that once and a man wrote it down, and I would like it noticed that I have said it twice.*

So they took about nine sheets up the eleven steps and worked on the floor of the shop with the yard door open and the rain coming in at the edge of it, and Marek carried the crate lid up separately, on his own, in two hands, because a lid is a lid and a crate is a crate and he was not going to have Sedge's hands on a thing with a mark in it.

---

Sedge put the two things side by side on the shop floor, on a board, under the light from the yard door, and said nothing at all about them for about four minutes, which was the longest he had been quiet in Marek's experience of him.

The lid of the ninth crate has a mark on the inside of it. A month, a tide phase, a harbour seal in wax, and a translator's name in a small hand with a space before it and none after.

The form has a mark on it too. In the same four parts, in the same order, in the same corner, at the same distance from the edge of the paper.

Sedge got his thumb on it and turned the sheet about ten degrees and looked at the two of them from further off, which is what a man does who has spent a long time with printing.

"Print it," he said.

Nobody said anything.

"That is not a stamp," said Sedge. "A stamp is a strike and it is a press and it is one at a time and it is a thing a man does to a paper. That is a *head*. That is set up in a case with a sort in it and it is part of the furniture of the form, and it comes down with the run, and it has come down with every run of this form for longer than that printer's ledger goes back."

He put his hand flat on the margin of the sheet and did not touch the mark.

"Sit down," he said, "and I am going to say a thing and Marek is not going to like it and I am going to say it anyway because I have been a clerk in a building on a hill long enough that twice in it and I would like to be the one who said it."

He said his line. *A form has to be cut by a man who does not know whether it is right.*

And then he said the rest of it, and it took about four minutes, and about half of it was Sedge talking and half of it was Sedge refusing to let Marek say a true thing in a smaller way.

"That form was not cut by anybody. It was set in type in a house in the hills and printed in a run of eleven hundred and something and sent to a season office, and the office is not in this basin, and the printer is not in this basin, and there is a man in that house somewhere whose job it was to set a head in a margin and I do not know his name and neither does the office and neither does the man who owns the ledger that says eleven hundred and something.

"And that is the whole of what is on that sheet, and there is nothing wrong with it, and I have now looked at it beside a mark a man put on the inside of a lid with a pen and a matrix, and the two of them are the same size and the same distance from the edge, and that is not because a man did a thing twice. It is because one of them is type and one of them is a man, and a man copies the type, because the type is what he has in front of him and it is on a hundred other crates in a shed at the bottom of a river."

---

Ondra Kelb had been standing at the roller for about ten minutes saying nothing, and he said it now, and what he said was the thing the whole afternoon was for.

"A mark that is printed and a mark that is written are the same mark," he said. "That is not an opinion. I have been asked to certify duplicates for four years and I have taken about nine hundred of them and I cannot tell you, standing here, with these two side by side, which of them a person put on. Nor can anybody. Not a judge, not a notary, not a man from a harbour. You cannot tell a written mark from a printed one by looking at it, because the written one was copied off a printed one, or off another written one, or off nothing at all, and the paper does not remember which.

"So if a person asks me in a room whether that mark is that man's mark, I have got two answers and one of them is a guess. And the guess is the answer everybody gives, because a guess about a hand is a thing a room likes. And a hand is the only part of that mark that is a person at all. The month is printed. The phase is printed. The seal is brass and a rope round it and four pounds a year to a man in a lease. And the fourth part is nine letters of somebody's name, and it is the smallest thing on the paper, and it is the only thing anybody put there."

He put his hand on the roller, flat, and then took it off, and Marek Vale saw him do it and understood about nine seconds later that he had been about to put his weight on it with his left hand and had changed his mind, and Marek did not look at the left hand and did not ask and would not, and did not tell anybody in the rest of that year that he had seen it.

---

"Take a duplicate," said Sedge. "Off the form. And then somebody in this yard is going to be able to say that the two are the same instrument, and I want to hear them say it with the right word in it."

They took about nine minutes over it. Damp paper, a roller, a press with a screw on it and a flat-edge board, and the thing that made it was not clever and was not fast and was done by two people because a taking press wants a hand on the roller and a hand on the screw.

The mark came off the form and onto the sheet exactly as it was on the form, at exactly the distance from the edge, in exactly the four parts, and the wax-coloured part of it came off as a grey smudge about half the weight of the original, and a child could tell them apart on a good day, and on a bad day in a room a quarter of a mile from the ground the paper is about, nobody could.

Sedge wrote on the bottom of the duplicate, in ink, in a clerk's hand: *This is a true duplicate of a sheet of the seasonal form of the run held in Bell Stair, taken this day in the presence of two clerks, one of whom certifies.* And then he put his name to it, because a duplicate is only a duplicate if somebody has put their name on it, and Marek Vale stood in a printer's shop in a yard in Bell Stair and understood where he had got to.

"And that is the moment," he said. "That is the moment a copy stops being a copy. Not the sheet — the name. The sheet is paper and the mark is furniture and neither of them is worth anything on its own. The name on the bottom is the instrument. Put a name on the bottom and the whole thing becomes a certified true duplicate of a form which is in a cellar under a chandler's yard belonging to a woman who is not a printer, and there is no difference between the two sheets at all, and the only thing that has made the one into a document is a man writing his name at the bottom of it, and that is the whole of the authority in this kingdom, and I have spent ten years learning it and I have never once seen it printed."

"You have," said Sedge. "It is on the form. It is in the margin. It is four parts and the smallest of them is a name, and it is on about eleven hundred sheets of paper, and the man who set it in type has a name that nobody has, and the man who writes the fourth part is one of nine men on a coast about nine days from here, and every one of those men is doing about nine hundred a year out of a sheet that does not exist any more."

---

Marek sat down on the shop floor with his back against the yard door and the rain at the edge of his boot and put the whole of it in one sentence, and it was the sentence of the day and he did not know it yet.

"A standing authority that certifies a projection has nothing to certify except a number," he said. "The head of the column is in type. The figure under the head is a hand. The seal on the corner of the sheet is a strike, not a signature, and the corner of the sheet is where the type put it. And the whole of it is a form, and a form is the cheapest thing in this basin to produce and the most expensive thing anybody in it has ever refused, and there is not one office in this kingdom in which a single word of it is entered, because none of it is a fact about a person."

He looked at the two sheets on the board.

"And the reason nobody in Morrowfen can do anything about a mark that is wrong," he said, "is that nobody in Morrowfen can read one. Not the office. Not the yard. Not the man who took the duplicate. And the only reading anybody in this kingdom has ever had of one was done on the fourteenth of the third month in a lane in Tallow Ward, when a clerk from the hills looked at a haulier's contract and the haulier looked back and neither of them said one word about it, because neither of them could."

Vesna Duss put a lamp on the board and looked at the duplicate and at the crate lid for a while and then went back down the eleven steps to her own dry room, and shut the hatch, and put the bar across it.

At the fourth bell the next day a man with no office read somebody's second line out loud twice at the ordinary speed in a shed eleven feet by nine in Tallow Ward, with a bench against the wall on the days the rota said, and about twenty people in the lane, and the fifth line of the form empty, and the sixth line empty and always would be, and a column on the wall list with nothing at the head of it, and he did not say one word about the yard, and he could not have entered one if he had said it, and a certified duplicate of a Crown March seasonal form was in a flat case under a trestle in the shed, and four names were on a sheet from a Crown March clerk's own case in Tallow Ward's lane, and neither of those two things was in the other, and there was no form in this kingdom in which either could be put in front of the other.
