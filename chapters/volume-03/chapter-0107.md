# Chapter 107 — The Twenty-Fourth

The room was over a chandler's shop on the low-ground road and it had one table in it and four chairs, and it was hired for a day by a Crown March granary official who had arrived in the basin on the nineteenth of the tenth month and who had spent the intervening days being pleasant to everybody he met, and who put the clause on the table at about the second bell on the twenty-fourth and did not sit down.

"There is no hurry in this," said Sabin Rell. "I want to say that first, because I know what a man with no office looks like when he is in a room with a piece of paper. There is no hurry in this at all. It is one sheet. I have signed about two hundred of them this year and I expect to sign about forty more before the year turns."

Marek Vale was standing by the wall because the four chairs were for the four people who were in the room, and the four people were Sabin Rell, a young man from Verrow who had the ledger and who was called nothing more than that for two days, Ilse Ferrow, who had come because she had heard on the low-ground road that a Crown March paper was being sealed in a room and who had a right to be there and used it, and Marek.

Ferrow had said on the way in that she was going to say very little, and she said very little, and what she did say she said into the corner of the table at about the third bell and never again, and it is set out further down because it was the only true thing anybody in that room said before the fourth bell.

---

The clause was one sheet and it had three clauses and a rule at the foot.

Marek had seen it on a river stair five days before, in a satchel, and he had not been permitted to hold it, and he was not holding it now. He was four feet off the table and he read it standing up, and he read it three times, and it took him about eleven minutes to be sure of the whole of it, and by the end of the eleven minutes he was sure of two things and neither of them was a lie.

The first was that the sheet had been sealed before anybody wrote a word on it. The harbour seal was struck in wax at the head of it. The Tide Reckoning mark was in the bottom corner, small, four parts, a month and a tide phase and a seal and a translator. The Crown March office's own seal was on the reverse and had been there since Verrow, which is a long way off and which nobody in this room has seen. The only things on the sheet that were not yet on the sheet were a day and a name, and both of those were in the same hand and were going to be in the same pen.

"I want to say what that is," said Rell, who had followed his eyes. "Because a man in a good coat will look at that for the rest of his life and I would like him to have it in the right words now.

"A granary clause is printed and sealed in advance because the printing press is in Verrow and the seal is in Verrow and the paper is bought by the room, and a room that has to have a piece of paper sealed in Morrowfen on the day it is made will wait for the next barge. Every instrument in this kingdom that matters is sealed before somebody signs it, and the signing is not the sealing, and there is a form for the sealing and there is no form for the signing. Last year I signed a document in Verrow on a sheet that had been struck in the third month, and I did not think about it. I did not think about it. I have stopped thinking about it and I have started writing the date twice, once in my hand and once in my own column, and the day I stop doing that is the day I should be moved to a shed."

"The counterseal," said Marek.

"The counterseal is the only interesting thing in the room and I have got it in my coat and it is Mr Sunn's, and I have paid him four pounds for a day and he does not know I am in this building."

That was said in the ordinary voice of a man explaining a delivery.

---

The second thing Marek was sure of was on the back.

There were two sheets, not one, and the second was behind the first and folded once, and it was a schedule, and the schedule was eleven lines long, and it was a list of four terrace holdings in eleven names.

He did not read it all. He read the heading and the fourth column and then he stopped for about two minutes.

The fourth column of a schedule of terrace holdings underwritten for a delivery has a space on it for the thing that will go wrong if the delivery does not come, and on the eleven lines the fourth column was the same nine words written out eleven times, which is the way a printed form works and is not a coincidence and is nobody's handwriting.

*The seed-keepers of the four terraces after this season.*

Marek Vale read those nine words four times standing four feet off a table in a room over a chandler's shop on the low-ground road, and in the ninth month of MR 413 a woman had written a sentence in her own hand on the fourth line of a board in a shed in Tallow Ward about a class, and the sentence was that a class is not a person and has never been a person and cannot be asked what it will bear, and she had certified a paper two years before that read *and the seed-keepers of this village after us bear the keeping*, and she had been up that road in the twelfth of the ninth month and had sat in a room off a seed room and had been named at the door and had got a hand on nothing.

One village. Four terraces. The same nine words, with the number in front of them changed.

It is a phrase. It is not a sentence anybody in a valley wrote at a table. It is a phrase on a form, and there is a form that goes to every depot in a region, and the number in front of it is filled in by hand, and the words after it are not.

He had come up that road with a sentence. He had carried it eleven switchbacks and up four miles above a bridge and it was the best sentence he had, and he had carried it because a woman had written it in her own hand, and he was standing in a room at about the ninth minute with the sentence ready and it was *a class is not a person, and nobody was standing behind it.*

He did not say it.

He said, instead: "Where is the form this schedule is printed on."

Rell put his hand on the schedule without turning it round.

"Third clause and a schedule, standard granary form, eleven names or nine or four, the schedule is the schedule," he said. "The number is filled in. The word before it — and there is a word before it on the printed form, *and* — the word before it is also filled in, because on a printed form you cannot print *and* on some lines and not on others, and I have seen four hundred of these and I could not tell you with a straight face which of them had eleven names and which of them had four."

"How long has the phrase been on the form?"

"Longer than my post," said Rell. "Longer than the depot's charter, I would say, and I am not going to put a year on it because I have never seen a print order and a print order is a thing a printer keeps."

A form has no date on it. Sedge had said that in a cellar nine days ago and had been pleased about it in a way that Marek had not understood at the time.

---

"You have read the fourth column four times," said Rell. "You are doing the thing I would do. So I am going to do the courteous thing and answer it before you ask, and then I am going to sit down, because we are both going to be here a while.

"The fourth column is the counterweight of the advance. If the delivery does not come, the advance is not a set-off out of a harvest, because there is no harvest, so it is a set-off against the party that stood behind the delivery. And the party that stands behind four terraces is the people who will be farming them next, and there is nobody else, and so the form says what the form says.

"And I know what I have just said. I have known it since the sixth of the ninth month and I have had the form in the satchel ever since I came down the river. It is not a fraud and it is not a decision I took. It is a column and I filled in a number."

"You filled in a number of what."

"Of holdings. Eleven. There are four terraces and eleven holdings in them, and I have counted them off the schedule, and the number is the number, and I have certified it to the board at Verrow and a man there has checked it against a table and has not come down here to look at four hundred acres of ground he has never walked on, and that is the correct way round. A granary certifies paper. It does not certify ground."

Marek looked at the eleven lines for a long time.

"How many people are in the eleven holdings," he said.

And Rell stopped, and it was the first time in four days that Marek had seen him stop, and he stopped for about three seconds and then he said the thing in a completely different voice, the way a man says a thing he has said out loud four hundred times in a room in Verrow and has never once had to say it to a person.

"You cannot ask me that," he said. "There is no figure. A holding is a holding and a household is not a holding and I could give you the number of holdings and I could not give you the number of people in them and neither could the man at the depot, and we are not going to try, because if you count the people in a district then the people in a district become a figure, and a figure can be entered, and a figure that can be entered can be added up, and the sum of the people in four terraces is the sort of number that a house in a good coat could take to a house in another good coat and turn into a yield."

That is a form putting a cost in a box with no person in it, and it is the fourth time Marek Vale has heard it said in a different mouth in ten months, and each time it has been said by somebody who is correct.

"You have said that before," he said.

"I have said it about four hundred times in a room at Verrow and it is a thing I believe," said Rell. "It is the reason a granary board is not a census and the reason the mean is not an estimate. I will put it in writing if anybody asks. Nobody has ever asked."

---

At about the third bell, Ilse Ferrow, who had said on the way in that she was going to say very little, said the only true thing anybody said in the room before the fourth bell, into the corner of the table, to Rell and not to Marek.

"Mr Rell," she said. "I have a licence that lapsed on the ninth of the ninth month and a house on this road and nine sheets in a drawer that I am not going to talk about. And I would like to ask a question that has nothing to do with the lapse and everything to do with the fact that I have answered every question truthfully for nine years.

"Do you know what you have just certified."

"I know exactly what I have certified," said Rell.

"Then you know that there is a line on that schedule that is a class, and there has never been a form in this kingdom in which a class consents to anything, and the reason is not the law. The reason is that a class cannot be asked, and a thing that cannot be asked cannot refuse, and an instrument whose counterweight cannot refuse is not false and is not true. It is merely unasked, and you have certified it as though somebody had been asked, and there is nobody."

"Yes," said Sabin Rell, and it was the shortest word he had said in five days, and he said it without heat and without any of the things a man says when he is defending himself.

"I have known there was nobody since the sixth of the ninth month," he said. "I have not known it differently since the fourth. And I am going to sign it, and if I do not sign it then a man at Verrow puts a no in a box, and I have told you what a no in a box is, and I would like you to consider that a woman with a lapsed licence is not the person who should be asking me for a different answer."

"I am not asking you for anything," said Ilse Ferrow, "and I have not said a word about your four clauses, and if anybody asks me in a year what I said in this room I will say that I asked you whether you knew and you said yes and then you signed it, and that is all, and it will not help you and it is not intended to."

---

At the fourth bell, Rell sat down and dated the sheet and signed it, and he dated it twice, once in his hand and once in his own column, which is what he said he had started doing a year ago.

The pen made a noise on the paper. Ferrow watched the mark go on at the bottom and did not say one word about it, and Marek Vale stood four feet off a table and looked at a small four-parted mark in the corner of a clause and at nine words in a column and at the pen.

Rell blew the dust off the sheet and squared it to the edge of the table and then, in the voice of a man who has been pleasant to everybody for five days and has run out of ways to be pleasant, said: "You are going to be useful to me or you are not, and either is fine, so I am going to ask you the question in the only form I am entitled to ask it, which is a courtesy between two men who have been of use to one another. Do you intend to do anything?"

And Marek Vale said, "No."

And meant it.

He had nine lots of sand out of thirty-nine in a yard four miles above a bridge, and eleven thousand and some hundreds of pounds in a cellar, and two hundred and eleven sheets of a form with a run of eleven hundred and something behind it, and a phrase on a printed form that a woman had confessed to in her own handwriting in a shed, and a mark in four parts that a man on the coast could not refuse to swear to, and a ledger in a depot in the hills that no man in this basin could be given, and about four hundred people in Rainmarket who had eaten well for nine days.

He had a sentence. He had carried it up eleven switchbacks and up four miles above a bridge and it was in his mouth, and it was the best sentence he had, and the whole of what was wrong with the instrument on the table was that nobody had ever been asked, and he had been a reader of a public register for five weeks in which the single rule was that a counterweight has to be named to the people who will bear it.

He had a room that could say it, at four hours a day, in Tallow Ward, free, to anybody who walked in.

He also had a register whose one rule was that if a counterweight is named as a district or a class there is nobody to name it to and the line does not go in at all. He had watched that happen in about ninety seconds in a shed to a man in a good coat who had built the first one.

The clause on the table would go into a room at Verrow in about three weeks. There was nothing in it that was not true, and no form in this kingdom on which a person might be stopped, and a man with no office could not enter a word on a wall, and if he said the sentence on the low-ground road tonight about nine people would carry it up the road and in three weeks it would be in a room, and in three weeks Rell would be in that room, and Rell would say the thing he had said five days ago on a stair — go and look for the room that signed the paper, not the room that is keeping the number — and it would be true, and it would be the whole of the defence, and it would be enough to get the clause confirmed, and there would be nothing afterwards.

"It was fair," said Rell, when it was done, and he was not quoting anybody, and he did not know he had said it. "The paper is clean, the figures balance, the number of holdings is right, and the market is in four regions' interest. There is no finding in this and there is nothing to appeal and I am not going to be blamed for it by anybody and I am not going to be thanked for it either, and I would like the record to say that a man in a good coat stood in that room for an hour and read my schedule four times and said no."

He put the sheet in the satchel and put the satchel under his chair and looked at Marek Vale, who was still standing four feet off the table.

"Go home," he said. "You are not going to do anything and I have given you the chance to say so to my face, which is more than most of them do, and I would like to go and be useful at something."
