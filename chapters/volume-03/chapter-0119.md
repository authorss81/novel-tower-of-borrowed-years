# Chapter 119 — Whether an Unreadable Mark Is a Witness

The second-floor hearing room in Bell Stair was built for thirty and there were nine in it on the sixteenth of the rains month, and one of the nine was Bel Harrow, who chaired, and who was not going to vote on what came up, and who had no clerk, and who served the minutes himself at about four minutes a page, and had been doing it in longhand, alone, since the fourth month.

The table of five had four people at it. Harrow in the chair. Sedge for the Chamber, who was a third-grade clerk with no service length anywhere and who said on the way in that he had been clerk of the day for nothing and had come. Zorka Vendl for the Fund, who declined money for a sitting, twice, in the first person, and had asked for it to be minuted that she was still in the room. Aldis Kern, for the ward, in his own name, under a standing provision he could recite in about nine words and did not recite.

And the Trust's seat, empty, for the fifth time, with *in the ordinary course* still in quotation marks in a minute in a drawer in that building.

Also in the room: Neris Kade, not a member and not entitled to a vote, holding a sheet whose only function is to hold four figures in the order they are called. Ilse Ferrow, who has a lapsed licence and a right to be in a room where Crown March paper is being dealt with, and who used it and is not a member. Marek Vale, who is not a member of anything and cannot be a member of anything, and who is the only person in the basin who can be in a room without being on a list, and who was let in by a door-keeper who did not write him down. And two clerks of that building, who took no part in anything and were not thanked for the room and went out at the fourth bell.

Marek asked the question at about the fourth bell and it took him about ninety seconds and he had been carrying a version of it for three weeks.

---

"There is a form," he said. "It is a Crown March form and it is correct, and it has been served on eleven parties in a district, and in the bottom corner of it there is a mark in four parts in a calendar that no officer of this room can read. And there is a tide table in this kingdom and it is in two rooms above a chandler's shop and it belongs to a man who will not swear to a hand and has told me in his own rooms that he does not know what the marks are.

"And on the twenty-fourth of the tenth month a man signed that form in a room over a shop on the low-ground road, correctly, in advance of a season, on a standing authority, and every figure in the stack balanced, and there is no form in this kingdom in which any person or office may be stopped.

"And what I want this room to answer is one question and I am going to put it in the plainest words I have got. *Is a mark that nobody in this kingdom can read a witness to the paper it is on.*"

Nobody spoke for about four seconds.

"That is the right question," said Ilse Ferrow, "and I asked it in that room on the twenty-fourth of the tenth month in a different form, and I got a man in a good coat to tell me he knew exactly what he had certified, and I would like it entered that I am not asking it for the second time for my own reasons."

---

Harrow wrote the question down before anybody answered it, which is what a chair does, and then he read the four terms.

The four terms have been confirmed at more sittings than anybody has counted and they take about eleven minutes. The chair presides until the board is satisfied it can sit without him, reviewed at each sitting and written. The Fund cannot pay a sitting and a ward cannot be assessed a rate at all and neither of those is going to change. Attendance and declining is standard March practice and is not going to be discussed. And term four: *a member who dissents may have the grounds written in their own words, in the finding, and the duty to write them is the board's and not the member's.*

"Term four stands," said Sedge.

"Stands."

"Stands," said Vendl, and then said, as she said at the last two sittings, "and I have been asked three times to take money for this afternoon and I have declined three times and I am still in the room."

"Stands," said Kern.

---

Then the Chair put the question out loud, which took him about eleven seconds, and the reason he put it out loud is in the minute in his own hand and is the reason anybody in this basin reads that minute at all: a question that is only written down cannot be answered later by anybody who was not in the room, and this board has a way of being asked the same thing in a year by a building that has read its own minutes.

"Members," said Bel Harrow. "Is a mark that no officer of this board can read a witness to the paper it is on. I am asking it as a chair and not as a member because I do not vote in a money matter and a certification of an instrument for service is a money matter, since what is certified on that sheet is an advance of eleven thousand and some hundreds of pounds and the recovery of it. So: four votes and one of them is not mine."

"Can I answer it," said Ilse Ferrow, "in one line, and then stop?"

"You may answer it in one line."

"No," said Ilse Ferrow. "Not because anybody is uncertain. Because the whole of what a mark does is that one man on a coast who has never heard of any of us can read it, and a mark that everybody else in this kingdom could also read would be worth nothing and would not exist, and there are about nine hundred of them a year. And so the answer cannot be *no*, and the answer cannot be *yes* either, and what is in front of this room is not a question about marks. It is a question about whether four people in a room are the right four people, and there is no form in this kingdom in which a room may be declared the wrong room."

---

The Chair wrote for about four minutes and then stopped and put his pen down and said the thing that made the afternoon, and he said it to the table and not to Marek.

"I cannot find on the instrument," he said.

Sedge said, "That is not the same as *no*."

"It is not the same as *no* and it is not the same as *yes* and there is no third thing in this kingdom that a finding can say, and I have got a sheet of paper and about four pages of it and about eleven minutes left before this room is four hours old, and I am going to write down that the board was unable to find upon the instrument, in those words, because those are the words, and because if I write anything else then a person in a building in about a year is going to read it and find a decision in it that this room did not take."

He wrote it. Then he turned the sheet about a quarter so that the table could see it, and it said, in a long clerk's hand, that the board had been asked whether a mark that no officer of it could read was a witness to the paper it was on, and that the board was unable to find upon the instrument, and that no officer of the Chamber of Accounts, and no officer of the Fund, and no person in this room, is able to read it, and that the tide table which could read it is in two rooms above a chandler's shop on the low-ground road and belongs to a person who is not an officer of anything.

And at the foot of the paragraph, before he signed it, he put one more sentence, and it is the shortest sentence in the minute and it is the reason this batch happened.

*A mark that no officer can read is not a witness.*

---

"The Chair cannot do that," said Vendl, about nine seconds later, in the flat voice of a woman who has been in a room like this nineteen years. "Bel. You cannot put a rule in a minute."

"I have not put a rule in a minute. I have put a finding in a minute, and a finding is what this room is for, and there is no office in this kingdom that a minute can make do anything, and that is the reason it is safe to write down and the reason it is going to be useless."

"It is going to be in a drawer."

"It is going to be in a drawer with four other things in it," said Bel Harrow, "and one day somebody in that building is going to take all five out and use two of them, and I would rather be one of the five than not be in the drawer at all, because the alternative is that this room said a true thing and did not write it down, and I have been a chair long enough to know that a true thing said out loud in a room and not written is a thing that gets said again louder by somebody who heard it and did not write it."

Nobody thanked him and nobody said it was useless out loud, and the Chair went back to the sheet.

---

Then Kern asked for it, and it is in the minute in his own words, and it cost him something, and he knew what it would cost him before he asked, which is the only reason it is worth putting in a record.

"Term four," said Aldis Kern. "In my own words, and I want the Chair to write them and not me, because I write a bad hand and I have never been able to hold a pen for a paragraph."

The Chair wrote. It took about four minutes and it is the only part of that minute with anybody's name at the foot of it in the first person.

And the grounds were these: that a yard in Tallow Ward has a room, and the room has a rule, and the rule is that a person may enter a line about their own paper and nobody may enter a line about anybody else's, and the yard has twice refused to fix a thing in its own method on that rule, both times on its own reason and both times without being asked twice, and that a round which repeats is the third box on the settlement desk's form; that the board was not unable to answer the question, and the board should be minuted as having chosen not to answer it, because a refusal can be repeated by anybody who turns up on the next morning and a choice has to be defended; and that he would like it entered that he knew, when he asked for his grounds to be written, that a minute is a record in a building a quarter of a mile from the ground his rule was made on, and that a man in a good coat can produce a yard's own method at that yard in about nine months, and that he had asked for it anyway, because a dissent nobody can read out is not a dissent, it is a temper.

---

Marek Vale sat at the back of a room he had asked a question in and understood, about four seconds after the Chair finished writing, that he had put Tallow Ward into a Crown March record.

Not the ward. The method. The rule a yard made in the rain at a tank with about forty people in it, with no name at the foot of it and no office behind it and a boy of nineteen reading a fourth line out twice at the ordinary speed — that had been a thing you could only say in the room where it had been made, to the people it had been made in front of, and it had now been said in a room in Bell Stair, in a long clerk's hand, with Aldis Kern's name under it, and it would be in a drawer in that building, and there is no form in this kingdom in which a man with no office may take a sentence back out of a room.

He had not known it when he asked. He had known it for about four seconds, which was four seconds more than he had known anything else in three months, and it was the whole of what he had, and he could not do one single thing with it.

Harrow folded the minute into four, and then into eight, because the sheet had run out at page four and the last two pages of the finding are on the back of a notice about a water office's list of fourteen lines, thirteen of which have been supplied and one of which is a school, and there is no office in the basin that has ever sent anybody against any of them.

"The finding goes down to Bell Stair tonight," he said, "in a locked box, with the four other minutes, and the Trust's seat is minuted empty for the fifth time, and there is still no date for anything, and there is nobody to send it to."

"Where does Kern's paragraph go?" Sedge asked. "Last time it went on a nail at a tank."

"It stays in the box," said Aldis Kern, from the other side of the table, before the Chair had answered, and he said it in about nine words. "I did not ask to have my name on a nail in a yard and I am not going to be the reason a Crown March record has a sentence of mine on a wall in Tallow Ward, and the minute is where I asked for it to be. The Chair has a drawer. I have a yard. I know which of the two of us is the one people come to, and it is not him, and it is not me either, it is the boy with the chalk, and he is nineteen and he did not write the rule and he reads it out."

The room got up at about the seventh bell. The Fund's member had not eaten and went out first. The Trust's chair stayed as a chair and stayed empty. Bel Harrow wrote the date at the foot of the last page in his own hand, because there is no clerk, and there has never been a clerk, and the reason there is no clerk is in the fourth term and nobody has ever asked about it because the duty to write a member's grounds is the board's and not the member's and the duty to write the minutes was never anybody's and became his.
