# Chapter 124 — The Ground That Does Not Answer

Nine rows of a second-terrace holding came up on the twelfth of the growth month, four days after nothing happened, and by the fourteenth there were about forty people on that shoulder and it was the largest crowd that ground had held since the flood year.

They came for a look. A crop coming up is not a thing anybody in the Red Uplands stands in a field to watch, and about nine of the forty had come for a look and the other thirty-one had come because a woman of fifty-eight with a new door on her house was standing in the lane by the drill rows and would not go in.

Stana Peric's mother did not count. That is the valley's whole sentence about her and it took Marek Vale about a minute to work out that it is a compliment, and by then he had understood the yard at the top of the lane and the store with the lock below it and a name cut in a board in a loft four hundred yards away, and had understood that the name on that board is the mother's, and that the store with a lock has a holder and the holder is a woman of fifty-eight whom nobody in four terraces counts, and had understood that he had put a season on a household in order to save a store that somebody was going to empty anyway.

"That is your mother's ground," he said.

"It is my ground and it is her ground and there is a form in a district that says which and I have never seen it," said Stana Peric. "Do you want to see the store."

She said it the way a woman says *do you want to see the store* to a man who is obviously not going to be thanked for anything, and then she took him down the lane to it, and it was a stone building the size of a cart with a door that has been planed four times and a lock that is not new.

"There is a notice on a nail in the seed house up the lane," she said. "It has been there since the third month and forty people have read it and none of us have taken it down, and it says a sum is set off against the next account, and the next account is the seed, and the seed is in here."

"How much of it."

"About a third of what is in there," said Stana Peric, "and I do not know the figure and I am not going to be told the figure, because I have asked twice and a clerk came up a lane in the third month and would not say it to me in a lane and said the paper was the only place it was allowed to be."

She put her hand flat on the door of her own store.

"And after the threshing, if these rows are what they look like, the seed in here is the seed out of them, and it is out of my ground and not bought, and the notice is about a sum and not about a store, and I do not know what to do with that and I have decided I am not going to think about it until the seed is in and shut."

---

Four miles and eleven switchbacks away, on the first terrace, Mileva Vukic spent the twelfth of the growth month doing four ordinary things and finding that three of them were wrong.

She is thirty-eight and she has a spring that comes up in the field behind her house at about the second bell and has come up in the field behind her house every year of her life, and on the twelfth of the growth month it came up, and it was brackish.

She put her hand in it and tasted it and put her hand in it again and it was still brackish, and she went and got a jar and filled the jar and carried the jar to the front and put it on the rail, and about nine people came and looked at the jar over the next two days and every one of them said the same thing, which is that the field had gone sour in the third month because the drain above her had been cut and there is a thing about a new drain.

That is what about nine people said, and Mileva Vukic said yes, that is what had happened, and she said it eleven times over two days, and she did not believe it after the fourth.

The second thing was the pear tree her father had planted, which is forty years old and stands by the gate, and which came out in leaf in the second month as a pear tree does, and which came out in leaf again on the ninth of the growth month and was carrying a second crop of blossom on a tree that had already set.

The third was a boundary stone at the top of her upper lot, a squat grey thing that has been the corner of two lots since before the flood year, which was standing nine inches to the left of where it had stood in the seed month in ground that nobody had touched.

The fourth was a heifer that has been on that holding since the seed month and did not come back in heat, which is not a thing a woman of thirty-eight says out loud on a first terrace in the growth month, and which she said out loud on the first terrace in the growth month.

---

Nobody on the first terrace could have said any of those things to anybody, and that is the entire finding of the twelfth of the growth month, and Marek Vale did not know any of it for six days and has never once been able to make it into a sentence that anybody could act on.

They could all be the drain. They could all be the warm nine days in the third month and the cold that came after. They could all be a thing nobody in this valley has a name for, which is what four hundred acres of the Red Uplands said about a harvest in the tenth month of MR 413, and which was true, and which did not make the water any less brackish.

None of them had happened yet. That was the part that made the jar on the rail the loneliest object in the basin.

A wall falls in a year. A wall is a fact about a wall and it is true the day it happens and everybody can see it and a man in a good coat can be shown it. A spring that tastes of the low ground in the growth month is a spring that will cost a family a season in the *second* year, when the upper lot will not hold a crop and the low lot will not hold a root, and there is no form anywhere in this kingdom in which next year's ground can be entered, served, sworn to, or shown.

Mileva Vukic put the jar on the rail and left it there for nine days, and then she put it inside and did not mention it again until the harvest month.

---

She came down to the second terrace on the seventeenth of the growth month and walked into a yard at about the second bell where about nine people were doing four jobs at once, and asked Zofia Teran a question that took her about a minute to get out.

"You keep it."

"I keep it."

"Then I want one thing in it and I am not going to sit down." Mileva Vukic stood in the yard with her hands behind her back the way people stand when they have come four miles to do a thing and have not decided whether to do it. "The twelfth. Whatever day you think it was, and I know you will not say the day because you have never once put a day at the head of a line in your life, and I am not asking you to put one at the head. I am asking you to write down that on about the twelfth of the growth month a spring on the first terrace came up brackish, and a pear tree came out twice, and a stone moved, and I am not asking you to write why."

Zofia Teran got the book off the shelf inside the door.

She wrote for about two minutes and she wrote it in four lines and she did not number them and she did not put anything at the head of the page, and when she had finished she read it back out loud once, at the ordinary speed, which she does anyway whenever anybody has been in her yard, and not because anybody had asked her to.

"Does that say what happened," said Mileva Vukic, "or does it say what I said happened."

"It says what you said happened," said Zofia Teran. "That is the difference between it and a record and you knew it when you walked down the hill and you came anyway, and I want that said in this yard, because in about four weeks somebody is going to come up this road who is going to want to know whether the first terrace had bad water in the growth month, and the answer is that a woman I have known nineteen years told me so on the seventeenth, and there is no form in this kingdom on which she can be served with the question."

"You are not going to tell them I was here."

"I am not going to tell them anything," said Zofia Teran, "because you came here on the seventeenth and it is a book and it is a woman's account of what she was told and what she was told is not what was said, and I have said that in front of about nine people in a yard in the third month and it has not changed since."

---

Marek Vale was not in that yard and did not know for six days that it had happened, and on the eighteenth of the growth month he was in a lane on the second terrace at about the fourth bell with a lamp and a season and a name, and Zofia Teran came out of the door with the exercise book under her arm and did not go past him.

"You have not been down here since the seventh," she said.

"No."

"Then you have not heard anything, and I am going to tell you one thing and one thing only, and then you are going to go up the road, and I am not going to say the name."

"I am not going to ask you—"

"I know what you are not going to ask me," said Zofia Teran. "That is the whole reason I am telling you.

"Somebody came down off the first terrace on the seventeenth with a thing that has got nothing to do with your ground, and it is a thing about a season, and I put it in the book in four lines with nothing at the head of it, and I am telling you that it is in the book because there is going to come a day when two of these are next to each other on a table and somebody is going to say *look at the day*, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a book of mine can be put next to a thing of yours, and if that day comes then the day is the only thing in either of us that anybody can check."

She went inside and shut the door, and Marek Vale stood in a lane above a stone bridge with his hands in his coat, and the two halves of that week fitted together in his head with a sound he could feel in his teeth, and he did not go up the road.

---

On the low ground that same week, a woman of forty-one called Margit Sova carried a basket and a list and no money down a road she had walked for twenty-six years, and stopped in the middle of it, and stood there for about a minute in the ordinary way a person stands in a road when the thing they have come for is not there.

The chaff stall had been on that corner since before the flood year. There is a reason for chaff: it is what a household with a hen puts down, and it is what a person boils a pot on when there is no wood, and Margit Sova had bought a double handful of it once a week for twenty-six years.

There was no chaff and the man who keeps the stall said the second sowing on the low ground had not come, and that it was thin and slow and that anybody who wanted chaff in the third month would be lucky, and that he was saying that because the stall had been empty four days running and he had run out of things to say about it.

She wrote it on her list. She wrote it the way she wrote everything on that list, in a hand that had got worse over twenty-six years, with no head at the top of the page and no rule and no total, and she has still got the list, and there is no office in this city that may be given it.

Then she went home and fed her hen what there was and did not tell anybody, because the price had stopped falling in the second month and had not moved since, and a woman who has been watching a thing for twenty-six years is not going to tell a street about a stall.

But the rate that had stood since the second month of MR 414, and that had made Rainmarket the best-fed city in nine years, was not a rate about grain any more by the eighteenth of the growth month. It was a rate about a stall with nothing on it, four days running, on a road where there had never been nothing.
