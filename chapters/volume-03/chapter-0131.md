# Chapter 131 — The Bar That Does Not Sit Flat

The bar across the store room at the Gate had been put there in the flood year and it had never once sat flat, because the door has dropped about a quarter of an inch on its hinge in nine years of wet, and a bar across a door that has dropped is a bar with a wedge under one end and a padlock through the loop of a chain at the other.

Milan Vray knew all of that without looking at it. He was the yard-man and the store was the yard's and the bar was his, in the sense that a thing a yard puts across a door at night belongs to whoever is standing in the yard in the morning, and that has been the arrangement in this yard for longer than anybody could put a year to.

Marek Vale came into the Gate yard on the twelfth of the harvest month at about the sixth bell, on foot, having come down eleven switchbacks and four miles in a day and a night, and he had a coat on that had not been dry since the growth month and he had not slept in a bed, and there were nine people in the yard because the yard is where people are at the sixth bell and the register had shut at the fourth and the shed was shut and the trestle was folded against the wall and the form was under it.

He had a question and he had been carrying it for four days and he got it out wrong.

---

"You cannot go and tell a whole terrace what a counterweight is," said Vray, before Marek had said anything at all. "I have been in this yard nineteen years and I have watched a good many men go up that road with a thing to say, and every one of them said it in a yard down here first, and not one of them has ever come back and said what happened."

Marek looked at him.

"I have not asked you anything yet."

"You have got the shape of it in your face," said Vray, "and I have seen that shape on a better face than yours. Sit down on the trestle, it is not wet, and if you say it standing up you will say it to the tank and not to me."

So Marek said it sitting down, and he said it badly, and it took him four minutes, and about half of it was him explaining a mechanism and Vray did not let him get through the first half of it.

"You keep telling me what a counterweight is," said Vray. "I do not need to know what it is. I have moved seed in this yard for nineteen years and I have never once been asked what anything weighs against what. What I want to know is what you are going to do with a terrace, and you have not told me that, and you have told me the other thing twice."

Marek said the other thing, and then he said the true one, and it came out in nine words, and it was: *I have got to be in the room, and I cannot get them in a room.*

"That is closer," said Vray. "Go on."

---

The four questions at the yard's counter were the only instrument Marek had ever found in this basin that did what he needed, and he put them out on the flat of the trestle with his thumb, one at a time, the way he had watched a rope-maker of thirty-nine put them out in a yard two years ago.

*Is that your line. Is that the person behind it. Is it your words. Is there a person behind it who is in this room.*

"He has been in a shed a fortnight being refused on the fourth one," said Vray. "I heard about it. Everybody in this ward heard about it and nobody in this ward was surprised, and that is not a thing to be proud of."

"I know what the four questions are for," said Marek.

"Then you know they are no use to you," said Vray. "Look at them. The first three are for a piece of paper a man has brought in with him about himself. Every one of them wants the paper. The fourth one is the only one that does not want a paper and the fourth one wants nine people in a room, and there is no office in this kingdom that can put nine people in a room, and the office that most nearly could is a granary board a night and a day up the river and it has never heard of a single one of them."

---

Then he got up, and went across the yard to the store room door, and put his hand on the bar.

"You have been looking at that door since you came in," he said.

"I have been avoiding it."

"Both." Vray got his shoulder under the bar and lifted, and the bar came up about a quarter of an inch on the dropped end, and the chain came slack, and the padlock came out of the hasp in one movement because a padlock in a hasp is the easiest thing in this basin to open. "That is the store. That is the yard's store and there is seed in it and oil and the bench-saw and about four hundred pounds of nothing much. It has been locked at this hour every night since the flood year and it has been opened every morning by a man with a key, and the man with the key is me, and I have a key on a nail inside the office and the office is locked with a second key and that is on a nail inside the house I sleep in, and that is the whole of it. Nineteen years."

He let the bar down and the padlock went back into the hasp in one movement and the bar sat on the wedge and did not lie flat.

"You cannot get it to sit," said Marek.

"You cannot get it to sit," said Vray, "and I have stopped minding, and about four years ago I put a second chain on the high side to take up the slack, and that is the only repair that has ever been made to that door and I made it myself and it is in no works anywhere in this city."

---

"Now listen," he said, and he leaned on the door. "You have been up a road with a thing you want to say to nine people and you have come down here to find out how to say it. And the answer is a key. That is the whole of the answer and it took me nineteen years and a dropped door to be able to say it without laughing.

"A counterweight is not written down. Nobody writes a counterweight down. You cannot write one down because the moment there is a word at the top of it there is a kind at the top of it, and a kind is a thing a person is put in, and then it is a schedule, and then the schedule is a thing a man in a good coat can produce at somebody in nine months. What you have been carrying up that road is a piece of paper with a kind on it and no names in it, and there is not a worse thing in this kingdom than that and I have seen two of them."

"Then what is it," said Marek. "Say the other half."

"It is held," said Vray. "A counterweight is held. The way that door is held. Somebody keeps it, the way a store room with a lock has a keyholder, and the keyholder is a person, and a person can be asked, and a person can say no."

Marek sat down on the trestle again.

"That is what I came down for," he said, "and I have had it since the ninth of the growth month and it took me four days to come down and say it in a yard."

"Then why did you not say it up there."

"Because up there it is a sentence," said Marek, "and I have learned what happens to my sentences up there and I have had it happen three times."

---

"You want me to hold it," said Vray.

"I want there to be somebody in the basin who holds it."

"That is the same sentence with a hat on and it is what the chamber wants and the Crown March wants and the harbour wants, and every one of them says it as a favour and not as a job." Vray shook his head. "No. And I am going to give you the reason in my own trade and it is about a nail, and you can take it up a road or leave it.

"That key is on a nail. I have never had it on a list. There is not a piece of paper in this city with my name on it that says I keep this store, and if there were, it would be a name on a paper, and then this store would be mine, and then I could be asked about it in a room, and then somebody would have to be able to give me another store, and then I would have to be paid for this one, and there is no money in this ward that has ever been able to pay a yard-man, and I have not asked for any and I am not going to.

"So: no name, no asking, and nothing anybody can put in front of anybody. And that is exactly why that store is safe and exactly why your thing is not. Your thing has a kind on it and no names, and a store has a key and no name, and one of those two is a man being careful and the other one is a piece of paper, and they look identical from a doorway."

---

"You could have me instead," said Marek. "I could hold it. I would not need a form, I would not need a nail, and I would not be in this ward in six months."

"That," said Vray, "is the first sensible thing you have said since you came in this yard, and it is the reason the answer is no, and I am going to say it slowly.

"I could come and ask you. That is the whole of what a keyholder is. It is not a name, it is a person who can be asked. And I would come, and I would ask, and you would not be here, because you would be up a road, and the answer would be a thing said to a keyholder, and it is not a form and it cannot be kept and it is worth nothing and it would be the only sentence anybody in this valley has ever heard about that paper.

"And you would have known that when you offered, and you offered anyway, and I want you to understand that I have understood it, and I am not saying it to be clever. I am saying it because in about four minutes you are going to go off up that road with a sentence and I would like you to go with the right one."

---

He put his hand flat on the bar and pushed it about a quarter of an inch and watched it not go down.

"About four minutes," he said. "Long enough. Here is the last of it and then I am going to sort the oil.

"A counterweight is not a receipt. You have been using that word in your head all the way down this road and I can see it on you, and it is worth stopping at, because a receipt is a thing you hand a man when he has handed you something. There is nothing in four terraces that anybody handed anybody. The nine words on that paper were printed before the four terraces had a bad year, and the only hand that has ever touched the whole of it is a man's in a room in the hills filling in a number of holdings, and he did it correctly, and he has been told that what he filled in is a class of people, and he went on doing it, and I am not saying he was a coward.

"But there is no receipt for it, because nobody gave anybody anything, and a thing nobody gave cannot be receipted, and a thing that cannot be receipted is not a debt that anybody can be handed to a door — it is just a weight sitting on a floor, and the only way anybody has ever picked one up in this basin is by deciding to, out loud, in front of the people it will land on, and there is no form for deciding and there is no office that will send anybody to watch you do it, and if you do it anyway then you did it, and nobody can prove it, and you will not be able to prove it either."

---

Marek Vale sat on a trestle in a yard in Tallow Ward for about four minutes and said nothing, and then he said the last part of it out loud once, to make sure it was true in a mouth, and it was.

"Two years," said Vray. "You have been in this city how long?"

"Ten years and about nine months."

"And in ten years and about nine months you have got to the place where the whole of it is one nail," said Vray, "and I would not give up the nail, and I would not be able to help you if I did, and that is a thing you are going to have to take up the road with you and I would rather you took it than that you left it here and came back in a month with a different question."

He unhooked the chain and took the bar down and shouldered it and put it back across the door, and the dropped end went onto the wedge, and it did not lie flat, and he did not look at it.

Then he went to sort the oil, and Marek went and got his bag out of the lean-to where he had left it, and put the strap over his shoulder, and went out of the Gate yard with one sentence and a day and a half of road in front of him, and there were nine people in the yard behind him and none of them knew that anything had been decided in it.

Marek Vale got about two hundred yards down the lane and then stopped in the wet by the tank, which was not his errand, and stood there long enough to work out that the whole of what he had come down for was a sentence that belonged to a yard-man, and that the sentence was that a counterweight is kept by somebody, and that a store room with a lock has a keyholder, and that a keyholder is a person, and that a person can be asked.

And then he worked out that the only person he had ever known in this city who could be asked about anything in that shape was a man he had passed in a lane a hundred times a year for ten years, who kept a nail, and who had said *I have been in this yard nineteen years* and had not once offered to be anything else.

The first terrace is a day of road and eleven switchbacks and he would be on it by the fourth bell after tomorrow, and there were nine people on it and a woman with a jar, and he had not the least idea how a man begins that, and he went up the lane with the bag on his shoulder and the oil can being tipped out behind him.
