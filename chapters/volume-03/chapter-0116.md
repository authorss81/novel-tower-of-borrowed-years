# Chapter 116 — A Column With Nothing at the Head of It

The man in the lane was forty-four and had come off the Crown March post on the fourteenth of the third month with a letter-case and a sealed sheet, and he asked at the tank whether there was a room in Tallow Ward in which a person could ask what a piece of paper was, and about nine people in the yard told him there was, and about nine other people told him it was a shed with a bench in it and a queue outside it, and both of those were true and he weighed them for about four seconds and then went and stood in the lane.

His name was Emmerich Tace and he was a clerk in the Chancellor's office at Crown March, and he said that when he said it, in the ordinary voice of a man putting down a thing he is going to be asked for later, and he said it in about eleven words, and about four people in the lane heard it and about four hundred yards of Rainmarket did not.

---

The Gate shed is eleven feet by nine. It had a germination bench against the wall on the days the rota said, and a trestle under the form, and a levy box on the second trestle on days the levy was being taken, and a wall list with a column at the right-hand end of it that had nothing at the head of it, and a notice in Sedge's hand about how anybody may ask.

There were about eighty people in the lane outside it at the fourth bell and about twenty in the shed, which holds twenty-two, and Teodor Lask was reading somebody's second line out loud with his back to a bench frame, twice, at the ordinary speed.

Tace stood in the lane for about four minutes and then came in and stood at the back with the case under his arm, and Sanne Droll came out of the yard and looked at him and looked at the case and said, before he had said anything at all:

"You are not setting that on the trestle."

"I am not."

"You were about to. I could see you measuring it." She put her thumb and forefinger about a foot apart. "That is a yard's board and a yard's bench and there is a notice on that wall that says what this room is, and it does not say a Crown March office, and if you put a piece of paper of yours on that trestle then in nine months a man is going to stand in that lane and say this is a ward's room and a ward's room is running a Crown March return, and I have watched this yard since the eighteenth of the ninth month and I am not going to be the reason there is a thing on that trestle with a district's name at the head of it."

"That is fair," said Tace. "Where may I stand."

"You may stand where you are. The lane is the ward's as much as the shed is. If you want the shed emptied for it there is no form for that and I have been here nineteen years and I would know."

---

He did not have to make a speech. That is what Marek Vale thought about afterwards, and thought about for about a week, and what the fourteen or fifteen people in the lane who were still there at the seventh bell said to each other on their way home: that the whole of the most expensive afternoon anybody in Tallow Ward has had since the year turned was a man standing at the back of a shed with a letter-case under his arm, asking questions, and being told the answer about nine times, flatly, by people who did not agree with him and did not know him.

He put the letter-case on the step and took a sheet out of it and did not unfold it, because Droll had told him not to, and the not unfolding it was, in the end, the only thing he did wrong.

"I have an instruction," he said. "I will read it and I will not read it out. It is a schedule of the persons in four upland terraces who will bear a settlement weight under the season instrument of the second month of MR 412, by name, and the office wants it in a fortnight."

"Who is the office?"

"The Chancellor's."

Marek Vale came forward about four feet and could not get any further than that, because the shed was full and the bench was in the way and this is what a shed is.

"What does she want it for," he said.

"That is a thing I am entitled to ask and you are not entitled to an answer, and I am going to give you the reason anyway, because she gave it to me in a room in the hills on the fourth and told me to use it if anybody stood in my way." Tace turned the sheet about ninety degrees in his hands without opening it. "She wants to know whether there is a room of people behind that paper. That is the whole of it. If there is a room then she can send a person to the room and have the terms with the people in it. If it is a district then there is nothing to send a person to, and she will do whatever she does next without knowing, which she does about forty times a season and does not enjoy.

"And that is correct, and I want it said in this lane that I have been given a reason and that the reason is a good one, because I have been in four rooms this month and been told to have one of these. And she says the fortnight, because the works go up in the third month and after the works go up these people are in the ground until the fourth."

---

Nobody in the shed wrote anything down. Teodor Lask stopped reading when the word *schedule* came out of the man's mouth and then started again at the beginning of the second line, which was correct, and which about nine people noticed.

Sanne Droll asked the question, and she asked it the way she asked the four questions at the counter.

"Have you got a form."

"I have got a form. It is a schedule of persons to bear a settlement weight. It has a head at the top of it and four columns."

"Then you have a piece of paper with a word at the top of a column," said Droll, "and there is one in this room that has a column with nothing at the top of it, and about nineteen people decided in about half an hour not to name it and then voted in four minutes not to fill it and not to rule it out, and I am not going to have either of those two things confused with each other in my shed, and you can tell your office that a yard's wall is not a form and I have not got the authority to turn it into one and neither has the yard."

Emmerich Tace looked at the wall list for a while.

"Can I copy the column head," he said. "I am not asking to fill it. I am asking to write one word at the top of a piece of paper of mine so that a clerk in a building in the hills has somewhere to put names when he gets them."

---

And that is where the afternoon turned, and it turned because a man at the back of a shed had been in a cellar eleven steps down and a room over a chandler's shop and a loft four miles above a stone bridge, and could read the wall list, and knew what it was.

The wall list has about forty lines on it. It is not a roll and it has no total and there is no head at the right-hand end of the fourth column and there has not been one since the second week, and the lines on it are about forty pieces of paper that people brought in and asked about, and every one of the forty was entered by a person about their own paper.

And Emmerich Tace stood at the back of the shed with a form in his hand and asked, quite reasonably, the only question anybody had asked anybody that morning, which was whether anybody in Tallow Ward knew a person in the four terraces of the Red Uplands.

And the wall list had four names on it that were not Tallow Ward's.

A woman who came down four miles and eleven switchbacks in the second month of last year with a piece of her own handwriting in a cloth. A season examiner for the Red Uplands who is not a member of anything and has no standing anywhere and was named at the door of this shed and got a hand on nothing. A man with no office who reads the register out four hours a day and enters one word of it and cannot be on any list because a person may enter a line about their own paper and nobody may enter a line about anybody else's. And a haulier who was in this yard in the ninth month about a road that was opened on purpose for a Crown March paper and who stood at the back and said so out loud in front of about nine people, on purpose, because he wanted it written down.

"That is four," said Tace, "out of about forty, and about a fifth of the people in that lane, and every one of them is a person who came down off those terraces, or came in here, and asked about a piece of paper."

"Yes," said Marek Vale.

"That is not a list of anybody who lives there. That is a list of the people who asked questions."

"Yes," said Marek Vale, "and I want to say it before you write it down, because I have been in a room over a chandler's shop on this road in the last ten days and I have been up that road twice, and I know what is on the other end of those four names, and it is not a valley. It is a list of everybody who has been in a room about this. It is the best a list can be in this basin and it is not a list of a district, and there are four hundred and some people in those four terraces and about nine of them are on any paper any of us has ever seen, and four of the nine have been in this shed."

---

Nobody in the lane said the next part, and Marek Vale said it, and he had the sentence ready and it came out about four seconds too late, which is a thing that has happened to him before and which he noticed happening.

"You are going to write the four down."

"Emmerich," said Tace, "I have been given a schedule with a head at the top of it and a column under the head and the office wants the column filled. And I have been in a room in the hills on the fourth of this month and I was told that a list with a head and nothing in it is not a partial list, it is an office that has not done its job. And that is a thing I have believed since I was twenty-two and I am not going to un-believe it in a shed in Tallow Ward because a man with no office has told me the four names are the wrong four. They may be the wrong four. They are the four I have."

He put the form on his knee and wrote the four names in the column in a small clerk's hand, and the mark in the bottom corner of his form stayed in the bottom corner and went unread as it has gone unread in every room in this kingdom since before the year turned, and about nine people in the lane watched a man write four names on a piece of paper and not one of them said anything, and Marek Vale could not say anything, because the first question at that counter every morning is *is that your line, is that the person behind it, is it your words*, and he was not on the form, and a person may enter a line about their own paper and nobody may enter a line about anybody else's, and he had helped write the rule.

---

Then Petar Duss came down the lane about a quarter past the sixth bell with a rope round his wrist and about nine other men behind him, because there had been forty carts on the bridge on the fifth and there were thirty on the sixth and a man who has been on the road thirty-one years knows what that is.

He stopped, because there was a man in a lane with a form on his knee and a foreign mark in the corner of it, and he looked at the corner for about three seconds, and then he said, out loud, in the ordinary voice, to about nine people:

"That is the one. I paid four shillings for that on a contract in the growth month to a man in two rooms over a shop on the low-ground road, and I never saw him put it on, and I have said on this road and on a bridge and on a stair that I knew the road was opened on purpose, and I have asked about nine people to write it down and about nine of them said no."

Emmerich Tace stood up off the step with his pen in his hand.

"You are Petar Duss of the fourth cart?"

"Thirty-one years on the road, four children, and a mark on a paper that belongs to nobody I have met."

"May I have the name of the man you paid."

"No."

"May I have the day."

"The growth month, and that is in a ledger of yours or it is not in a ledger of yours."

"Then, sir, you are on this form as a person who has stated a thing about the opening of an upland road."

"That is not what I said. I said I knew the road was opened on purpose and I would like somebody to write it down."

"Yes," said Tace, and wrote, and did not argue, and closed the form, and put it in the case, and said that the office would want him in a room in the hills in about eleven weeks and that he would be glad to come and that he would rather not be asked about the mark in the corner of anything at all.

---

Marek Vale wrote it down that night, in his own hand, on a sheet of his own, in the room above the lockkeeper's shop: *Petar Duss states that the upland road was opened on purpose for a Crown March paper, in the growth month, and that he did not know by whom, and that he has said so aloud at a bridge, on a stair and in a yard.*

He read it and then wrote two lines under it, which were: *Written by a private person with no office, from a conversation on a bridge, written in a lane, at the sixth bell. This is not evidence. It is not a copy. It has no form and it cannot be produced in any room and there is nobody in this kingdom that may be given it.* And then he put it in his coat, and not in the register, and not in the shed, and did not tell anybody he had written it, and understood while he was doing it that a man in a lane four miles above a stone bridge had been the fourth of the four names on a Crown March form and that his own name was on it, and that a haulier had been the fifth, and that Tallow Ward had just become a place where a list of the people who ask questions was being compiled in an office in the hills out of a shed with a bench in it.

---

From the step, where Aldis Kern had been standing for about an hour and had not gone in, he said four things and nobody thanked him for any of them.

"The bench comes in at the fourth bell on the days the rota says and it goes out at the sixth and it is not the ward's and the ward does not pay for it, and the yard has been told that and has been told it twice and has said no twice."

"That is not a fix to anything," said Sanne Droll.

"It is not a fix to anything. A fix is a thing you do to a hole so that the hole stops being a hole. We have had a hole in this yard since the eighteenth of the ninth month and we have refused twice to fix it on our own rule, and a round that repeats is the third box on the settlement desk's form, and I have said that on this step in the rain and I am saying it again on a dry day and I would like nobody to write it down." He looked at the lane. "We cost the yard its water on the days the bench is in and about nine people stopped coming and we have not got the nine and we are not going to ask for them, and if your office wants a list of that it can come and get a woman to say it out loud in here the way we have been saying it since the ninth of the ninth month."

Nobody thanked him and nobody wrote it down, and the lamp on the list in a building in Bell Stair was supplied in the third month, and the thirteenth of fourteen lines on it has been supplied since the third of this month, and the fourteenth line is a school off the north lane and is still nothing at all, and the fifth line of the form in the shed is empty and the sixth line is empty and always will be, and at the seventh bell the reader read the fourth line out twice, at the ordinary speed, to a room that had emptied, because the room does that.
