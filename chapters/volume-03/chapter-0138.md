# Chapter 138 — Eleven Lines on a Piece of Sack

Mirren Fisk lives in two rooms over a chandler's yard in Rainmarket that she has held for eleven years on a lease that has two names on it, one of them dead, and she keeps the Share's bench book at the bottom of a press in the front room and the seed house's counting slate in the back room, and the piece of sack is in a drawer in the front room under a folded apron, and it had been in that drawer since the third day of the harvest month last year and she had never taken it out of the front room in front of anybody.

She showed it to Marek Vale on the eleventh of the ninth month at about the fifth bell, and she did it without preamble and without a sentence of introduction, and she said:

"Sit there. Do not put your coat on that chair. I am going to take it out and I am not going to say anything about it for about four minutes, and then I am going to say why I have taken it out, and I told a man who was in this room a year ago that I would not, and he is not you, and I have thought about that for a week and I have decided to do it anyway."

She took it out.

It is a piece of sack about two hands wide and a foot and a half long, cut off a bale, and it has eleven lines on it in a hand that is not a clerk's hand, and there is nothing at the top of it, and there is nothing at the foot of it, and there is no column of names at the head of the lines and there is no total at the bottom of them, and each line is a household's rate.

---

"It went into the back of the bench book under the date," said Mirren Fisk, "and then it came home with me in the apron, because a sheet with nothing at the head of it does not stay in a building. That is not a principle. That is a thing that happened and then it happened again the next time and then I understood that it always happens.

"Do not touch it. There is no order on that and there is no reason for it. I would like you to look at it and not touch it, and I would like it noticed that I have never asked anybody to do that before and that I am asking it now and I have not got a rule."

---

"Here is our rule," she said, "and I did not write it and I cannot unwrite it and it is nineteen years old and it is correct.

"A lot of ours that does not come up becomes eleven days of labour, and the eleven days are divided across the members, and the days are divided by trays, and if a member's own tray is the one that failed then it is her tray and it is her days. And then, if the days do not come back in kind, it is a third of another member's kept-back, and Ansgar Drel carried his down from the top on his own back when the eleven went sand, and nobody asked him to and I would have carried it if he had not, and then the balance is a rate on the member's ground, and there is no form in this kingdom in which a rate on a member's ground goes, and it goes nowhere.

"Eleven lines, and the eleven are the eleven whose trays came up as sand, and eight other members owed days without a tray of their own failing, and the eight are on the sheet of days and not on that, and that is correct too, and I have never been able to make that sound right to anybody and it is right.

"And I have said all of that in a yard twice and to a man from a fund once, and I said it out loud in a room in Bell Stair in the middle of the year, and I have never been asked it by a person who wanted to know what a rate is. You want to know what a rate is. Do you."

---

"I do," said Marek.

"A rate is a charge on a ground," said Mirren Fisk. "That is the whole of it and there is not a second half and there is no version of it with a second half, and I have had two years to look for one, and I have found that every time somebody hands me a form with a second half in it, the second half is a person's name.

"That is what happens. A rate goes on a ground, and the ground is a piece of the low ground with a roll number on it, and the roll number is a number, and a number about a ground cannot be told anything and cannot be asked anything and cannot be in a room, and so a man puts a name in, because a charge has to have somebody on it or it is not a charge, it is a note. And the name is the name of the woman who holds the ground, and she has not agreed to anything, and the office will say in about nine months that the holder was notified, and the holder is a woman who was notified of a thing that happened to a piece of dirt.

"And you have been in this city ten years and you know that sentence, because a man in a good coat worked it out on a flat stone in the third month in front of nine people, and it is on a nail in a seed house four miles and eleven switchbacks up that road, and I have read it more than once and I have never once been able to improve it."

---

"You are talking about a receipt," said Marek.

"I am talking about what a rate is not," said Mirren Fisk, "and yes, and I am going to say the rest of it because you have brought me a piece of paper and I have been waiting two years for a piece of paper and I am not going to pretend I am calm.

"A receipt is a thing you hand a man when he has handed you something. You go into a shop and you give a man two shillings and he gives you a piece of paper that says what he took off you, and the paper is for you, and it means that the two shillings are gone and done with, and nobody can come at you about the two shillings again. That is what a receipt is and that is the whole of what a receipt is for.

"And a counterweight is not a receipt. There is nothing in any of this that anybody handed anybody. The rain came in the warm week and the low ground's second sowing did not come and eleven trays came up as sand out of a shed that was shut up wet in the flood year, and nobody in this basin gave anything to anybody, and so there is nothing to receipt, and so there is no piece of paper that can be handed to a woman saying *here is what you gave and here is what it cost*, and there never will be one, and the only thing anybody can do is write a figure on a piece of sack and carry it home in an apron and know that in about two years a woman is going to be standing where you are standing with a number she cannot pay in money and does not have in seed, and she is going to be told it is a rule, and she is going to say yes, because there is nothing else to say.

"And that is how it was decided and by whom and on what day is nobody's business to say, and I have said that in a yard as well."

---

The door at the bottom of the stairs was open and Neris Kade came in with a satchel and four sheets in it and put the satchel on the table and did not sit down.

She did not look at the sack. She looked at the four lines on it for about four seconds from where she was standing and then she took a sheet out of the satchel and held it in front of her and said:

"I have four questions that I ask at a counter. I have asked them at about nine counters since the ninth month of last year and they are not my questions, they are a yard's, and I have never been able to improve them and I have never wanted to improve them.

"May I put them to that."

"You may," said Mirren Fisk.

Kade put her own sheet down on the table, which she did not have to do and which nobody asked her to do, and she did not pick the sack up, and she said:

"May I ask, is that your line." And then she stopped, and she stood there for about four seconds, and she said: "I cannot. There is nothing at the head of it to ask the question of. That is not a fault in the question. That is the whole of what the question is, and I have been asking it for two years at about nine counters and I have never once had a thing in front of me that I could not put it to, and I have just met one."

She picked her sheet up off the table and put it back in the satchel.

"I am not going to ask you who wrote it or when," she said to Mirren Fisk, "and you are not going to tell me, and I am going up the street to certify four sheets in a counting room and I am going to do it at the ordinary speed. Good morning."

And she went out, and she did not take the sack with her and she did not take anything else, and nobody in that room thanked her, and Marek Vale did not thank her, and there is no note of it anywhere.

---

When she had gone, Mirren Fisk put the piece of sack back in the drawer under the apron and shut the drawer.

"Right," she said. "Now you can tell me what the paper is for, and then I am going to say no to it, and then you are going to tell me that you expected me to say no, and then I am going to say something else, and we will have spent an afternoon."

Marek put the form on the table.

It is five ruled lines on a sheet of Chamber paper with no boxes and no head at the top of four of them and nothing at the top of the fifth, and it had been in the inside of a wet coat for weeks and it had been on a river and in a granary yard and in a wire basket, and it went on the table flat and it looked like nothing at all.

Mirren Fisk read the four heads and she read the fifth line last, and it took her about four seconds, and then she put her thumb on the fifth line and left it there.

"That is not a hole," she said.

"No."

"No, it is not, and I am glad, and I want you to understand that I have had a hole in a form in this basin in my head for two years and I have never been able to say so out loud without it sounding like a complaint, and a man I do not know cut it on purpose and left it bare and put a straight-edge on it, and he has never met me and he does not know what it is for and I am not going to tell him."

---

"What is it for," she said.

"Two women have a balance coming that is not going to come back in kind, and you are going to have to put it on their ground in the fourth month, and I thought a form was better than a piece of sack."

"No it is not," said Mirren Fisk. "And I am going to tell you the four reasons, and then you are going to be able to put the form in your coat and take it with you, and I would rather you took it than that you left it here.

"One. A form is a thing a man in a good coat can produce at a woman in nine months, and a piece of sack goes home in an apron, and I have been living on the difference for two years and I am not giving it up because a clerk in Bell Stair felt better about it.

"Two. My rule has never had a form and if it gets one it stops being mine. It is a rule we made in a room with a floor and it is nineteen years old and it is the only reason anybody in this low ground can stand being told a number by a woman they can walk to. The day it is on paper it belongs to a building, and I have seen what a building does with a thing in about four days and so have you.

"Three. I am not going to hand a woman a form with a rate on it before the rate exists, and the rate does not exist until the fourth month, and the rule does not go round the houses until it has been round once in a yard and said out loud in the room it is going to be said in.

"And four, and this is the one that is really the reason, and I have been not saying it since you put the paper down.

"I have eleven lines on a piece of sack and I am going to go to eleven houses in the fourth month and say a number out loud in a kitchen to a woman, and I do that as a person who has been in her kitchen. If I do it off a form then I am an office, and the moment I am an office she is being notified, and notified is a different word from told, and I have had two years to work out the difference and the difference is the only thing I have that is worth anything."

---

She sat down.

"You may leave it," she said. "And I am going to do something in about nine days that I have not told you I am going to do, and I am telling you now so that you can be in the room and can stop me if I am wrong, and I am not going to be wrong, and if I am wrong you are the wrong man for it and I would like you to say so at the time and not afterwards."

"I am not going to stop you."

"I know," said Mirren Fisk. "That is why I am telling you."

She got up and took the slate and the string and went down to the seed house, and Marek Vale sat in two rooms over a chandler's yard in Rainmarket with five ruled lines on a sheet of Chamber paper in front of him and a drawer that had just been shut, and worked out that he had been in this room for about two hours and had been given a form he could not use, a rule he could not improve, a woman he could not argue with, and an afternoon in nine days, and that he was going to take it.

And he worked out the other thing while he was sitting there, which was that a woman with a jar on a rail had told him in a gateway on the fourteenth of the harvest month that the telling lasts about eleven weeks, and that about a month of that was gone, and that he had been up that road once since and had not been back, and that he had not the least idea what a man says the second time, and that in about nine days he was going to watch somebody else try it.
