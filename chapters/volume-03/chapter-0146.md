# Chapter 146 — The Head of the Column

Emmerich Tace had come off the Crown March post on the fourteenth of the third month of MR 414 with a letter-case and a sealed sheet and had been in a lane in Tallow Ward at the seventh bell, and on the fourth of the third month of MR 415 he came off the same post with the same case and stood in the same lane for about a quarter of an hour before he came in, which he had not done the first time.

There were about seventy people in the lane. The register opened at the fourth bell in a shed eleven feet by nine with a germination bench against the wall on the days the rota said, and Teodor Lask was reading somebody's second line out loud with his back to a bench frame, twice, at the ordinary speed.

"You are a year late," said Sanne Droll.

"I am a year late on a fortnight," said Tace, "and I would like it understood that I did not come because I was told to come. I have been three times to that office with a request and twice I was told that the office could not ask a person to be told something, and once I was told that it could, and the once was a clerk of about thirty who said it on the way out of a room and would not put it in writing, and I took it as an answer and I came."

He put the letter-case on the step and did not open it.

"I have it," he said. "It has been in a drawer in a room in the hills since the sixteenth of the third month of last year and I have been told to bring it in person and not by post, and I have had it in a drawer for a year, and I would like to begin by saying that I have thought every day about that year and I have not once worked out whether I could have carried it sooner, and I have concluded that I could not, and that is the first honest thing I have said about it."

---

The sheet was one sheet, printed, with a head at the top of it in type, and the head said *persons in the upland holdings who will bear the settlement weight under the instrument of the second month of MR 412*, and under the head there was a hand-filled number, and under the number there was a ruled column about nine lines deep.

Tace put it on the second trestle flat, and did not put it on the second trestle under the form, and Sanne Droll looked at the place he had put it and then at him.

"Take it off the trestle," she said.

"I have put it on the edge," said Tace.

"That is the edge of the trestle. Take it off it. There is a yard's form under that board and I am not going to have a Crown March schedule lying on a yard's board, and if a man in a good coat comes in here in nine months he will be able to say that a ward's counter dealt in a settlement weight, and it will not be true, and it will be in a room."

He took it off the trestle and held it against his own chest and said:

"I have been told the number at the top of that sheet is the number the office expects and I have been told that if the sheet comes back short the office will send somebody to find out why, and I would like to know what I am supposed to say to that at a door in four miles and eleven switchbacks."

"You are not going to say anything at a door," said Marek Vale. "Nobody is going to say anything at a door. There is no form in this kingdom in which a person may be sent to ask a terrace why it has not sent anybody, and that has been the position for two years and it will be the position in the fourth month."

---

"I have written nine," said Tace.

He put the sheet on the bench frame at the back of the shed, which is not a trestle and is not a board, and squared it with his case, and turned it about ninety degrees so that it faced the room, and then he took a pencil out and ruled nine lines beside the printed column and stopped.

"Eight of them have names on them and one does not," he said, "and I am going to do the thing I have been not able to do since the sixteenth of the third month of last year, and I would like the room to be told that I know what I am doing before I do it."

He did not rule a head.

He ruled nine lines and left the space above them empty, and then he put the pencil down on the bench and squared it up against the edge and stood there.

"There is no head on it," he said.

"No," said Marek.

"Then it is not a schedule. A thing with nine names in it and nothing at the head of it cannot be read by anybody in my office, including me, and I have been carrying it for a year and I have taken it out about nine times and every one of those times I have wanted to put a head on it, and I have wanted to put a head on it because a head is the only thing that makes a piece of paper a schedule, and without a head it is a list somebody wrote, and a list somebody wrote is worth nothing in a room in the hills."

"Then it is worth nothing in a room in the hills," said Droll.

"Yes. And I am going to do it anyway, and I want to say the true reason and not the good one."

---

"The true reason is that I know what the four names at the bottom of my own file are," said Emmerich Tace. "And I wrote them. And the four of them are a woman who came into a shed in this lane with a cloth over her head and asked what a paper was; an examiner who is not a member of anything and holds a sheet with four figures on it; a man with no office who read out other people's papers for four hours a day; and a haulier on the low-ground road whose paper has a mark on it that nobody in this city can read.

"And I wrote those four in a room in the hills off the back of a file, out of an office that had compiled them, and I have had them in a drawer for a year, and in about four of those five minutes I have wondered whether any of those four people knew they were on it.

"And not one of them did. Not one. And I did not write that down either, and that is the second thing I have not written down, and I have been not writing it down for about a year and I have got rather good at it."

---

The register was still going at the back of the shed and Lask had started a line from the beginning and was on his second reading of it, and Tace looked at him and then at the nine lines.

"May I put four questions to that," he said. "They are not mine. They are a yard's and I have heard them at about nine counters in this lane in a year and I would like to ask them because I am the man who is carrying it and I have never been in a room with it."

"Ask them," said Droll.

"Is that your line." And Tace stopped, and stood there for about four seconds, and said: "No. That is not a fault in the question. There are nine names on it and about nine hundred and some people in those four terraces and I have been told the number of them is not on any form in the kingdom and I have believed that since the fourteenth of the third month of last year.

"And the other three want a person in the room and the four terraces are four miles and eleven switchbacks away and there is nobody in this shed who is behind any of it, and so I have asked one and I have got nothing and I am going to say the other three out loud at this counter because a line is read out twice at the ordinary speed and I would like a person who is not a clerk to hear them."

She said them. He wrote them in the margin of his own case with a date and no name, which is the only mark he made on anything.

---

"And now the part I have carried up the road for," said Emmerich Tace, "which is that the Chancellor's office sent me with a question and not with a list, and the question is this. Does a room of people stand behind that paper.

"That is all. Not names. Whether there is a room. Because if there is a room then she can send a person to the room and have the terms said to the people in it, and if there is not then she will do whatever she does next without knowing, which she does about forty times a season and does not enjoy. I have had that reason in my own mouth since the fourteenth of the third month of last year and I have not improved it and I am not going to."

He turned the sheet about ninety degrees again.

"I am going to tell you the answer and then I am going to tell you what the answer is worth, and the two of those are not the same thing, and it has taken me a year to be able to say both out loud in the same breath.

"The answer is no. There is no room. There are about four hundred and some people on four terraces who do not know each other, do not meet, and have never met in a building, and I have wanted one of them to be in a room for a year and there is not one, and I have not put that in writing and I am putting it in writing now because it is on my sheet.

"And the answer is worth nothing, and here is why, and I have got about four minutes to say it and I would like to be quick about it.

"A list of names is a list of people who can be sent to. That is the whole of what a Chancellor does with one. And the only way a name gets onto a list is that somebody else writes it down, and the yard opposite knows what happens when somebody else writes it down, because the yard is the only room in this basin where a person may enter a line about their own paper and nobody may enter a line about anybody else's. And the yard's counter has produced a list of nobody for a year, and it did not produce it out of courage. It produced it out of a rule, and the rule is four questions long, and about nine people have stood in that lane and been asked the fourth one, and the fourth one is *is there a person behind it who is in this room*, and the answer in this basin has been no every time.

"So the list the Chancellor cannot have is unobtainable for a reason that has nothing to do with anybody's courage, and the reason is that a list needs somebody to have asked, and the only place in this kingdom where anybody is asked anything is a counter, and a counter is a room and a room is a thing that can be produced at a household in nine months, and the moment it is produced it is a list of people who were asked and not a list of people who agreed."

---

Sanne Droll put her hand flat on the bench frame and said:

"You have a haulier on that sheet."

"I have four names and I have given you three of them."

"You have given me two of them and the man with no office and the woman with the cloth. You have not given me the fourth." She turned round all the way. "Say it."

"Petar Duss," said Tace. "Thirty-one years on the road, four children, a rope round his wrist, a paper with a mark on it that nobody in this city can read. He was entered in my office in the third month of last year out of a return that came in off a season's account and I have never met him and he does not know."

"And this yard refused his line."

"This yard refused his line."

"On the fourth question?"

"On the fourth question and on the third," said Droll, "because the mark is the only part of that sheet with a person in it and a person cannot swear to a mark, and I said that at the counter about nine weeks ago and I have not checked whether I am right and I have not asked anybody to check, and it stands.

"And I would like it noticed that the yard refuses the one name on that sheet that has a paper behind it, and that the yard's own counter has never entered a name of its own accord, and so a Chancellor is being sent a list of nine people out of which the only one anybody in this basin has ever been asked anything about is the one the yard would not have entered, and the eight the yard would have entered are the ones somebody in a room in the hills wrote down on their own."

---

Nobody thanked him and nobody said anything kind, and Droll went back to the counter, and Lask went back to the second line from the beginning.

There was one more thing in the case and Tace took it out at the last minute and put it face up on the bench frame, and it was a sheet of the same paper with five lines on it in the same hand and one sentence at the foot of it, and nobody read it out loud except him, and it said that the Chancellor accepted that no room stands behind the paper and directed that no person be sent, and that the schedule be returned unheaded, and that the request for a list of names in the upland holdings be closed.

"She wrote that in nine days," said Tace. "It took me a year to get it and she did it in nine days, and I have been carrying a sheet with nine names on it in a drawer in the hills for about four hundred days in a drawer in a room in the hills where a clerk of about thirty told me the office could ask a person to be told something on the way out of a door.

"And she has said the request is closed. Not answered. Closed. And I am not going to be thanked for bringing it and I would like it said that I am not going to be thanked, and I am going to go and post this at the desk in Bell Stair at the third bell and I am going to walk back down the hill, and I am not going to be able to tell anybody in that office what is in a shed eleven feet by nine, and I have not tried, and there was never a form on which I could have sent it."

He put the nine lines with nothing at the head of them back in the case with the printed schedule, which still has its head in type, and the number at the top of it which is the number the office expected.

Marek Vale stood in a shed and worked out, in about nine seconds, that there were four people in this basin he had known for a year who had been entered on a piece of paper in a room in the hills and had not been told, and that one of the four was on the low-ground road four miles away with a rope round his wrist and a sheet of paper folded in the left breast of a coat that was not his own, and that he had known all four of them, and that he had not walked four miles, and that the reason he had not walked four miles was that he had wanted to be able to prove it first.
