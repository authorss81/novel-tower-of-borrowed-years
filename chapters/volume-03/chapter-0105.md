# Chapter 105 — The Man Who Came Down the River

The barge came up the river on the morning tide on the nineteenth of the tenth month with a Crown March flag on it and a load of oats out of a depot at Verrow in it, and it tied at the second stair on the low-ground side, and a man of fifty-four came up the stair with a satchel and a coat that had been on a boat for two nights.

He stood at the top of the stair and looked down the river for about four seconds, and then he looked up at the roofs of Rainmarket, and Marek Vale, who was on the stair because he had been on it every day for a fortnight watching barges come up empty and go down full, understood that this man was counting.

He was counting the roofs. He put his hand up flat against the sky over the town and moved it slowly from left to right the way a man does who has bought a field.

"Four, five hundred roofs in this town and the same again in the yards behind it, and I have never had cause to count them and I have just counted them in my head, which tells you what kind of morning I am having."

"Your name is Rell," said Marek.

"It is. Sabin Rell, granary, the depot at Verrow, twenty-nine years." He came up the last four steps properly, which took two of them. "And I know who you are, and I am not going to pretend I do not, because you read out a woman's sentence off a wall in Tallow Ward in the ninth month and it went round this basin on the mouths of four hundred people, and about half of them told it wrong, which is the usual way, and the half that told it right has the words *a bundle is not one line* in it, and I have thought about that sentence for a month and I have come down a river to see whether it is any use."

"Will you find out?"

"I have found out in about four minutes," said Rell. "That is not a criticism. It is a description of a conversation, and there were nine of you in it, and eight of them agreed with you and one of them asked you a question, and I have been in this trade for twenty-nine years and I know the difference between a room that agrees and a room that thinks, and there is one question in yours. So I came down."

---

The boatman was a woman of about thirty called Sefa and her grandmother had worked the stair before her, and Rell went down onto the deck and put his hand into the oats to the wrist and brought up a handful and let it run through his fingers and did it twice.

"Clean," he said. "Dry. No smut in it. That is Verrow's floor and I have had the same floor out of the same silo every year since I was twenty-five."

"That is coming down," said Sefa. "That is going down to you, not off you. **Oats are not coming up this week. Nothing is coming up this week.** There were three boats a day on this stair in the second month and there is one today and it is going down, and if you have come to look at a load, that is a load going the wrong way for you."

"I have come to look at four loads a week going the right way for me," said Rell, "and I have come because in six days the granary board at Verrow has to certify an export out of this basin for a quarter, and I have to put a number on a piece of paper, and I have to be able to stand up in a room of eleven men and say the number is the number and not a hopeful figure, and I am going to be asked what the low ground is doing."

"And what is the low ground doing?"

"The depot's despatches are up by half on last year and the low ground's rate has fallen by about a third in nine days," said Rell, and he said it in the voice of a man delivering good news, and he was not being cruel and he was not stupid and he had a satchel with a thing in it that he was going to do in about two days that was going to cost four terraces everything they had.

Then he stopped, because he had heard himself, and he turned round.

"Sefa," he said. "Say the other half."

"The other half," said Sefa, "is that you have come up here to look at a load, and you have put your hand in my husband's oats, and the oats came up a road in about nine days, and the road was opened for a Crown March paper, and a man with a lantern is standing at the top of it and I have been past him four times this month and he waves about nine carts through and does not wave us, and I said that to him on the second and he said *you take them through on the Crown March paper and the Crown March paper goes through*, and I have got a rate from a clerk on the low ground and I have got a road from a man with a lantern and I have not got anything at all else, and you have got a floor out of a silo and I have got a river, and you are welcome to the river."

Rell looked at the deck planks for a while.

"Yes," he said. "That is fair, and I am not going to argue with it, and I would like to be careful about one thing, which is that what I have come to do and what you have just described are not the same matter, and I would like to keep them apart, because if I do not keep them apart somebody in about three weeks is going to tell a room that a Crown March granary official opened the road to steal a harvest, and it will not be true, and it will be true enough that nobody will be able to hear the rest of it."

He came back up the stair and sat down on the second step with the satchel across his knees.

"I did not open the road," he said. "I have never been to that road and I did not know it was shut and I would not have had the authority, because a road is not a granary matter. It was opened by a traffic office, and there is a Crown March traffic office in Verrow and a great many other offices and about a third of them are run by people who are good at opening things and the rest are run by people who are frightened of not opening a thing, and the two are in the same building, and I have been in that building for twenty-nine years and I have never once been able to find out which of the two I am sitting next to."

"You are going to tell me what is in the satchel," said Marek.

"Yes," said Rell. "Not because you have asked nicely. Because I would rather it was on the stair in the open than in a room with four walls and a table, and because I have a bad feeling about the number of men who are going to ask me about it in the next three days and I would like one of them to have it first from me, in the sun, in about four minutes."

---

He took it out.

It was one sheet. It was not thick. It had a heading and three clauses and a rule at the foot and a seal on it and a mark in the bottom corner, and the mark was the smallest thing Marek had ever seen on a piece of paper and he had seen one on a haulier's contract four months ago.

"It is an underwriting," said Sabin Rell. "Four words. A district is underwritten for a delivery. What that means in this basin, since you will want the plain version: the depot at Verrow has paid a sum of money early against a harvest that has not happened, to a house that has sold the paper on, and the paper says that when the grain comes the depot takes the grain and the money is a set-off, and the risk of the advance not being repaid out of the delivery has been carried by the four terraces on the upland road, because the four terraces are named on it and because they are the people who will grow the grain.

"I am not going to tell you the sum. **It is eleven thousand and some hundreds of pounds and I cannot give you the exact figure because the exact figure is in a ledger in Verrow and it is not mine, and if I gave it to you on a stair you would carry it down a road and put it in a book and about nine people would be ruined by a number that has been in a cellar since the flood year.**"

"And the clause is correct," said Marek.

"The clause is correct," said Rell. "It is the fourth clause of the standard granary form and it has been the fourth clause of the standard granary form for longer than I have been in this building, and there is nothing in it that is not a matter of public print, and I will put my name to it on the twenty-fourth, here, in this basin, in a room with a table in it, and the sealed original goes back to Verrow in a locked box with the ledger, and I am going to put my name to it because if I do not the board at Verrow has to put a *no* in a box and a no in a box is a man in Verrow losing his post, and he will not lose his post over me, and I will lose mine over him, and there are nine hundred people in this basin who eat out of that bin and I have never once in twenty-nine years pretended that my post is worth more than their dinner. So I am going to sign it."

He said all of that in the flat voice of a man reading out the terms of something and did not appear to have said anything remarkable at all.

"There is nothing wrong with that," said Marek.

"No," said Rell. "There is nothing wrong with that and I have been trying for six weeks to find what is wrong with it, and I am going to tell you what I have got, and I am telling you because you are the only person I have spoken to on this stair who has not asked me whether the four terraces are getting their money."

Marek did not say anything.

"The depot enters a district at the ten-year mean," said Sabin Rell. "That is the practice. Not this year's figure, not last year's, not a man's opinion of a field. The mean of ten years, because the mean is the only number in the book that cannot be argued with by anybody who has been in the district, and because a depot that enters a district at its own estimate of this year is a depot that is arguing with the district.

"And I want you to understand that I have said the sentence *the ten-year mean* out loud in a room in Verrow about four hundred times and I have never once had anybody ask me where it is.

"**It is not in the book I am carrying. It is not in any book in Morrowfen. It is a number in a table in a depot in the hills that I have read once and cannot produce and am not permitted to produce and would be asked to swear to, and the reason I am not permitted to produce it is that the ten-year mean of a district is the depot's estimate of a district, and the moment a depot can produce its own estimate then the estimate is an offer instead of a measurement, and an offer can be sued.**"

He put his hand flat on the satchel.

"So the four terraces are underwritten at a mean that nobody in this basin has ever seen, on a season in which they are, at this moment, nine lots of thirty-nine out of sand, and I have known about the nine lots since the fourth of the ninth month and I have had the form in the satchel since the sixth of the ninth month, and I have not signed it, and I am going to sign it on the twenty-fourth, and I would like you to be able to say afterwards that I told you both those things on the same morning and in that order."

Marek Vale sat on a river stair with his hands on his knees and found that he had two numbers and no sentence.

*Nine lots out of thirty-nine out of sand.* He had it. He had been up the road for it.

*Eleven thousand and some hundreds.* He had it. He had been in a cellar for it.

And the middle of the two — the thing that joined a cellar of two hundred and eleven sheets to nine trays of sand on a terrace four miles above a bridge — was a number in a table in the hills that a man of fifty-four was not permitted to produce, and there was no form in this kingdom in which anybody might be given it, and there was no office in this basin that held it, and the man who held it was sitting next to him on a stair and had told him about it voluntarily, in the sun, before anybody asked.

"Mr Rell," said Marek. "What is the ten-year mean of the four terraces."

"I have told you I cannot produce it," said Rell.

"You have told me you cannot produce it. I am asking whether you know it."

Sabin Rell looked out at the river for a while, and a barge went down with a mark on a piece of paper in a coil on the deck, and the man at the top of the stair did not look at it.

"Yes," he said. "I know it. Everybody in that depot knows it and nobody has ever said it outside the depot because it is not a figure that is anybody's business outside the depot, and that is the arrangement, and the arrangement is the reason that a form in a cellar in Bell Stair is a mystery to a man like you and a number to a man like me and **the same piece of paper, and that is the whole of what I have come down the river to say, and I have said it, and I would like to go and be somewhere else now.**"

He stood up and did not open the satchel.

"Two minutes," said Marek.

"Two minutes," said Rell, and sat back down, and was extremely good company for exactly two minutes, and asked Marek three questions about the register that no Crown March official had asked about the register in the month it had been open, and did not once say the word *Chamber* in an unkind way.

At the top of the stair, on the way down to the barge, he stopped.

"You will try to stop it," he said. "I want to be fair with you. I have been in this trade twenty-nine years and I know exactly what a man in a good coat is going to try to do with a document, and it is going to be that a mean is not a season and the clause is wrong, and the clause is not wrong, and you are going to lose that in a room, and I would rather you did not lose it in front of nine people who have come four miles to find out about a road.

"**Go and look for the room that signed the paper, not the room that is keeping the number.** The number will be in a book and there is no form in this kingdom that lets a man in a good coat out of a book. I have been doing this for twenty-nine years and I have never once known where the number is kept. I have known all along where the paper is."

He went down the stair and got onto the barge and the barge went down the river with a mark on a piece of paper in a coil, and the low ground's barley rate fell again before the tide turned, and there was no sea in that sentence at all.
