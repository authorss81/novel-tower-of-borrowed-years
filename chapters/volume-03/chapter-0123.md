# Chapter 123 — Nine Lots Come Up

The ninth of the growth month, the third bell, before light, on the second terrace, above a lane where a new drain had been cut in the third month and where a wall that had been holding up a terrace's worth of ground since the flood year was four feet shorter than it had been and standing in its own rubble.

Stana Peric's drill rows ran down the shoulder from the lane. There were nine of them and they had been sand since the seed month and the crust on them was the colour of a hearth and about a finger thick, and her mother's house was below them and her mother's house had a new door on it that had been hung in the fourth month, and the five children were asleep in it.

Marek Vale had been on the terrace since the second bell with a lamp he did not light and had been on that ground for about an hour, and he had done the thing he had come to do four times, and it had not worked four times, and the reason it had not worked four times was that he had been saying it to the field.

*This season, this lot, here.*

Nothing. The crust did not move and the sand under it did not move and about an hour of the small hours went by and a man who reads other people's papers out loud for four hours a day stood on a woman's drill rows in the dark and said a thing to about four inches of soil.

He said it to the house instead, on the seventh, in a yard, and it had not worked, and the difference between the two was a *there*.

---

What worked in the end, and it worked at about a quarter past the third bell when the light came, was this.

He stopped saying the season. He said the name, and he said it the way a man says a name in a room when he is asking somebody to look at a person and not at a coat, which is to say it once and then not say it again, and then he said the four words, and the four words were not *this season, this lot, here*.

They were: *Stana Peric, and the season you have got.*

And that is the whole of it and he did not know it was the whole of it for another four days, and he has never been able to give it in fewer words than that, and the four days were the worst of that year.

The season did not arrive. Nothing arrived. The light came up over the shoulder and the rows were exactly as they had been and he stood there for about half an hour in the growing light and did not know whether he had done a thing or nothing, and then he went down the lane to the cut and sat on the low wall where the drain had gone out and was glad in a way he was ashamed of for about a minute, and then stopped being glad.

---

He was still on the wall at about the fifth bell when Tomas Vale came up the lane from the culvert with a coil of rope over his shoulder and looked at him and did not ask.

It is the whole of what Tomas Vale did on the ninth of the growth month at about the fifth bell: he came up a lane, saw his brother sitting on a wall in the light, did not ask, and sat down on the other end of it about four feet away with the rope between them.

"You have been up since the second bell," he said.

"I have."

"Because there is nothing at the top of this lane worth being at for an hour before the light, and a man who is up here at the third bell is a man who has come to do a thing on somebody's ground at a time when the family is asleep in it."

Marek did not say anything.

"I am not going to ask," said Tomas Vale. "I have told you what I want in a kitchen and I have got it once and I am not asking for it again. Say one thing and then we will talk about the culvert, and the one thing is whether anybody's name is on it."

"No."

"Whose words are in it then."

"A woman's," said Marek. "On the second terrace. She is a member of nothing and does not want to be a member of anything and she has been to the doors of about nine houses since the seed month and she has a school exercise book and she has not given her name to anybody for a year and she gave it to me in her own yard in front of nine people, and she did it knowing that the woman would find out from her ground and not from a person."

Tomas Vale sat on a wall above a stone bridge in the light with a coil of rope between them and looked at the nine drill rows on the shoulder and did not say anything for about a minute.

"Her name," he said.

"Her name is not yours and it is not mine and I am not going to give it to you on a wall."

"I did not ask for it," said Tomas Vale. "I asked whether anybody's name was on it and you said no and then you told me whose words are in it, and that is twice in one answer and you did not hear yourself do it. That is the fourth time this year and it is the first one that cost somebody else something instead of you."

---

They talked about the culvert for about forty minutes and the drain for about twenty, and both of those were the work, and neither of them was a favour.

The culvert on the fourth line is four miles down the road and its inlet is four feet below its invert and the invert is the floor, and the floor is the wrong place, and Tomas had pegged the road open in the growth month so that nothing would go over the top of it before the winter, and there were eleven stakes in four miles of road and a rope between them at about the height of a man's knee, and Marek carried rope for two days and was not thanked and did not expect to be and was told by a man with a bar exactly where to put his feet.

At about the seventh bell Tomas said the thing he had come up the road to say, and he said it while walking, without stopping, which is how he said everything that mattered.

"The drain is going to be right," he said. "The wall is not going to be right. I can tell you now and I can tell you in the harvest month and it will be the same both times, and I have not told the terrace and I am not going to, because there are four people in this valley who are going to lose that wall whatever anybody does and two of them are on the third terrace and one of them is old and one of them is not coming back. So that is a thing I am carrying, and I am telling you I am carrying it, and I am telling you in a lane and not in a room, and if you write it down I will find out and I will not be angry, I will just know."

"I am not going to write it down."

"I know," said Tomas Vale. "That is not why I told you."

---

Marek Vale got back down eleven switchbacks on the ninth of the growth month and reached his own room above the lockkeeper's shop in Tallow Ward at about the eleventh bell, having eaten nothing since a heel of bread at the second bell, and got as far as taking his coat off.

Then he put a hand on the rail of his own bed to steady himself, and the hand was on a thing he had touched about four thousand times, and he stood there and waited for the thing he was about to do, which was to put his coat over the chair.

The coat was not on the chair.

He knew the coat. He knew the chair. He knew that the coat went over the back of the chair and not on it, and that the hook was by the door and that the hook was for a hat he did not own, and he stood with his hand on the rail and could not for about nine seconds produce the fact of his own coat on a chair, and it was not gone. It was on the back of the chair. It had been on the back of the chair the whole time.

He hung it up.

---

Then he sat on the edge of the bed in a room above a lockkeeper's shop and worked out, methodically, the way he worked everything out, what had been taken and what had not.

Nobody had got a year older. Nobody in four terraces had lost a hand's worth of anything. There was no wound on him and no mark and nothing to show anybody, and there was a small book on a shelf in the room with the things he had promised his mother in it, and every one of them was still a thing he had promised.

What had gone was a season of *knowing a thing*. He could not produce his own habits without first arriving at them. He got up on the tenth of the growth month and reached for the boiler and did not know why the boiler mattered, and then knew, four seconds later, that it mattered because of the frost and because a man in this building had been up since the fourth. He did the same with the door, and the same with the ledger on the shelf, and again with the tap.

It was not a hole. That was the part he kept coming back to and could not get past. **It was not a hole.** If it had been a hole he would have known what it was, because he had seen holes his whole professional life and he knew the shape of the ones a year makes. This was the year, intact and complete, with a thing gone out of the middle of it that was not a fact but a *use*: a day came in and he did what he always did and he had to look up what he always did.

The cost of a season, it turned out, was not a season of somebody's life.

It was a season of a man's *hand* — his own, and the fact that it was his own was the last good thing about it, and he understood about eight days later, in a ditch on the low ground, what the other kind of that costs.

---

He got up at the fourth bell on the tenth of the growth month and read somebody's second line out loud twice at the ordinary speed in a shed eleven feet by nine in Tallow Ward with about eighty people in the lane, and the fifth line of the form was empty, and the sixth line was empty and always would be, and a column on the wall list had nothing at the head of it, and he got through the morning without once losing the thread, because Teodor Lask read the fourth line that day and not him.

He thought about the ninth of the month four times in three days and did not write it down, and there is a note in his own hand on the back of his left hand, in pencil, that says a count of sheets and four days of a rate that has stood, and under that, added on the tenth of the growth month in the same pencil, four words, which are not a document and are not for anybody:

*season, third bell, four days.*

At the fourth bell of the eleventh he got out of his bed and got as far as the door and found he knew the door.

Four miles above a stone bridge, on the shoulder above a lane, nine drill rows of a second-terrace holding that had been sand since the seed month did something that nothing on that terrace had done in nine years, which was to take four days and then come up, and about nine people watched it come up over a fortnight and nobody said one word about it, because a crop coming up is not a remarkable thing, and the only man in this basin who found it remarkable was four miles down a road with a lamp he had not lit, and he had told nobody, and there was no form on which he could have told anybody, and by the fourth of the harvest month those nine rows would be the reason about nine people in this valley believed that a season can be pointed at a household, and that belief would be a lie told by a field, and the field would be the only witness and it does not answer.
