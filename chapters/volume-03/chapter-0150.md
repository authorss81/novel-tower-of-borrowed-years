# Chapter 150 — A Paper in a Coil

The barge came off the low-ground reach at about the second bell on the eleventh of the fifth month of MR 415 with a full bed of barley on it and a coil of paper on the boards by the fore hatch, and the paper was about the size of a sheet of Chamber paper and it was tied with a piece of string in a coil the size of a man's fist, and it was going to Saltglass and it was not coming back.

The man who put it there was Orren Vaas, forty-one, a factor in Rainmarket, and he was standing on the tailboards with a slate under his arm and no coat, and he had eleven hundred feet of nothing to say.

"I am not going to be asked about it," he said, "and I am going to tell you about it anyway, and I would like it understood that I have been about two days working out that I was not going to be asked, and that I have been wrong about that for about a year and a bit, and that this morning I have had it the other way round for the first time."

---

"You have been in this basin about nine months," he said, "and you have been to about nine offices and you have asked me about four things and I have answered about two of them, and I am going to answer the other two now, in this order, and then I am going to get off this barge.

"The first is the paper. I bought a copy of a schedule of four holdings in the third month of last year from a man in a good coat who did not tell me what it was and did not charge me much, and I paid a good price for it twice, once in the second month of this year, and the second buyer is a house in Saltglass and it is not a house I have ever been inside and I have never been given a name for a man in it.

"And what they have bought is a security. They have bought a schedule of four terraces in a year that has been paid twice, in a basin where a season was granted to those four terraces in writing in MR 412 by people who knew what they were doing, and they wanted a thing that was worth something in the event of a short delivery, and there is a great deal of that about and none of it came from a criminal and I have said so to two clerks this month who did not want to hear it.

"And the clause I put in the second sale says that the paper stands or falls with the mark upon it, because that is the clause, because every schedule of four upland holdings in this region has the same clause on it and it is the fourth clause of a standard form and it is a matter of public print."

---

Marek Vale had come down to the river stair on foot and had been standing on the second step for about a minute.

"Read me the mark," he said.

Vaas took a folded paper out of his coat and unfolded it and gave it over, and it was a printed sheet with a column of figures on it and a hand-filled number and a mark in the bottom corner in four small parts.

There is no office in this kingdom in which anybody can read it, and that is not because it is difficult. It is a month, and a tide phase, and a harbour seal in wax, and a translator's name, and three of the four are objects you could put in a hand and one is a movement, and a mark that names a day can be checked against that day by anybody with a calendar, and a mark that names a month can only be checked against a day by somebody with a tide table, and there is one in this basin and it is in two rooms above a chandler's shop and it belongs to a man who will not swear to a hand and who has said out loud in his own rooms that he does not know what the marks are and has not known for nine years.

"I have never been able to name the month in it," said Orren Vaas. "I have had it in a drawer since the third month of last year and I have looked at it about nine times and I cannot name the month and I have never met anybody who can, and I have concluded that the month is not the point of it, and I am not going to find out before this barge is at the mouth."

"You are not going to name it, and you are shipping it," said Marek.

"I am shipping it. And I would like you to understand the shape of what I am saying, because I have thought about it for two days and I have not got a better way round it. There is no form in this kingdom on which a man may be served with *stop that*. There is no office in this kingdom to which a person may take a piece of paper with a mark on it and say *I cannot read this and neither can you and therefore it is not worth anything*. I have looked. I have been looking since the fourth month and I have asked two clerks in the hills and one of them said the word *registry* and the other said no, and the one who said no was right and he is paid to be.

"And a mark is not a signature. I am aware of that and I have been aware of it since the fourth month, and a man in two rooms above a chandler's shop told me in a room with a tide table on the table, out loud, in the growth month of last year, and I did not take a note of it, and I have turned it over about nine times since. A signature says the paper is good. A mark says where a paper can go. And I am about to send a piece of paper to a coast where four men can read it, and every one of those four men will be able to say where it came from, and not one of them will be able to say whether it is any good, and neither can I, and that is the trade and I have been in it for nineteen years."

---

The barge was loaded and the pole was up and the rope was coiled, and Marek Vale stood on the second step of a river stair in a city that feeds three regions and had been the best fed in nine years and is now going to be short in about two years, and he worked out four separate things in about nine seconds and could do one single thing with none of them.

He could not stop the barge. There is no form on which a man may be served with stopping a barge.

He could not report the mark. There is no office in this kingdom that will read one, and the only tide table in this basin belongs to a man in two rooms who has said out loud that he does not know what the marks are, and who would be asked to say whether a particular hand did a particular thing on a particular day in a cellar four miles from a river, which is a question he has answered once and will not answer again.

He could not find out who had given the schedule to the man in a good coat in the second month of MR 414. Nobody in this basin knows, and a name is the only thing that can carry a question like that into a room, and the only name he had was a haulier on a low-ground road, and the haulier had not given it to him and would not.

And he could not do the other thing, which was the thing he had been doing for about a year, which was to get it into a shed and put it in a drawer where a building would find it in nine years and a man in a good coat would produce it at a yard. He had done that once already, with a yard's own method, in a room in Bell Stair, on his own asking, and it had cost a yard a thing it had made in the rain at a tank, and there is no form in this kingdom in which a man may take a sentence back out of a room, and he would not do it a second time even for this, and about nine days ago he had stood in a room in Bell Stair and said exactly that out loud and had it written down in a finding and had not been thanked for it.

---

He went up the hill to Bell Stair at about the fourth bell and put a pound and a little over on the counter at the posting desk and asked for a stamped sheet.

The first one had gone on the seventh of the second month of MR 414 and the answer was due in the third month of that year and has not come. It is paid for and sealed and on a shelf above a chandler's shop and it is a door and it is not a rescue, and it may never be a rescue, and there is no version of it in which a letter comes back and explains anything.

He wrote nine lines and sealed it and gave it to the man at the counter, and it asked the same question, in the same words, and the question was whether the translator's name in that mark is on the harbour's list, and the answer can be *yes* or *no* or *the name is not on the list*, and it will come in about six weeks if it comes at all.

"You have sent one of these before," said the man at the counter.

"Last year."

"Has it come back."

"No."

"Then you are sending a second one to a place that did not answer the first," said the man at the counter, "and I have taken the money and I am not going to say anything about it, and I would like it noted that I noticed, because I am the man who will be asked in about a year whether I noticed, and I would rather be able to say that I did."

---

He came down through Tallow Ward at about the sixth bell on his way to the yard, and the shed was shut and the notice was on it, and there is a wall list inside it with about forty lines on it and a column at the right-hand end of the fourth that has nothing at the head of it, and it has had nothing at the head of it since the second week of the seed month of MR 414, and nineteen hands declined to name it in a fortnight and about four hundred yards of a lane voted in four minutes not to fill it and not to rule it out.

Below that list, on a yard's form cut in a copy room in Bell Stair by a man with a carpenter's pencil and a straight-edge and a piece of window glass worn round at one end, the fifth line is the hours and it is empty, and the yard has refused to fill it twice on its own rule about a round that repeats. The sixth line is the days and it is empty and always will be, because there is no box in this kingdom for the days.

Nobody has named the column. Nobody has named the fifth line. The flat board with four ruled sheets is under the trestle and has not been out, and the certified true duplicate of the seasonal form is in a flat case under the same trestle and has not been out either, and the notice in Sedge's hand on the wall says what this room is and does not say a Crown March office, and the room is a practice and is not a remedy and no office in this kingdom has ever been told that it is either.

Marek Vale stood in the lane outside a shut shed in Tallow Ward for about a minute and looked at a wall through a doorway he had not opened, and there was nothing in the lane.

---

He was on the low-ground road at the first bell the next morning with a bag on his shoulder and nothing in it, and he was going up eleven switchbacks, and there was no form on which anybody could have been told he was going and nobody asked him to and nobody would ask him whether he had been.

He had been up that road twice since the fourteenth of the harvest month and had not been back. He had a sentence ready and it had been ready for about a month and he had not used it, and he had worked out in the growth month and again on a night in the third month, walking, that he did not have the least idea what a man says the second time, and that the second one would sound like a receipt for the first one, and that there is no form in this kingdom on which a person may be told that the eleven weeks have run out.

The eleven weeks ran out somewhere in the winter. Nobody in this basin can put a day to it. A woman of thirty-eight on the first terrace said in a gateway that she would know by the season when it had stopped, and she has a spring that came up in the same field every year of her life and a pear her father planted that is forty years old and a heifer that has not come back in heat, and she is not a member of anything, and she has not been asked anything since the fourteenth of the harvest month, and there is nobody who is going to go and find out.

So there is no second time. That is what he worked out at about the fourth turn of the switchbacks, at about the second bell, standing still on a road with a bag on his shoulder, and then he went on up it, because the road is there and the sowing was in on the ninth and the tenth and the district's rate came on on the second of the month and there is a man on the low-ground road with a rate and a straw cart and a piece of paper folded in four in a coat that is not his own, and there is a woman of thirty-eight who will be the woman with the jar for another two months, and there is nobody in this basin who is going to say one word about any of it to her in a gateway before the next harvest unless a man who has nothing in his bag goes up the road and stands in it.

A barge went down the river in the morning with a full bed of barley on it and a coil of paper by the fore hatch, and a mark on the paper in a month nobody on the bank could name.
