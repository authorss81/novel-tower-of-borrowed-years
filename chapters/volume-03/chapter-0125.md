# Chapter 125 — The Silence in the Shed

The rota on the shed wall in Tallow Ward is a sheet of oiled board with days on it and nothing at the head of it, and the fifteenth of the growth month was a day with Marek Vale's name against it, and the name is his because a yard said so out loud at a tank in the harvest month of MR 413 and refused to put it in ink, and nobody can take the four hours or the road or the words off him, and there is no paper of it anywhere.

The register opened at the fourth bell. Eleven feet by nine. A germination bench against the wall on the days the rota said and a trestle under the form and a levy box on the second trestle and a wall list with about forty lines on it and a column at the right-hand end of the fourth column that has nothing at the head of it and has not had one since the second week.

About eighty people were in the lane. About twenty were in the shed, which holds twenty-two. Sanne Droll was on the step and Bessok Tan was at the counter with the chalk, and Aldis Kern was not there and had not stood at that counter since the ninth month for reasons nobody in Tallow Ward had ever asked him for.

He read the second line out first, and then the third, and then he came to the one that was hers, and stopped for about four seconds, which he had never done.

Kade's entry is on that wall. It is the only line on it that is anybody's in the first person, and it is in her own hand, and it has her name at the foot of it and the day she put it up, and there is not one person outside that lane in this basin who has read it.

It says that the pledge was accepted as a class and not as an individual, and that the clause beneath it reads *and the seed-keepers of this village after us bear the keeping*, and that a class is not a person and she has known that for two years. That the witnesses were real and were not paid by the cooperative and she checked them twice. That the paper is well made and the mechanism is lawful and every figure on it balances and nobody has to be punished for anything. That she saw what it was going to do, and that it was going to leave one nineteen-year-old holding four terraces of seed drawers in ten years, and that she saw it at the time and wrote a sentence about it and went on certifying.

And then the last two lines: that she has had two years to write that sentence in a room and did not, and that she is not asking anybody for anything.

---

He read it once, at the ordinary speed, with his back to the bench frame the way the reader stands, and about twenty people in the shed and about eighty in a lane heard a sentence that had been written by a woman in a room on a wall in the same city.

And nobody said anything at all, which was correct, because the rule is that a line is read twice at the ordinary speed and the room does not talk over a line.

Then Bessok Tan said, from the counter, in the voice she uses for the whole of Tallow Ward: "Second reading when you are ready. The queue is not going anywhere and neither are you."

Marek turned round and picked up the chalk and put it down again without writing anything, and read it a second time.

---

The silence after the second reading is a different silence and that is the whole of the chapter and nobody in that shed ever found out why he was the one who noticed.

The first silence was the silence of a room that has never heard a thing. About four hundred people in this basin have not heard it and they hear it now and it goes past them like weather, and they are right not to have an opinion, and the yard is doing its job.

The second silence was the silence of a room that has heard the same sentence on almost every morning since the register opened, in the same voice, and has stopped finding it remote.

Nine or ten of the people in that shed are on the fourth column of a Crown March form.

Marek knew which nine. He had read the form, or a column of it, and he had stood in a yard in the third month and heard about nine people say that a class cannot be traced to a door, and he had watched a woman's own line be refused at this counter in the ordinary course because the person behind the paper was not in the room. He knew that the fourth column of that form said *the seed-keepers of the four terraces after this season*, in nine words, in type, on a printed sheet, and that there were four hundred and some people in those four terraces and about nine on any paper, and that the only piece of paper in this basin on which a counterweight has a number on it is the Hartlow roll in a loft, and the number is two.

Two people on a roll of six women, one of them nineteen.

He had known that since the tenth month of last year and he had checked it twice and he had wished, in the plainest terms he is capable of wishing anything, that it were two for all four terraces.

---

A woman near the back, who was about fifty and who had a coil of rope over her arm and who was in the shed for the fourth week running, said:

"That is the examiner's."

"It is," said Marek.

And then she said the thing that broke the silence, and she said it as a practical question, and it is the reason the register works:

"Where is she, then."

It was the questions at that counter every morning, in a different order. *Is that your line. Is that the person behind it. Is it your words.* And a fourth form of it was the one nobody had ever heard asked: *where is the person who wrote it.*

"She is on the low ground," said Marek Vale, "with a satchel of returns, and she is not in this room, and there is no form in this kingdom on which anybody may be applied for, and there is no form on which I could send a message up a road to ask her to come down and stand here while a man with no office reads her own sentence out loud again in a room full of strangers."

"So she cannot hear it."

"She heard it the first time," he said. "She was in this room the first time and she asked for it to be read."

The woman with the rope thought about that for about two seconds and then said, "Then that is a thing I did not know and I am not going to have an opinion about," and went out into the lane, and about nine other people did the same thing over the next four minutes, in ones and twos, and the shed got to about eleven, and Bessok Tan put the chalk on the counter and said the reading was done and would anybody like the bench.

---

Then Marek Vale did the thing that made the week.

He put his hand flat on the form under the trestle, the one with six ruled lines and no boxes and a tank and a date at the head of it, and he did not read it and he did not go into anything, and there was nothing in it for him, and he put his hand on it because it was the only piece of paper in that shed he had ever been allowed to touch in a way that meant something.

The fifth line was empty.

The fifth line is the hours. It is empty because the hours are nobody's paper. A yard proposed filling it with a hundred and twenty-two trays a day, which is sixty-one twice, which is a person's hours and is the only hours anybody in this basin had ever said out loud, and the yard refused it, twice, both times on its own rule, and the rule is that a round that repeats is the third box on the settlement desk's form, and the reason given was that a figure which appears on a board the same way twice turns the board into a roll.

The sixth line is empty and always will be.

Marek stood in a shed eleven feet by nine with his hand on a form and understood the entire shape of what he had done in the growth month, and it took about nine seconds, and nobody saw him do it, and this is what it was.

He had spent a season. He had spent it out of his own hands, in his own year, and it had cost him nothing that a person could be shown. There was no mark. There was no entry. There was no form in this kingdom in which a season taken out of a clerk with no office could be served on anybody or sworn to or produced, and no office in this city that would have taken it, and no register in this basin that would have had it, because the register's first rule is that a person may enter a line about their own paper and nobody may enter a line about anybody else's, and what he had done was neither, and did not belong to either half of that sentence.

And what he had bought with it was a *household.*

A household is a person. That is the whole difference and it is the whole of what he has done this month, and it is the reason the fourth column of a Crown March form is the size of the hole it is: **because Marek Vale has gone out of a room in Tallow Ward with no office and no form and has turned a class of four hundred and some people into one woman with five children and a store with a lock, and a man with a season can do that, and nothing in this kingdom can.**

And the fifth line on the form under his hand was empty, and the hours in it would have been hers, and the yard had refused to put them there on the yard's own rule, and the yard was right.

---

At the fourth bell the next morning a boy of nineteen read somebody's second line out loud twice at the ordinary speed with a bench against the wall on the days the rota said, and the fifth line of the form was empty, and the sixth line was empty and always would be, and a column on the wall list had nothing at the head of it, and the room did that whether or not anybody heard a sentence in it.

Four miles and eleven switchbacks above that lane, a season of the basin's was sitting in nine drill rows on a shoulder above a drain, and a woman of thirty-one with a store with a lock had her hand flat on its door and did not know why, and a man with no office had not gone up the road, and had not gone up the road for the first time since the seventh of the month, and there is no form in this kingdom in which anybody may be applied for.
