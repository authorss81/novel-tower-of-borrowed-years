# Chapter 111 — A Tide Table and a Lease

The road down off the Red Uplands has eleven switchbacks in it and Marek Vale came down all of them on the seventh of the second month with his coat buttoned wrong and two numbers on the back of his left hand in pencil, and he had been at the top terrace for one day and a night and had slept four hours on a bench over a seed room with his boots on.

He came down for one thing and had done the arithmetic before he was a mile off the top. There is one tide table in this basin. It belongs to a man in two rooms above a chandler's shop on the low-ground road. A day of road is a day of daylight, and he had spent the daylight on a bench.

His brother was still up there. Tomas had gone out at the fifth bell to look at a culvert four miles below the stone bridge and had said, without turning round, that he would be at the threshing floor until the sixth, and Marek had said he would be down by the fourth bell tomorrow, and neither of them had said anything else about it, and Marek had walked eleven switchbacks in the other direction with a reason in his coat and had not looked at it once.

---

Halvard Sunn opened the door of the front room in his shirtsleeves and looked at the wet sleeve and then at the stair behind him, which belonged to a man who had been complaining about a foreigner on his stairs for about a year.

"You have come down the road," he said. "I have not seen you in this room since the twenty-first of the tenth month and I would like it noticed that I have not mentioned it once. Sit down. Do not take your coat off."

He put a lamp on the table and then, while he was doing it, he put the almanac on the table, and the brass matrix, and the wax, and the pen, in the same places, in the same order.

"I am going to do the thing you came for and I am going to do it without ceremony, because the last time you sat in that chair we spent an hour on the theory of it, and I have thought since that the objects were the wrong half."

---

What he put on the table was not a mark. It was the almanac open at a page with two colours in the tide columns, and his finger going down one of them, and Marek's finger put in beside it, and the two fingers going down together from a month at the top of the page to a line about two thirds of the way down.

"That is the tide at the harbour on the day it names, and on the day after, and on the day after that, and the water stands the same way on all three, because there are three days in most months where it does." He shut the book about halfway. "Tell me which of those three is the seventh day of the second month in your calendar."

Marek looked at the two fingers on the page.

"I cannot tell you."

"You cannot tell me and I cannot tell you and there is nothing in this room that can tell you, and it is not a fault of the print. The book is right. Our year does not start where yours starts, so the whole of my calendar slides against yours about eleven days every month of your year and comes back round." He took his hand off the page. "I have said that to you before and you have written none of it down, which I notice. Now tell me what you want, and if you want me to swear to a hand I am going to say no, and we are both going to be where we were on the twenty-first of the tenth month, and you will have spent a day of road."

---

"I want to know whether you keep a copy of the marks you make."

"Depends what you mean, and you are going to be annoyed with the answer, so I will give you the whole of it in the order I would defend it in a room."

He went through the second door and came back with a book two inches thick in a board cover and put it on the table with the spine away from Marek, which was not a thing a man does by accident.

"Every mark I have made in this room in four years is on a leaf in that. I take an impression of it in wax when I make it, and I write beside it the instruction I made it from, in my own hand. You may look at it. You may not have it. It does not leave this room, and neither does anything you write down about it, and the reason is not me being careful. It is nineteen months of a lease.

"Three clauses. The instruments of a notary's trade — the press, the matrices, the wax, the books, the almanacs — are the tenant's, and the landlord may not open them or sell them or have anybody look at them. The tenant shall not keep his rooms open to persons of the trade of other harbours without producing his licence, which is a clause about me and about four men in this kingdom. Eleven months' notice. The lease is nineteen months old and renews in the ninth month, and the man downstairs has asked me twice to be allowed to look at the books, and I have said no twice."

---

Marek read it for about nine minutes and did not take his coat off.

He was not looking for a hand. That was the thing he had told himself in eleven switchbacks and it was wrong, and it took him until a leaf about halfway down the book to work out why.

There was no order in it.

He had spent ten years in a copy room and four in a Tally Hall before that, and every book he had ever read in had a spine of some kind. A spine is an argument about the future: this before that, this beside that, this because of that. The pattern book was in order by month. Four sheets to a month, in the order of Sunn's own calendar, and beside each impression the instruction's date in a hand, and about four in any month carried no day at all because the instruction had carried none, and the day the wax went on and the day the mark named were not the same kind of thing and the book had no way to say which was which.

"You cannot sort it by the day the paper was signed."

"I have never tried. The paper is not mine and its date is not mine. The day I put wax on it is mine and it is in the book, and in nineteen years I have never had a reason to want the other one."

"I have been looking for the wrong thing since the fourth of the tenth month," said Marek.

"You have been looking for a hand. A hand is on every one of those leaves." He turned one over with a fingernail. "And here is the only thing I have, and I have been sitting on it since the twenty-first of the tenth month with nobody to say it to who wanted to hear it.

"A mark is not made out of my judgement. It is made out of an instruction. A man in a house writes a sheet, and the sheet says put a mark on the papers going out this month, and this is the month and this is the tide phase and this is the harbour and this is my name, because I am the translator on that coast this month. I do the four parts in about ninety seconds. I do nine hundred of them a year. I do not read the papers and I am not permitted to read the papers.

"And the instruction is not in that book. It is a sheet in a house nine days up this coast in a room I have never been in, and the house is not a public office and is not obliged to keep the sheet, and it does not, because a sheet that says put this on that, on a date, is a thing you do not keep once the thing is done. I asked a man I have drunk with, in a house, in the ninth month, what happens to the instructions. He said they are kept until the tide and then they are burned, because a house that keeps a year's instructions has a year's worth of dates and figures in it, and that is a paper nobody wants in a house."

He shut the book.

"That is the whole of what I have. It is not a crime and there is nobody in it to be had, and it took me nine years to notice, and I have told nobody in this kingdom."

---

Marek sat in a cold room with a pound in his coat and understood that he had met the first thing in this kingdom that is produced without a record of who produced it.

Every office he had worked in kept everything and complained about it. The posts, the copies, the returns, the dead sheets, the trays that came back from a season office, the run number on a corner of a printed form in a cellar. A thing in this city was hard to get rid of and easy to trace, and it was the same difficulty doing both.

This was not that. It was a man doing about nine hundred pieces of work a year out of a sheet that does not exist any more, on the strength of a list that is a list of notaries and not of marks. And the hand on a piece of paper in a cellar four miles from a river, if anybody ever asked about it, would be answered by everybody in the trade, including the man whose wax it was, with the same sentence: a mark is a mark, and a mark is not a person, and a person is not responsible for a piece of furniture.

"A standing authority that certifies a projection has nothing to certify except a number," he said. "And the head of that column is in type and the figure under it is a hand, and there is nowhere on the sheet for the hand to say how it got the figure."

"Yes," said Halvard Sunn. "I do not know what a projection is. Say the other half of it."

"The other half is that the hand does not have to be anybody," said Marek. "A number can be carried forward from one season to the next in a hand that is nobody's. And a mark can be carried from one coast to another in a hand that is nobody's. And there is not one office in this kingdom where either of those is entered, because neither of them is a fact about a person."

Sunn got up and moved the wax to the drawer and put the pen beside it, which is the housekeeping Marek had watched him do on the twenty-first of the tenth month, and which meant the conversation was over.

"Write to the harbour," he said. "Write it here. Not because of anything. Because you will write it better at this table than you will in a room in Tallow Ward at the fourth bell with about eighty people behind you, and because I am going to correct one word in it."

---

It took forty minutes and it cost him the pound, and the only word in it that was changed afterwards was *obtained*.

*I am not a notary and I have no office. I pay for this out of my own money and I am told it will take about six weeks and may be answered in one of three ways. I want to know whether the translator whose name stands fourth in a Tide Reckoning mark on a sheet that came down a river to Morrowfen in a box on the fifth of the second month is on your list.*

*I cannot describe the mark and I am not going to try, and I will say why, because a man in your trade will know it in a moment and I would rather he knew I understood it than thought I was a fool. There are four parts. A month and a tide phase, which are printed. A harbour seal, which is struck in wax and is a thing. And a name, which is a person putting a pen into wax. The first three I can see and the fourth I can only see as writing. I have seen the fourth part on nine papers since the fourth of the tenth month and I cannot tell whether it is the same writing, and I am not going to say in this letter that it is or is not.*

*I am told the first three parts are objects and the fourth is a movement, and that a mark which names a month rather than a day cannot be checked against a day without a tide table. I have looked at the tide table in two rooms above a chandler's shop and I believe him. If the name on your list is a name I may be told, that is a thing I can hold. If the answer is that the name is not on the list, that is also a thing I can hold. If the answer is that the harbour keeps a list of notaries and not a list of marks, I would be obliged for that as well, and I would rather have it in six weeks than guess at it tonight.*

He read it twice and signed it and did not date it, because he did not know the coast's date, and the one thing he had learned since the year turned was that a date in the wrong calendar is where all of this begins.

Sunn put the envelope on a shelf by the door with two other letters on it. The coastal post went out on the tide and the tide was on the ninth of the month, and a letter put on that shelf on the seventh of the second month would be in somebody's hand in about six weeks, and it might be answered in one of three ways, and one of the three was *the name is not on the list*, which has the shape of a no and is not one.

---

He went down the stairs at about the fifth bell and the man underneath the chandler's shop was in his doorway with a ledger under his arm, and said good evening and shut his door.

At the end of the road a boy of about nine stood with a cart and somebody else's list, and he had come up the lane from the low ground.

"A man came off a barge at the second bell with a locked box and eleven sets of a paper," he said. "He's going to the Crown March rooms over the shop on the ninth. He's to be there at the first bell."

"His name."

"Lenge. He's a clerk. He's thirty-three." The boy shifted the cart. "He asked for a desk and a lamp. He wasn't expecting anybody."

"Right," said Marek Vale. "Thank you."

He had about a day and a half before a desk and a lamp and a locked box arrived in a room in this city, and he was going to be in that room, and he had a pound less than he had had at the fourth bell and six weeks of nothing in front of him.

He went up the north lane to the room above the lockkeeper's shop and put the two hundred and eleven where it was and did not wash the four days off, because a rate that has stood is a fact, and a man who washes facts off his hand at the fourth bell is a man who wants a better one by the sixth.
