# Chapter 112 — The Head of the Column

Petrus Lenge had the desk by the window and the ledger open on it and the locked box on the floor at his feet, and he had got the room over the chandler's shop for three days on a slip — meaning that the depot had paid for it and had sent the slip up with him, and the slip named the room and the three days and the day of the month it was to be given up, and there was nothing on the slip about who might sit in it.

He was thirty-three. He had been six years at the depot at Verrow and four of them on the projection column, and he had a lamp, a rule, a pen, a blotter, a slate, and a bundle of eleven sets of a form that had been sealed and stamped in the hills before anybody had written a word on it.

Marek Vale came in at the first bell and Lenge did not get up.

"You were in the room on the twenty-fourth," said Lenge.

"I was standing in it. The chairs were for the four."

"That is not the same as being at the table and you are not at the table." He put his hand flat on the form without lifting it. "Sit on that stool. I am not being unfriendly. I have eleven sets to complete and a returns sheet to go up with them and the post goes at the third bell, and I would rather be interrupted by a man I know than by a chandler."

---

The copies were eleven.

There is a schedule of four terrace holdings in eleven names, and a schedule of eleven names has eleven parties, and a set goes to each party so that a holding is not told what a man in another holding has been told. The form was a Crown March standard granary form and the first three clauses were the same in every set, and the fourth clause was the underwriting, and in the bottom corner of each sheet was a mark in four small parts that Marek had seen in the tenth month and that nobody in Morrowfen could read.

Lenge filled them in with a hand that had no flourishes in it and went down the page the same way eleven times, and about the fourth set he stopped and swore at the blotter and had to reach behind him for a second one.

"You are going to ask me what the fourth clause does," he said, without looking up.

"I am going to ask you what the head of the third column says."

"That is better and it is a smaller question, and it is the only one of yours I am going to answer before the third bell." He turned the sheet round on the desk and pushed it towards Marek, and then took it back, because a Crown March form does not face a man with no office. "Read it standing up."

Marek read it standing up.

The third column had a head printed above it, in type, and under the head there were eleven ruled spaces, and ten of them had a figure in them, and the eleventh was empty.

"It is the projection for the delivery season," said Petrus Lenge. "Every party on the schedule is entered at the figure for the district it is in, and there is one district in this column and eleven parties in it, so the same figure goes in eleven times, and a man in a good coat looking at eleven identical figures is looking at one. The head of the column is not mine and has never been mine. I do not set it. It is set in the hills and it is printed in a run and it comes down with the form."

"And the figure under it."

"The figure under it is a granary's forward estimate of a district's yield, entered in the ledger in the year before the yield, and I enter it, and I am the only man in the depot who is allowed to enter it, and I have been doing it four years." He squared the sheet. "That is a projection. I have just told you what one is and you did not need it, which I would have bet on."

---

"Why is it entered in advance," said Marek.

"Because the book has to balance in the year it is balancing." Lenge said it without any impatience at all, and then he stopped working, which was the first thing he had done all morning that was not the form. "That is the whole answer and it is a better answer than most men in a good coat get, so I am going to say it twice and then never say it again.

"A depot's book is a book of what the depot expects to have and what the depot expects to owe, and it is made up at the end of the year, and if you leave a column open the whole of that end of the year does not close. Not badly. It does not close. A book with a hole in it is not a book that is a little wrong. It is a book that has thrown away a page, and every figure above the hole and every figure below it is then a figure against nothing, and a man cannot sign a page that is not there.

"So the column gets a figure. And if the figure is wrong, then a man in six weeks looks at the figure and the number is wrong, and a wrong number is an arithmetic matter, and an arithmetic matter is a thing a book has, and it can be corrected in ink with two initials beside it, and the year still closes.

"A blank is not an arithmetic matter. A blank says the office cannot do its job, and a blank cannot be corrected in ink, it can only be filled, and the only person who can fill it is the man who left it empty, and he has to leave the office. I have watched a man leave a depot over a hole in a column. I did not enjoy it and I did not think at the time that it was going to happen to me."

---

Marek put his hand out flat on the desk beside the form and did not touch it.

"There is a terrace in that schedule that has not sent a return."

"One of the four terraces has not sent a sheet this year," said Petrus Lenge, in the voice of a man reading a difficulty off a list he had been reading for four years. "The upland returns are made by the terraces themselves, one sheet, once, in the second month, and that terrace has sent it every year for nineteen. It did not send it this year and the post came and it came and I was told by a traffic clerk on a stair, and I have put that in my own report in the plain words that a party has not returned and the office does not know why, because I am not permitted to send anybody to ask why, and because if I had a reason I would be asked what my reason was based on."

"And you have still got to write a figure under that party."

"I have to write a figure in the eleventh space."

"Out of what."

"Out of the page," said Petrus Lenge. "Out of last season's page, which is nine lines up and which I am going to turn to in a moment and read."

---

He did it in front of Marek, and he did it the ordinary way, and that was the part that made it.

He turned back a page. The column stood there with the same figure eleven times down it, in a hand four years older and by a different man, and he read the eleventh one off, and he wrote it into the eleventh space of this season's column, and he squared the sheet, and he went back to the fourth set and finished the name at the foot of it.

About a minute and a half. He did not hesitate over it and he did not explain himself while he did it, and afterwards he said one thing about it and then he would not say anything else.

"That is not a decision," he said. "I want that on the front of it. That is not me deciding that terrace will do what it did last season. That is me putting the only figure in this room into the only space in this room that has nobody else's figure in it, and if I do not do it then eleven parties get a set of a paper with a hole in it, and one of them gets a paper that says in effect that a man in Verrow has declined to write a number for four hundred acres of ground he has never walked on, and that paper goes up a day of road to that holding and it is not going to be corrected in ink with two initials. It is going to be a hole with a man in it."

"Then the figure is a guess."

"The figure is last season's figure, and last season's figure is a measurement, and it was a measurement of that ground in the year before last, and I am putting a measurement in a column for a season nobody has returned for." He looked up. "That is what a projection is. You wanted a clerk who has to write the number and here he is and he has just told you what he is doing and he is not going to be talked out of it by a man who has nothing to put in the hole instead."

---

Marek Vale had nothing to put in the hole instead. He had a sentence — a class is not a person, and nobody was ever asked — and it was a good sentence and it was the best thing he had carried up eleven switchbacks twice, and it was about four hundred yards of road away from what was in front of him.

"It is not a class," he said instead, because he had looked at the eleven spaces on the schedule behind the form and had said the thing that was in front of him and not the thing he had come with. "It is a terrace that has not sent a sheet. And you are not inventing a number. You are carrying one forward, and the man who carried it forward last year is not in this room, and neither is the man who carried it forward the year before, and that figure is the only thing anybody in this kingdom knows about four hundred acres of ground on a road with a day of switchbacks in it, and there is not one line on that sheet that says whose it is."

"The head of the column is in type," said Petrus Lenge. "I did not set it. And the figure under it is mine, and there is nowhere on this sheet for me to write how I got it, and there is a man at the board who has to be able to say the number is the number, and if I do not write it then there is no number to say."

He squared the sheet for the second time.

"That is the whole of it. That is the whole of what a projection is and I have said it to you in about forty words and I would like the record to show that you asked me four times."

---

At about two bells the chandler's shop sent a boy up with a second lamp, which Lenge did not want, and then sent a boy up again to say that the boy with the first lamp had taken the shop's oil and would be paid for it or would be, in the end, worse. Lenge put a shilling on the table and gave him the shilling, and then wrote eleven names in eleven places, which took him a quarter of an hour, and then ruled a line at the foot of the returns sheet and put nothing under it, because there is no total on that sheet and never has been.

"What is the sum on the clause?" said Marek.

"You are not going to like the answer and it is the same one the man who signed it gave you on a stair in the tenth month."

"I remember it."

"Then you know it. Eleven thousand and some hundreds, and the certified sum is on each of the eleven sets, and the share that falls on any one holding is in the district schedule at Verrow and is not mine to give you, and if I gave it to you on a stair or in a room or on the road you would have it written down in a week, and about nine households in four terraces would be ruined by a figure that has been in a cellar since the flood year." He closed the box and put the key in his coat. "The figure is on the paper. The paper is the only place it is allowed to be."

---

At a quarter before the third bell he tied the eleven sets into a second bundle and put the returns sheet on the top of it and stood looking at the bundle for a moment.

"I am going up that road on the eleventh with these," he said. "Eleven parties, four districts, and a schedule. I go up on the eleventh and I come down on the twelfth if the weather is kind and on the fourteenth if it is not, and I have to serve them in person because a set of a paper is not served by leaving it on a table, and the depot's standing instruction is that a party is served and not a holding, which is a distinction I did not know was a distinction until I came down here."

"Will you take somebody."

"I will not take somebody." He picked the bundle up. "I have to be let in. Eleven households, four terraces, a stone bridge and a road with a day of switchbacks in it, and about four of those doors are held by people who will send me away, and a man from a Crown March office gets one chance at a door and if he is turned away from it the depot's position is that he was served and refused, and he will not have it and neither will I, and about nine sets will come back unopened and the whole of the year will have a hole in it in a different place.

"So no. Unless you have come with me because you would be useful and not because I asked, in which case get your coat, because it is a day up and a day down and I have not got the pair of us to spare on the road."

Marek Vale took his coat off the stool and did not say what he had wanted to say when he asked the question, and they went up the low-ground road together, and the post went at the third bell behind them with eleven sets of a paper on it in which nine figures were other men's and one was a clerk's and one was a hole that was not a hole but a copy of a number a man had written in an earlier year on a page nobody was going to produce.

He had said no to Sabin Rell on the twenty-fourth of the tenth month, in a room over a shop, and had meant it, and had been right for a day, and was now walking up a road to eat breakfast in four hundred yards of somebody else's arithmetic.
