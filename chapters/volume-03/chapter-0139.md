# Chapter 139 — Five Ruled Lines

The copy room on the hill in Bell Stair has a bench along the wall under two windows, and the windows face east across about nine feet of yard, and on a wet afternoon in the harvest month the light comes in sideways off the wall of the building opposite and does not last past the fifth bell, and Sedge had the lamp out on the bench at about the third because of it, and the lamp was at his elbow, and his own shadow went across the paper every time he moved it.

Marek Vale had come down eleven switchbacks that morning and had walked up the causeway with mud on him, and he put a piece of paper on the bench and said:

"I want a form for a rate on a member's ground."

Sedge did not touch it.

"Sit down," he said. "You have told me a thing and I am going to do the opposite of what you want for about four minutes and then I am going to tell you what I am going to do, and you are not going to like the four minutes, and I would like it noticed that I have thought about how to shorten them and I have not, because the four minutes are the work."

He took a sheet of Chamber paper out of the pad on the bench and squared it, and put a straight-edge on it, and did not rule anything for about two of the four minutes.

"You have not said what it is for."

"I have told you what it is for."

"You have told me what it is *about*," said Sedge. "That is a different sentence and I have watched about nine men in this basin confuse the two in one afternoon. What is it for. Not what is it about. What is it for, and who is going to use it, and what is it supposed to stop happening."

Marek said it.

"Then it is a form," said Sedge, "and I am going to cut it, and I am going to tell you the whole of what I am doing while I do it, and the first thing is that I am not going to put a heading on it and the second thing is that I am not going to count you my forms, and both of those are on purpose."

---

He ruled the first line with a carpenter's pencil and a straight-edge and went over it twice with the flat of a smooth thing he keeps for the purpose, which is a piece of window glass worn round at one end, and which is the only tool in that room that nobody else is allowed to touch.

"One. The thing itself, in the words the person behind it used, and not improved, and if there is no person behind it then the line stays empty and that is correct and you may go and be disappointed somewhere else."

"Two. Who holds the other end of it, and since when, and if nobody holds the other end then it says nobody holds the other end."

"Three. The return in kind. In kind. Labour, seed, hours, whatever it actually was, and a figure is not a return in kind and a figure is not going in this line."

"Four. Anything anybody else has to say about it, and there may be nothing on the fourth, and there is very often nothing on the fourth, and that is not a failure of the form, it is the commonest thing that happens on it."

He ruled the fourth and squared the pencil up with the edge of the sheet and stopped, and put the straight-edge down, and sat back.

"There is no fifth."

"There are five lines," said Marek.

"There are five lines," Sedge agreed, "and I have ruled four, and I am not going to rule the fifth yet, and I want to tell you why, and the why is the only interesting thing in this room tonight and it has taken me about a day and a half and a form that is not this one."

---

"A form is not a hole," he said. "I want to say that before anything else, because I have heard the other thing said in this city by a man in a good coat and I do not agree with it, and the yard's form has two lines in it that are empty on purpose and the sixth one is empty for ever and it is one of the best pieces of paper in this kingdom. A hole on a form is a thing you can be shown and a hole on a form is a place to put something. So this is not a hole and I am not cutting one, and if you go and tell the seed house there is a hole in it, you will be telling them the wrong thing and they will try to fill it.

"What is at the head of a line is a kind. That is the whole of it and it is why there are no boxes. A box is a thing a person can be put in. A line is a thing a person has to write in. And the word at the top of a line is a kind, and the kind is chosen by the man who cuts the form and not by the person who is stood in front of it, and every bad instrument I have ever had to file in this building got filed in a box with a kind on it, and every bad instrument anybody has ever put in front of me this year had a word at the top of it that somebody in a house I have never been to picked out of a case of type.

"So I am not going to name what this form is about. I am going to rule five lines and I am going to name four of them out loud to you, and I am going to leave the fifth one with nothing at the top of it, and the reason is that I do not know what the fifth one is called, and I have been up two nights working out that I do not know, and it is not that I have not thought about it."

---

"I have thought about it," he said, "and here is where I got to, and you may laugh and I would rather you did not.

"A rate is a charge on a ground. A ground is a piece of the low ground with a roll number on it and a man or a woman who holds it, and the holding is a fact about a person and can be entered, and the rate is not a fact about a person at all. A rate is a fact about the ground. And the ground is not here. The ground is four miles off and it is under about nine people's feet and it cannot be told anything, and it cannot be asked whether it will bear it, and it cannot refuse, and it is not in this room and it is not going to be in this room, and there is no arrangement of paper that puts a piece of the low ground in a chair with a hand on it.

"So if I put a word at the top of the fifth line, then that word is a kind, and the man who is stood in front of my form with a rate to pay is going to read that word and be told what he is, and the word is not going to be *ground*, because *ground* is a kind and a kind is a box, and if it is *ground* then I have filed a charge against a class, and I have done the thing in this basin that everybody says must never be done, and I have done it with a pencil and a straight-edge in a copy room with a lamp at my elbow, and nobody will ever be able to say who did it, because there will be no name on it.

"And I am not going to do that, and the way I am not going to do it is by having no word there at all, and that is a thing I have never done before and I have been in this building long enough that twice in it and I have never once left a line bare that somebody could put a head on afterwards, and I have not been sure about this one for about a day and a half, which is longer than it has ever taken me about anything, and that is how I know it is the right one and it is also how I know it is the wrong one and I cannot separate the two and neither can you."

---

He picked the straight-edge up again and put the pencil on the sheet and did not rule.

"Now I am going to say my line," he said, "and I am going to say it badly, and I am telling you that in advance so that when it does not come out right you do not think somebody has come in and taken it off me.

"A form has to be cut by a man who does not know whether it is right."

It came out flat. It came out about as flat as a thing can come out, and it is the same sentence in the same order as the two times he has said it in this basin, and he heard it, and he stopped.

"Three times," said Sedge. "Once in a yard in Tallow Ward to about a hundred and forty people, and it was the best thing I have ever said in my life. Once in a printer's shop under a chandler's yard with my hand flat on a margin, and it was nearly as good. And once this afternoon to a man in a wet coat, and it has come out like a rule out of a book.

"That is the cost of it and I have been paying it since the seed month and I did not know that was what it was. A man who has said a thing three times has stopped being able to say it fresh, and a man who has stopped being able to say a thing fresh is a man who ought not to be cutting anything at all, and I am cutting a thing with a lamp at my elbow, and I know that, and I would like it said out loud in this room by somebody, because if nobody says it then it is a thing I have noticed on my own and I have never in my life been able to trust a thing I have noticed on my own."

"It is said," said Marek.

"Thank you," said Sedge. "Now I am going to cut the fifth line and I am not going to name it and you are not going to name it, and if you name it in a yard in Rainmarket I will hear about it in about nine days and I will come down that hill and take the form back off you."

---

He ruled the fifth line.

He ruled it the same way as the other four, with the same straight-edge held at the same angle from the same edge of the sheet, and the space above it and the space below it are the same space as the space above the fourth and the space below the fourth, and that is the whole of his technique for that line and it took him about four seconds and it is the only four seconds in the evening that were not difficult.

The spacing was even. He had gone over it twice with the glass and he had not made it even and uneven, and when Marek said *why even* he said: "Because if I make the fourth one tighter or the fifth one looser then I have told the room which of them I thought was the important one, and the only man in this city who is qualified to say which of these is important is the person who fills it in, and he is not in this room and he has not read it yet."

He cut the sheet off the pad with the knife, and squared it, and put it on the bench, and there were five ruled lines on it and no boxes and a lamp and a straight-edge and a piece of window glass worn round at one end.

"That is the form," he said. "It is not the yard's form and it does not go near the yard's form, and the yard's form has six lines and I am not cutting a seventh and nobody is going to put this one in that shed. And it is not a copy of the yard's form, and I want that in your head in my words, because in about four months somebody is going to say it is, and it is not, and the difference between them is that the yard's form was cut for a person about themselves and this one is cut for a charge, and I do not know which of those is the worse thing to cut a form for and I have not got anybody to ask."

He looked at the five lines for a while.

"I have been in this building long enough that twice in it," he said, "and I do not know whether that is right."

Marek Vale stood in a copy room on a hill in Bell Stair and put two fingers on the fifth line, where there was nothing at the top of it and nothing at the bottom of it, and understood, standing up, in about four seconds, the whole of what had happened to him in three days and eleven switchbacks: that the absence was not an oversight, and that it was the only part of the five lines that had been cut by a man who had understood the thing, and that there was no office in this kingdom in which anybody could be told so, and that in about two months, or whenever eleven weeks ran out, he was going to have to go up a road and say the same thing a second time to a woman with a jar on a rail who had told him in advance that it would only be told once, and that he had not worked out how a person says a thing twice without the second one sounding like a receipt for the first one.
