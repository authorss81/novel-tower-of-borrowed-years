# Chapter 108 — A Terrace at the Door

The bench was in the shed on the twenty-eighth of the tenth month because the rota on the seed house wall said so, and about eighty people were in the lane outside the Gate shed by the fourth bell, and Teodor Lask was reading somebody's second line out loud with his back to a germination bench and a light in his hand because the shed does not have windows that open.

The germination bench took up eleven feet of the eleven feet by nine. There were about twenty people in the room. Two of them were on the bench.

A woman came down the lane at about the fourth bell with a cloth bundle over her arm, and she was forty-four and she had come four miles and down eleven switchbacks and had been on the road since the second, and she asked at the door whether a person could have a paper read out.

Sanne Droll is the yard's second person at that register and is a rope-maker of Ropewalk, thirty-nine, and she is asked the same four questions like anybody else, and she was on the trestle in the yard all morning with the levy box, and she came in and heard the ask and answered it before anybody else could, which is her job and is not a favour.

"Anybody may ask," she said. "Nothing is charged. Bring her in at the front, because there are eighty in the lane and the fourth question is asked at the counter and not at the door."

The woman at the front of the lane was not the woman with the cloth.

The woman at the front of the lane had been there since the fourth bell with a folded paper in her hand, and she was about fifty, and she had come off the north road, and she wanted to know whether a sum of money that she had been told she owed was a thing she owed, and it took eleven minutes and it was a rate on a shed and the answer was a shed and not a house and there is no form in this kingdom on which a shed can be asked anything.

Then the woman with the cloth came in, and there were about eighteen people in the shed, and Teodor Lask put his hand flat on the board.

"Sit down if you can and stand if you cannot," he said. "I read it twice and I do not improve it. Is the paper yours?"

"It is a copy of a thing on a schedule," said the woman. "I have brought the schedule's fourth column. I wrote it out myself at the top terrace because I could not carry the schedule and I was not going to leave it. It is in my hand. It is not signed by anybody. **I have had a piece of my own handwriting in my apron for nine days and I have not known what to do with it, and I have brought it four miles to a shed.**"

Her name was Zofia Teran and she was forty-four and she kept the second terrace's tally in a school exercise book, and she said that last part in the middle of the room without being asked and about nine people looked up.

"Who is the person behind this," said Teodor Lask.

And Zofia Teran stopped, and looked at the cloth, and answered in a way that the room went quiet for.

"Nobody," she said. "I have thought about it for nine days and I have got to this. My holding is on it. I am on it, in the way that a number is on a thing — my name is against my holding and not against the paper. The paper is between the depot at Verrow and a house I have never been to, and it is about barley, and the fourth column is what happens if the barley does not come.

"And I am not allowed to say the fourth column, and I have got about nine days to think about why not and this is the answer and I have not liked it. The fourth column is not a rate and it is not a sum and it is not a name. **It is a class of people. I am in it this year. I am not expecting to be in it in nine years, and the people who will be are the ones I have been raising.**

"So the paper is about my ground and the fourth column is about people I will not be standing next to, and there are some of them I could name and I am not going to name them, and I have not asked any of them, and I have had nine days to decide whether to ask and I have decided not to, and I would like it written down that I decided not to and not that I forgot.

"And I have brought it here to find out what happens to a piece of paper when the person it is about is not the person who is holding it. I have not got a second reason."

---

Neris Kade had come up the road with her and had been in the lane for an hour and forty minutes and had said nothing at all to anybody, which the yard has a rule about and has never found a way to enforce.

She came in at about the fourth bell and the reader named her at the door, because that is what a second person is, and he said it in the ordinary voice in which a man says a thing that happens every morning.

"Second person. Neris Kade, season examiner, not a member, not of this yard."

"Yes," said Kade.

"You do not get a hand on anything and you do not enter a word and if you are lying I cannot find out and I never could."

"That is the rule and it is a good one," said Kade, "and I have read it in a room off a seed room as well as in this one and I have never said so and I am not going to now."

She stood against the wall by the door with her hands behind her back for the whole of it, which is what a second person does.

---

Marek Vale was behind two trestles and a board with a form on it, and the form has six ruled lines and four of them have ever had a hand on them, and he could not enter one word of any of it, and he had been a reader of that room for forty days, and the only thing he had was the sentence a yard gave him at a tank in about a hundred and forty people's hearing on a date with nothing above it, which is that if he did not stop when somebody said *that is not what I said*, he was a man in a good coat and not a reader, and anybody in the yard was to say so to his face.

"I am going to read you the rule," he said, "and then I am going to tell you the answer, and I am not going to ask you anything, and you are not going to like the answer and I am not going to improve it.

"The rule is this. It goes in as its parts, with the sum, the date, the discount and the receiving party on each line, and the counterweight named to the people who will bear it and the number of people who will bear it, in the words those people used. And if a counterweight is named as a district, there is nobody to name it to, and the line does not go in at all.

"That is the whole rule, it has been the whole rule since the eighteenth of the ninth month, and it has been said out loud in this yard about nine times a day. And there is a first half to it that people do not say out loud, and I am going to say the first half out loud because you have come four miles.

"**A person may enter a line about their own paper. Nobody may enter a line about anybody else's.** Not a factor, not a house, not an office, not a man with no office. And the first question at that counter, every morning, is *is that your line, is that the person behind it, is it your words* — and I asked that question and you answered it, and you said nobody, and you said it correctly, and you have been carrying the answer down a road in a cloth for nine days.

"So the line does not go in. And I want to be exact about the reason, because the reason is not the one you have been telling yourself on the road."

He read the rule out again, all of it, at the ordinary speed, and then he read the reason out.

"The paper is between the depot at Verrow and a house you have never been to. You are on it the way a number is on a thing — your name is against your holding. **And the fourth column is a class of people, and a class of people is nobody, and the reason a class of people is nobody is the whole reason the line is not going in, and it is not a technicality and it is not a gap in the rule. The rule was written for a person who can be named to their own door. A class of people cannot be traced to a door, and so there is nobody to name the counterweight to, and so the fourth line stays empty, and so the line does not go in at all.**

"That is the rule, and it is the first rule, and you were the person it was written for, and I am sorry about it in the only way a room can be sorry, which is by reading it out."

There were about twenty people in a shed built for twenty-two and nobody said anything for about four seconds, and then the reader asked the second question, because the reader asks the second question every morning and it is a question with a room in it.

"Is that your line?"

"That is my ground," said Zofia Teran.

"That is not what I asked and I am not going to ask it again," said Teodor Lask, "and the answer is no, and it is the same answer you gave, and I am going to read the fourth line out now and it is going to be empty and I am going to read it twice."

Neris Kade said, at the wall by the door, in a voice about nine people could hear and that nobody has ever written down: "You could have made a ruling. Somebody was standing in this room who had been waiting all month to make one."

"So somebody could," said Marek Vale. "And there is no form on which any of us is anybody's record, and in nine months somebody who was not here is going to read a rule off a board and nobody is going to be able to say who made it or when, and I have watched that happen to a yard's own method once already and I am not doing it twice."

"I know," said Neris Kade. "I have read it in a shed off a seed room as well."

---

The reading took eleven minutes and it was done properly, which is the only word for it.

Nothing went in, because there was nobody to enter it and there is no half of a line.

The reader read the pieces of it out anyway, to about twenty people, standing up, with a germination bench against his back, because that is the second half of the rule and it is the half nobody remembers. The date is the sixth of the ninth month and it is the date at the head of a column of a form that has no date on it. The receiving party is a house. The sum is not stated here. And the discount is not applicable, because there is no assignment on this paper; it is a grain contract and not a rate arrears, and that is a thing a person cannot tell in a shed and a person can tell at a counter.

And the fourth line is the counterweight, and it is ruled, and it is blank, and the reader read it out as blank, twice, at the ordinary speed.

"The fourth line is empty," he said. "I have read the rule. The counterweight on this paper is named as a class of people and it cannot be read out and it cannot be written in and I am not going to pretend to you that it is there."

And he read the empty line out again, twice, at the ordinary speed, because that is what a room does.

Zofia Teran stood in the middle of a shed built for twenty-two with about twenty people in it and a germination bench against the wall and listened to a boy of nineteen read a ruled line that was empty.

"I want to say what I have got," she said, "and then I am going to go, because I have been on the road since the second and I am going to be back at the top terrace before the dark or I will not be useful to anybody in the morning.

"I have got what I came for. **Your rule is right and it is the rule that is stopping me and I would like to be exact with you, because I have been four days trying to work out how to be angry and I cannot manage it.** I have come four miles and a half and I have had about two hours in a lane, and a boy of nineteen has read my own handwriting out twice at the ordinary speed, and there is not one figure in this that I could not get in a room in Verrow if I walked up there and asked at the right counter with the right paper, and I cannot get there, because I have four children and a walk of two days, and the depot at Verrow is not going to be glad to see me.

"So I have got a thing I could not get before, and it is that the room says the fourth line out as empty. **In nine days I have had a piece of paper that says what will happen to me and I have had a room that will say out loud, to anybody who asks, that there is nothing in the fourth line.** And that is not a seed and it is not a rate and it is not a room with a man in a good coat in it, because a man in a good coat could take that sentence into Verrow in a fortnight.

"And I am not being thanked and I am not being given anything and I have been standing here for a quarter of an hour in a shed with a bench in it and I have not been able to find out one thing, which is what is supposed to happen. So I am going to say it and go, and I would like nobody to stop me and I would like nobody to walk up that road with me."

Nobody stopped her. Sanne Droll came out of the yard and stood in the lane and watched her go, because Sanne Droll is the yard's second person and it is not her job to stop anybody and it is not nothing to her either.

---

At the seventh bell the lamp went on and about nine people who had been in the lane at the fourth bell were not in it at the seventh, and the room had still not found a way to know which nine, and the fifth line of the form on the board was empty, and the sixth line was empty and always would be, and the rota on a wall in Rainmarket had days on it and nothing at the head of it.

Sanne Droll came in from the yard and stood at the back with her arms folded for about a minute, because she is the yard's second person and it is not her job to stop anybody, and then she said one thing to the reader that is not in the rule and has not been said in that shed before.

"You read it out," she said. "You have read it out twice in front of a person. **That is the first time this room has said a thing about a paper to the person the paper is about and not to the room.** And I would like the yard to have noticed it and I am not going to have anybody write it down."

Nobody wrote it down.

The reader looked at the board for a while and then took the chalk and wrote, at the foot of the wall list, under the column that has nothing at the head of it, a single line in his own hand, which was the date, and which is the only thing he has ever entered on that board that was not a line somebody was asking for.

Zofia Teran went up the road at about the seventh bell, alone, in the dark, with eleven switchbacks and four miles of it in front of her and a cloth bundle on her arm with her own handwriting in it, and she was not walked up the road by anybody, and the lane was still full behind her, and in a shed eleven feet by nine with a germination bench against the wall a boy of nineteen read the fourth line out again, twice, at the ordinary speed, to nobody at all, because the room had emptied and he reads it twice.
