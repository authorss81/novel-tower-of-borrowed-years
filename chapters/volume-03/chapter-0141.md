# Chapter 141 — What the Yard Cannot Enter

The barrow came round the tank at about the sixth bell on the twenty-second of the ninth month with a hundred-pound sack on it, and Milan Vray took the sack off with his back, because he had been taking sacks off with his back since the flood year, and set it down on the folded trestle against the wall, and the trestle took it.

The register had shut at the fourth bell. The shed was shut and the notice was on it. There were about nine people in the yard because the yard is where people are at the sixth bell, and Marek Vale came round the tank with a slate under his arm and a piece of chalk in his hand, and put the slate down on the trestle on top of the sack, and Vray moved the sack nine inches without comment.

"There is no office," Marek said. "That is the first thing, and I want it in front of me before the other two. I have been looking for the room for a fortnight and there is not one, and I am not going to make one, so I am going to say two things to this yard and then I am going to stop talking."

"Put the chalk down."

"I am not writing anything."

"You have had that chalk in your hand for a minute and a half and you have not written anything with it, and I have watched about nine men do that in this yard," said Vray. "Say your two things."

---

The first thing was that the advance was eleven thousand and some hundreds of pounds, that it was already in the ground in four hundred acres above a stone bridge, and that an office a night and a day up the river had a standing instruction and a man whose whole business was taking it back, and that it could be done in nine days.

The second thing was that leaving it where it was and spending it on seed in the four terraces instead was not correct, had no office behind it, could not be produced by anybody, and was the only one of the two that did not put a bad autumn onto four households in a season that had already failed once.

Vray listened to both of them standing up, and did not interrupt either, and when the second one was finished he said:

"I have heard both. I am not going to choose between them, and you have not asked me to, and I want it noticed that you have not asked me to, because every man who comes round this tank with two things has asked me to."

"I have not."

"No," said Vray. "You have put them on a trestle and you have come to a yard. And now I am going to tell you what a yard is, and then I am going to go and put a bar across a store, and you may say one thing before I do that."

---

Sanne Droll came out of the yard office with the levy box in her hand and stopped about four feet off, because a rope-maker of thirty-nine who has been the yard's second person at that register for a year does not stop closer than four feet to a slate on a trestle.

She was going to carry the tin through to the office. She put it down on the wall instead, and she said:

"Whose paper is it."

"Say the four questions."

"The first three are for a piece of paper a man has brought in with him about himself," said Sanne Droll, "and every one of them wants the paper. The fourth one is the only one that does not want a paper, and the fourth one wants a person in this yard, and you have not got a person in this yard, and that is not a fault in the fourth question. That is the whole of what the fourth question is."

"I have not got a paper either," said Marek. "I have got two sentences and a figure that is not allowed to be a figure."

"Then the first three cannot be put to it," said Droll, "and the fourth one is the only one left, and you have brought me a thing with no person behind it, and I cannot enter it and I cannot refuse it, and I have never once been in a position where I could do either with a thing like that and I have been at this counter for a year."

"Which of those two is the worse one."

"That is a question you have brought to the wrong counter and you know it is, and I am going to answer it anyway because you have come up the road with it and there is nobody else in this ward you can put it to.

"Refusing is the easy one. Refusing is a thing a person can do with their mouth and it costs nothing and it leaves nothing behind and there is not one of us in this yard who cannot refuse. We refuse about nine things a week and I could refuse you in about four seconds and you would go up that road and think about it and be back in the rains month.

"Entering is the other one. Entering is a thing that gets a line on a board, and a line on a board is a thing a person can be shown, and if there is a line on that board in the morning then this yard has said that eleven thousand and some hundreds of pounds is a thing a person brought in with him about himself, and it is not, and it is a district's figure in a book in the hills, and I would have entered a district."

---

Marek stood in the yard with that for about a minute.

"A district's figure," he said.

"That is what it is, and I have not seen it. A man in a good coat came down this lane in the tenth month of last year and stood about where you are standing and said the basin feeds three regions, and he said it in a room and not in a shed, and he was right, and about nine people in Tallow Ward have told that story since and every one of them has said the word *district* and not one of them has said the word *household*, and that is not a thing any of us noticed at the time, and I have had a year to notice it in."

She put her hand flat on the trestle next to the sack.

"And you cannot put a district in a line in this shed and I am not going to, and neither is the man at that counter, and the reason the two of us are agreed about that without ever having said so is that about nineteen of us found out the same way in a fortnight in the seed month and one of us was nineteen and read a fourth line out twice at the ordinary speed and did not write it, and there is a notice on that wall in Sedge's hand that says what this room is and it does not say a Crown March office, and I would like it noticed that a notice is the only thing in this yard that has ever cost nothing."

---

The yard-man came back from the store without having said anything and stood at the other end of the trestle, and Marek turned round.

"You have not been listening."

"I have been listening for nine minutes," said Milan Vray, "from about twenty feet, which is the distance a yard-man stands at, and I have understood about half of it, and the half I have understood is that you have come here to find out whether this yard can do a thing."

"Yes."

"No," said Vray. "And I am going to tell you why not, and I am going to do it in one sentence and then I am going to say the other thing, and the other thing is the reason you came, so you will forgive me."

He put both hands flat on the trestle.

"This yard can say no," he said. "That is the whole of what it has ever had. A man in a good coat can do the same thing, and he can put my yard's no in his own mouth and he can be in a room in nine months and say that a yard in Tallow Ward made it, and there is nothing in this kingdom I can do about that and there is nothing anybody has ever been able to do about that, and I have stopped minding it and I have not stopped being careful."

---

"You have come to the right yard and you have come for the wrong reason," he said. "You are not here because you think a yard can do a thing. You are here because there is no other room in this basin that will let you stand in it and say two sentences without one of them ending up on a nail.

"And there is no other room because a yard is the only kind of room anybody in this kingdom has ever built that does not want a decision out of it. That is what you are standing in. There is a shed with a form on a trestle and four hours a day and a notice on the door that says what this room is, and there is nobody behind any of it, and if you say two things in here this evening then in about four years there is a man in a good coat who can say that a yard said them, and the yard will not be able to say he is wrong, because the yard will not remember."

"You will remember."

"I will remember," said Vray, "and I do not write, and there is a boy of nineteen who reads a fourth line out twice at the ordinary speed and does not write either, and that is not a system, it is two men, and one of us is going to die first and after that it is a building with a notice on it."

He straightened up and looked at the sack on the trestle.

"I am not going to be thanked for any of this," he said, "and the yard has not been thanked for anything since the eighteenth of the ninth month and is not going to be, and I have made my peace with that and I would like it written on the slate if anybody is going to write anything on the slate, and nobody is going to write anything on the slate, so it can stay off."

---

Aldis Kern came out to the step and did not come further, which is what he does, and he listened to about four minutes of it and then said one thing and went back in.

"There was a set-off on a nail in a seed house four miles and eleven switchbacks up that road since the sixth of the third month," he said, "and it is still on the nail, and every person in this ward has heard about it and about nine of them have asked me this month whether it is going to be served, and I have said no to all of them and I have said it eleven times and I did it the same way eleven times and I did not think about it once, and I would like that noticed in a yard by somebody who is not me."

"And when somebody asks you in four years whether a yard refused it," said Marek, "you will not be able to say you did not."

"No," said Aldis Kern. "That is the arrangement and I made it and I would make it again, and I have never once been able to work out whether the eleven refusals did anything at all or whether I have been standing in a doorway being useful at somebody."

---

Marek Vale picked the chalk up.

He wrote the first thing on the slate, in four lines, in his own hand, and it took him about a minute, and then he read it back to the yard and it was wrong in the way he expected it to be wrong, which is that a sentence about taking money back in the third month of a year reads in the ninth month of the same year like a thing somebody meant to do.

Then he wiped it with his sleeve and wrote the second thing on the same slate, and that took him about nine seconds, and then he stopped with his hand flat on the slate and did not wipe it.

"You have written two things on one board," said Vray.

"Yes."

"That is a form."

"It is not a form, it is a slate, and there is a rag in this yard and I will wipe it and the yard can be about its business, and I will tell you why I have not wiped it yet, and it is not because I want to read it in the morning."

"Then it is not because you want to read it in the morning," said Vray.

"No," said Marek. "It is because there is nobody in this kingdom I can hand a slate to."

---

He wiped it.

It took about four seconds with a rag that had been on a nail in that yard since the flood year, and it did not come off entirely, because slate does not come off entirely, and what was left on it by the seventh bell was a grey field with two sets of half-words in it that nobody could read and that nobody could take away, and the tank, the trestle, the sack and about nine people going out of the yard were all in the same state.

Sanne Droll picked the levy tin up off the wall and carried it into the office and shut the door. Milan Vray went off to put a bar across a store. Petrik came along the wall with the chalk after the lamp was lit and put the days on the oiled board, and there was nothing at the head of the board, and nobody told him to put anything there.

The wet mark on the trestle was still there in the morning, and it was not legible, and there is no office in this kingdom in which a man may be sent to ask what was written on it.
