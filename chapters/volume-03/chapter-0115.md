# Chapter 115 — A School Exercise Book

Zofia Teran kept the tally in a school exercise book because a school exercise book costs nothing and has a page for every day in it whether you use it or not, and she had been keeping it since before she had the children, and on the ninth of the third month she read it out loud in her own yard at the fourth bell with nine people standing about in it and two of them holding a bar.

She read it flat. That is the thing about it and it is the reason it is worth an hour of anybody's time. She read it the way a person reads out a list of things that were said to them, at the ordinary speed, and she did not improve it, and she did not stop at the parts that made the yard go quiet, and about nine people in a lane in a valley heard a flat voice go for about forty minutes over everything that had happened to the four terraces since the ninth month of last year.

Then she shut it and said that was all of it, and that it was not a record, and that anybody who wanted to call it a record could go and try. She said the reasons before she started, because she had been asked for the book twice and had said no twice and had worked out that a woman who keeps a book in a yard owes people the reasons.

One: it has no name at the head of it and no name at the foot of it, and in this basin a thing with nobody's name on it cannot be brought into a room by anybody, which is not a complaint, it is the arrangement.

Two: it is hers, and she is not a member of anything, and there is no form in this kingdom in which anybody may be applied for, so the book cannot be sent to a house by anybody in it, and if she were to walk it down to a hall in Bell Stair she would be a woman carrying a document into a building she has no standing in, and about nine people who have been in such a building would tell her that.

Three: the door is on the page and the man at the door is not, and she has decided that that is how it stays, because the day she begins putting men on doors is the day the book stops being a thing she keeps about what she was told and becomes a thing somebody could produce at somebody.

Four: it cannot be checked. A page in a book can be checked against every other page in the book. A book in an apron cannot be checked against anything, and a thing that cannot be checked cannot contradict anybody, and so it cannot be a record, and she is quite clear about the difference between a book that cannot be checked and a record.

Five, and she said this one twice: it is a woman's account of what she was told, and what she was told is not what was said.

---

"You can ask me what is in it," she said, before she started, "and I will read it, and if I read it you are not to write any of it down, and I do not care what it is about. That is the whole of the terms and I have never had anybody to put terms to before and I have thought about them for about a year."

Nobody said anything, and a man in the yard said, "Read it."

The book is a school exercise book. It has a page for every day of about twenty years, and most of the pages are empty, and the ones that are not empty have four things on them: a door, a day, and what was said at the door, and who said it. It has no name at the head of it. It has no name at the foot of it. It has a child's arithmetic on the flyleaf and a list of the days the water came in another year, and it is the best record of the season that anybody in this basin has, and it is not a record, and here is the whole of the reason in five things she said at the end.

---

What she read was this, in the order of the doors and not the order of the story, which is the order it was written in.

*Fourth of the ninth month. A door on the first terrace. Told that the sowing has come early and that it came from the hills and that there is a paper for it, and that the paper is at the depot and not in the valley. Asked me not to write it down. I did not write it down. I am writing it now, four days later, and that is a thing I have decided to do and not a thing I was told to do.*

*Eleventh of the ninth month. A door on the second terrace. A woman of fifty-one. She said the low ground is eating and we are not, and she said the price of flour, and she said the price of flour in a voice she did not choose, and she asked me not to say where I had it. I have not said where I had it.*

*Second month. Hartlow, at the low door. The keeper of the roll. Asked me to say a thing to his face so that it has been said to his face, and then said that if anybody took it up a road in a bag he would have had the only thing that was his taken off him. I said it to his face.*

*And then, in the same hand, lower down, and this is the part I want read out and this is the part I am not proud of:* *A door. A garden door. A man I have known nineteen years. He said the road was opened on purpose and I asked him for what and he said for a paper, and he said he would rather it was written down that he knew, and I said I do not write things down for people. And I have written it down.*

---

Somebody laughed, once, and stopped.

"That is me," said the man in the yard, who was about fifty-one and had a bar in his hand.

"Yes."

"You wrote it in a room. I said it in a lane at a door in the rain and you have it in a room."

"It is not a room. It is a yard and it is a book and it has not been out of my apron in about a year." She turned round about nine pages. "There. That is where it is. If you want it out I will cut the page out with a knife this afternoon and you can have it, and I would like it said that I offered."

"I said what I said."

"You said what you said in a lane at a door in the rain, and I wrote it down four days later in a kitchen, and I have put the day on it, and there is a difference between those two things that you would not care about and I have thought about it every day since and I cannot make it go away. So I will not cut it out and neither will I say you did not say it."

The man with the bar stood in about nine feet of a lane and did not say anything for about four seconds, and then he said, "Leave it," and went back to the cut, and that was the end of it and nobody thanked anybody.

---

"You may have the names," she said. "I have decided. I said on a road in the tenth month that I would not name them and I have not named them and I was right, and I have been wrong about being right, and I am not going to be wrong about it twice.

"It is my book. The class is mine to talk about because I have been in those doors and they have said things to me and I did not ask them for anything. I have not asked one of them a single question about the paper. I have had a year to do it and there is a form in this kingdom for about four things and it is not one of them, and if I ask a man whether he is going to be in a class of people after this season, then he is being asked, and if he is being asked then something has been done, and nothing has been done. I decided not to and I would like that said in whatever room it is written in, and it is not that I forgot. Nobody forgets a thing for a year."

So she gave them nine names.

She gave them plainly, and she said a thing about each of them that had been said at a door and not anything else, and she did not say whether any of them knew what the fourth column of a schedule said, and she did not say whether any of them was going to be in a class of people in nine years, and when a man at the back said *which nine* she said, "That is not what you asked and I am not going to answer it, and you did not ask it, and I am going to stand here and let the yard decide that it asked it."

---

And then a man in a good coat with no office in it said the thing that made the yard sit down, and he said it by accident, and about nine people heard it.

He said, "You are reading it the way the reader reads it at my counter."

Zofia Teran stopped.

"Two times, at the ordinary speed, and not improving it, and the flat voice," said Marek Vale, "because I was in that room on the twenty-eighth of the tenth month when a woman came four miles and eleven switchbacks with a piece of her own handwriting in a cloth, and a boy of nineteen read her fourth line out to about twenty people twice and would not pretend there was anything in it, and it is the best four minutes of my year and it happened in a shed in Tallow Ward and I did not do it and neither did the yard."

"You are saying I learned it off a boy in a shed."

"I am saying nobody can invent that. There is no office in this kingdom that knows you should do that, and no form, and the only two people alive who have heard a woman read her own words out flat to a room are me and you, and one of us is in a coat and one of you is not." He looked at the exercise book in her hand. "And I want to say that I have never once been able to do that, for three months, in a shed, four hours a day, because when I read out somebody's second line I am reading it to a room and I know it, and you have been doing it in a yard to about nine people for about a year and nobody ever told you that it was a thing."

---

She shut the book.

"I am not going to be the woman who came down the hill," she said.

The yard was quiet for about four seconds and Marek Vale found, about nine seconds later, standing in a lane four miles above a stone bridge, that he had been about to say something, and that he was not going to be thanked for it, and that he was not going to be forgiven for it either, and that in the tenth month a man with a very small wick in a lamp had stood on a step in the dark and said *I am not going to be the thing you came up here about*, and that this had been said once already, on a road, by the only man in the valley with a right to say it, and that it did not change here, and that a woman in a lane was entitled to say the same thing about herself without owing either of them anything for it.

"Nobody is asking you to be anything," he said.

"Then I would like it read out," said Zofia Teran, "that I said it, and that it was refused, and that the book is not a record and cannot be produced in any room in this kingdom, and that I have been asked for it twice by a man with no office and have said no twice, and that the reason it is the best thing anybody has is that there are nine entries on it from this season and the fourth one is a road somebody opened on purpose, and the reason it is not a record is that it is a woman's account of what she was told, and what she was told is not what was said, and there is not one of the nine of us in this yard who could stand up in a room in Verrow and be asked about it."

She put the book under her arm and went back to the rope and the child's coat, and the two men in the cut went back to the cut, and the fourth bell went, and the work went on, because the work goes on and does not know that a book was read in it.

Marek Vale walked down eleven switchbacks with a fact in his head that he could not enter anywhere, and on the second terrace the wall in the drain had about four feet of new silt in it and about nine people were on the second terrace's ground who had not been on it in the second month, and the set-off was on a nail in a seed house, and the projection for the coming season was in a room over a chandler's shop on the low-ground road with a figure in it that a man had carried forward from a page nobody was going to produce.
