# Chapter 137 — The Fourth Line From the End

The floor of the Elmsworth seed house is swept twice a day and it is swept by a girl of about thirteen who is not a member and is paid by the day, and on the ninth of the ninth month she was on her second sweep at about the fifth bell with a broom made of about nine lengths of hazel bound with tarred twine, and she swept the chaff off the boards and the sand off the boards and then swept about four inches of it back under the bench where it had come from, which she has done every day since the seed month and which nobody in the building has ever mentioned to her.

The lamp has been in since the third month and it burns from the first bell to the seventh and the sixty-one trays stand along the long wall under oiled paper in two ranks, and the count goes on the bench book at the seventh bell with nothing at the head of the page, and the boy who does the count is a member's son of about eleven called Petrik and he can count to a hundred and about nine and he does not write.

He was not writing now either. What he was doing with the chalk was the days, on the oiled board on the wall by the door, which is the rota, and which has days on it and nothing at the head of it — not the day, not the season, not the Share's name, and not a keeper's name, and a woman of thirty-four found that out in the growth month and said leave it bare, put the days on, because the day it is on a board with a name at the top of it somebody will work out that the trays are a resource and the days are mine.

Petrik puts the days on. He puts them on in a hand that gets better and then gets worse across the week and then gets better again. Nobody has ever told him what to do about the top of the board and nobody is going to.

---

The caps are on a nail by the door and there are sixty-one of them and they are the Share's and they are worked round, because a cap is a thing a person wears and it is not a thing a person holds.

Every one of them has a name in the band in ink. Fisk wrote them in the seed month, all sixty-one, in about an hour, off the roll, in a hand that is better than the count's. She wrote them because the seed house is cold in the dark and sixty-one people work at a bench in it twice a day, and because a cap with a name in the band is the only thing in this basin that looks exactly like an answer and is not one.

The cap of the woman on the fourth line from the end of the roll was on the bench, and the girl filling cones at that bench was wearing it, and the cap of the fourth line from the end was also on the nail behind her with the same name in the band of it, and it had been on that nail since the bad autumn, and nobody had ever moved it and nobody had ever asked.

---

Mirren Fisk was at the far end of the bench with a slate and a string and was doing the thing she does at the sixth bell, and Marek Vale came in off the low-ground road at about the fourth bell on the ninth of the ninth month, on foot, four days off a boat, with a bundle of sixty-one returned forms still in his coat because he had not worked out what to do with them, and he stood in the doorway for about four seconds so that everybody in the room knew he was in it.

"There is one thing in this building that nobody is going to ask her," said Mirren Fisk, before he had got nine feet off the door.

He stopped.

"Not a rule and not a favour and not a thing I have come to ask you to agree with," she said, without turning round, and she carried on with the string. "It is an arrangement and I am making it and I am telling you what it is so that nobody in this room has to be careful and so that nobody in this room gets praised for it later.

"The fourth line from the end of that board is a rate on a ground. It is going to be a rate on that ground in the fourth month because the second sowing did not come and our own rule does the eleven and the third of a sack and then the balance, and the balance goes on the ground of the line, and that has been our own rule since before either of us and I did not write it and I cannot unwrite it.

"That line is a woman of forty-one and she has been ill since the flood year and she did not come on the ninth. And the girl filling cones at that bench does the days of that line, and has done them since the third of the seed month, and is on the board because the woman is and not because she is.

"So the one thing nobody is going to ask her is whether the balance on the fourth line from the end is hers. Nobody. Not you, not me, not a woman from a fund, not a clerk out of Bell Stair, not a man from the hills, not me in nine months, not her mother asking her from a bed, and not the boy with the chalk.

"And I have not said that because she is sixteen. I have said it because a balance belongs to a ground and the ground is the woman's and the woman is a person and the girl is a person, and there is no place in this kingdom where those two get added together into one thing that can be asked, and the one place they get added together is on our own rule, and our own rule is not asked anything, and that is the whole of why it is dangerous."

---

Marek Vale stood four feet off the end of the bench in a seed house in Rainmarket and did not say anything for about four seconds, and what he was thinking about was not the girl.

He was thinking about a yard in Tallow Ward and a rope-maker of thirty-nine saying *the second line is what the person behind it said* into a sheet, and a man in a good coat not writing it down because the word wanted to be *household*, and a clerk of sixteen on a line because her mother was, and a house book with a line struck through it in a third ink.

He was also thinking that he had come down a main road to ask a woman of thirty-four a question about a form, and that the woman of thirty-four had got to the thing he was going to ask about before he opened his mouth, and had got there in about four sentences, and had not made him ask.

"I am not going to ask her," he said.

"I know you are not," said Mirren Fisk. "You have been in the doorway for four seconds and you have not looked at her once and that is the correct number of times to look at a person in a room you have come into late. Sit down. You have come off a boat."

---

He sat down on a bench that was not hers.

The girl filling cones did not look up, and did not stop, and nobody in that room adjusted anything on her account, and that is what a seed house is. It did not take any cleverness to arrive at. It took about nineteen years of a rule that nobody had to think about twice.

Fisk did not lower her voice, and that is the part nobody will ever notice, because in nine months somebody in this basin will write down that the keeper of the Elmsworth seed house spoke plainly in front of a girl of sixteen about a rate on her mother's ground, and that will be wrong, and the wrongness will be in the writing down rather than in the room.

She spoke at the ordinary volume, from the far end of the bench, with her back half turned, about eleven lines on a piece of sack, and she did not send anybody out, and she did not move the girl, and she did not say *she is young* and she did not say *when she is older*, and she did not look at the bench while she said any of it.

"Eleven lines and no head and no total," she said. "The same as the last time, and the same as every time, and I have said the words to you and you are going to want to put them somewhere and I am telling you now that you are not going to, and I would like it noticed that I have said that before."

"They are going to go on eleven grounds," she said, "in the fourth month, and I am going to walk to eleven kitchens and say a number out loud in a room, and I have never once done that off a piece of paper and I am not going to start in the ninth month. Four of them are on this road. One of them is the fourth line from the end."

---

It came at about the fifth bell and it was nine words long.

Petrik came off the ladder with the slate and the string and stopped at the end of the bench and said, to the middle of the room, in the way of an eleven-year-old with a job:

"Whose tray is the twenty-third for the last day."

And Mirren Fisk said, at the ordinary volume, without turning round and without looking at the bench: "The fourth line from the end. It is the same as it was yesterday. Put the slate down and bring me the twine."

And Petrik put the slate down.

And that was the whole of it, and it lasted about four seconds, and nothing in that room moved. The woman of thirty-four at the far end of the bench did not lower her voice when she answered, and did not look at the girl, and did not put her own body between the girl and the question, and the girl filling cones did not stop and did not look up, and nobody in the room turned round afterwards and looked at where she was sitting. If you had come into that building at the fifth bell on the ninth of the ninth month and asked any four people in it what had just happened, three of them would have told you about the twine.

---

There was sand on that floor and there is always sand on that floor, and the sand is out of the Share's own trays, because a tray that has stood under oiled paper in a shed that was shut up wet in the flood year brings a certain amount of that shed up with it for about nine weeks, and the girl with the broom sweeps it and it comes back under the bench and nobody in that building has ever given it a name and nobody is going to.

"You are not to sweep it at the end," said Mirren Fisk to the girl with the broom, at about the sixth bell. "You are to sweep it at the beginning. It comes back and it does not matter and I will not have anybody stood about counting it in the last hour of the day."

"That is what I do," said the girl.

"I know what you do," said Mirren Fisk. "I am saying it out loud, because a man in a wet coat has been looking at the floor for a quarter of an hour and I would rather he heard it from me than worked it out and asked."

---

At about the sixth bell the boy with the chalk came along the wall with the slate and read the day's count off it, and the girl filling cones said, without stopping and without raising her head:

"Twenty-two are the same ones as yesterday and the twenty-third wants turning at the fourth bell, not the third, or you get the middle of it wet."

Petrik looked at the board. "It says the fourth."

"It says the fourth for the first tray and it says the third for the rest of them and somebody has been copying it down since the second month," said Wenna Drel. "Turn the twenty-third at the fourth."

"That is not what the board says."

"That is what the twenty-third wants," said Wenna Drel, and went on filling cones, and Petrik turned the twenty-third at the fourth, and it was right, and that was the whole of what she said in that building that day, and nobody in the room thanked her for it and nobody thought about it again until the count at the seventh bell.

---

The count went on the bench book with nothing at the head of it, and the sixty-one went into the trays, and the lamp went on being the only lamp in a room that had been cold for six months, and the girl with the broom put the hazel against the wall and went, and Petrik put the chalk in his pocket and did not head the board.

The cap with the name in the band stayed on the bench where a girl of sixteen had left it, and its twin stayed on the nail behind it where a woman of forty-one had left it in the bad autumn, and in the fourth month a keeper of a seed house was going to walk up the low-ground road and say a number out loud in a kitchen about a rate on that ground, and she was going to be right to go herself, and the girl was not going to be in the kitchen, and that was going to be arranged and not discussed.

Marek Vale went out into the low-ground road at about the seventh bell with a bundle of sixty-one returned forms still in his coat, because he had not worked out what to do with them, and there was a woman at a tank in Tallow Ward who had known for four months that he was carrying a thing he could not put in any room, and she had never asked him about it and he had never told her, and both of those were correct, and he got about two hundred yards down the road before he understood that he had been doing the same thing for four months and had got further with it than with anything else he has ever done.
