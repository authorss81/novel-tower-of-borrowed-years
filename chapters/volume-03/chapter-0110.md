# Chapter 110 — A Cargo Going Down

The barge went down on the fifth of the second month at about the fourth bell, and the low ground's barley rate had fallen by about a third and had stopped falling, and the town had been eating since before the harvest month and had started to say so, and the granary offices of this basin were, in the ordinary language of a board of five somewhere, content.

Marek Vale was on the river stair because he had been on the weighbridge on the fourth of the tenth month and had stood on a stone deck and read a rate out loud to forty carts, and it had turned out that a man could do that, and that it was not a post and there was no form for it, and that if you did it twice in different places then about nine people would come and stand near you and ask you things.

So he was on the stair at the fourth bell with the rate written on the back of his hand under a count of two hundred and eleven, and he read it out to every boat that came up, and he read it out twice, and he said the number out loud because Sefa had asked him to and he had said he would and had not come back for three weeks.

The rate had stopped falling. It had been the same figure for four days.

"That is the first time since the ninth month that it has not gone down," said Sefa, "and I do not like it, and I will tell you why: a price that stops falling is a price somebody is holding. It has not been a price anybody has been happy with for a month. It is a price that is being kept there by a person who is fed."

"Is it?"

"I don't know," said Sefa. "I know I have four children and a stair and about nine boats a week instead of thirty, and I know that a man in a depot in the hills paid for the oats on this boat, and I know he is not doing it out of kindness. **If a price stops falling in a good year then somebody is carrying the difference and I would like to know who, because in this basin the answer is always a person who is not in the room and it is always the room's fault.**"

---

The boat was going down loaded, which was the thing nobody in Rainmarket had seen before the year turned.

There had been one loaded boat down on the nineteenth of the tenth month, when Rell came up, and there were four on the second of the second month, and there was one going at the fourth bell on the fifth, and it had about nine hundred sacks of barley in it and a coil of paper on the deck under a tarpaulin, lashed with a rope, in a box that was not lashed well.

Petar Duss was on the stair at about the third bell with a rope round his wrist, loading four carts for the four o'clock up-road, and he had a piece of paper in the cab of the lead one with a mark in the bottom corner of it in a calendar he cannot read, which had cost him four shillings in the growth month and had not been worth it, and he said so, and he said it at the third bell on a stair to about nine people and meant it.

Then the captain turned out to be a man of about thirty called Rennick, who had been on the stair eleven years and who had, in eleven years, never once had a certificate in a box on his deck.

"New," he said.

"It is new."

"Paper's new," said Rennick. "Grain's not. I've had grain going down for eleven years. I've never had paper. That box wants a lid and a nail and about four inches of pitch and whoever built it should be in a different trade."

"Who put it in there?"

"Depot," said Rennick. "Verrow. A man with a ledger came down on the second, asked for the box, brought the box, put it in, wrote a thing in the book, went up on the second again. Nice enough. Gave the boy a shilling for holding the rope. **He did not look at the paper once, and I did not ask him to, and that is how a crew stays a crew.**"

The tarpaulin came off about four feet where the coil was and the coil was a sheet rolled round twice and tied with string, and on the outside of the roll, where the string crossed it, there was a mark.

It was the smallest thing on the paper. It was four parts in a small hand: a month, a tide phase, a harbour seal, and a translator's name with a space before it and none after.

Marek Vale looked at it for about eleven seconds on a river stair in daylight with the water moving and about nine people on the stair, and then he put his hand in his pocket, and he said nothing at all, and there was nobody on that stair who could have read it if he had said what it was.

He had been up two flights of stairs in the rain on the twenty-first of the tenth month and had been told, by a man who makes a very good living out of a mark, that a mark decides where a paper is admitted and not whether a paper is good, and that the list at a harbour would say *yes* or *no* or *the name is not on the list*, and that it would not tell anybody anything about what the hand did on a particular day in a cellar four miles from a river.

He had not written to the harbour. He had a pound and no office and a list of instruments that nobody in this basin could produce, and he had been waiting since the twenty-first of the tenth month for a piece of somebody else's paper that he could not get into a room, and there had not been one.

The mark went down the river on a boat. It was going to Saltglass, where there is a list, and it was not coming back, and the sum it authenticated was in a ledger in the hills that a man of fifty-four was not permitted to produce.

---

The woman on the stair was forty-one and she had been on the river road since the third bell and she had a basket and a list and no money, in the arrangement that about nine households in this basin keep in a good month, which is that you buy in the first week of the month and you wait for the second.

Her name was Margit Sova and she cooked on the low ground and she had come down to find out whether the price would keep going, and she had been on the stair about twenty minutes when she got tired of listening to a man read a number out loud and asked the question, and she asked it to Marek because he was the one talking.

"Who is it," she said, "that says a thing is all right."

Marek turned round.

"That is not a thing anybody can answer," he said.

"You have been on this stair twenty minutes reading a number at people and you are the only one talking, so I am asking you."

"Yes," he said. "That is fair."

"Then answer it or tell me you cannot, and do not stand there being careful with me, because I have got a basket and about four days of flour in it and whatever you say I will be able to tell by the sixth."

Marek Vale stood on a river stair and worked for about four seconds and then said the true thing, in plain words, in the way you say a thing you have had in your mouth since the twenty-first of the tenth month and have not been able to put down.

"A person with a seal," he said. "Standing in a room. At a desk. On a day. Saying that a thing is as it is on a page. And that is the whole of it, and the whole of it is a person, and the person is always a person and never a district and never a class and never a number.

"And in this basin there are about nine of those and about four of the nine have never been to the ground the page is about, and one of them has said a number out loud in a book in the hills that nobody in Morrowfen is permitted to see, and he told me that on a stair on the nineteenth of the tenth month, before he signed anything, and I have not been able to put it down since."

Margit Sova looked at the barge.

"And that," she said, "is why the flour is nine and two and not six."

"That is not what I said."

"It is what you said with the other half of it," said Margit Sova. "You have not said it in that order because you are a man who is careful. **Somebody in a room is holding the price and the price is not moving and I do not care what the paper says. I care about the sixth.**"

---

Then she asked the second question, and she asked it the way people ask the second question, without any idea that it is the second one.

"And who says *the person with the seal* is the right person."

Marek did not have to think about it, and that was the part he could not account for afterwards.

"Nobody," he said. "**That is the whole of the thing and I have had it since the twenty-first of the tenth month and I could not get it out of my mouth, and it is not mine. A notary said it to me in two rooms above a chandler's shop and I have carried it down four terraces and up a cellar and it is the sentence and the sentence is that a signature does not say whether a thing is true. A signature says where a thing can go.**

"That is all. That is what the mark does. It does not say your flour is cheap. It says a piece of paper in a coil is admissible in a port in another kingdom, and it says that because a man at a desk pressed a bit of brass into some wax on a day, and a mark that names a month instead of a day can be put on any day inside that month, and there is one tide table in this basin and it is in two rooms above a chandler's shop and it belongs to a man who will not swear to his own hand."

Margit Sova stood on a river stair with a basket and did not say anything for about four seconds.

"That is the first time anybody has said it like that to me," she said, "and I have been in this basin thirty-one years and I have heard about four men in a room in my life and none of them ever got past *the price is the price*."

"I am not the man who worked it out."

"I know who did," said Margit Sova, "because you have just told me a notary said it and you have never once in twenty minutes used anybody else's name and you have not used his now. **You have told me a man you know and not his name, which is a thing a man does when he is about to be somewhere on the fifth and would rather be known as a reader of numbers.**"

The barge got its warp and went. It went down past the second stair and out under the bridge, and the paper went with it in a box that was not lashed well, and the rate stood where it had stood for four days, and the town went on eating.

---

Marek Vale came off the stair at the fifth bell and stood in the lane, and did the arithmetic of the next two days, which took him about nine seconds and which he had been doing all morning without noticing.

He had nine lots of sand out of thirty-nine in a yard four miles above a bridge, and a keeper of a roll of six lines who was going to die in the winter and had a key in his coat, and a class of people written nine words to a line, eleven times, in a column in a schedule in a depot in the hills, and a season works with nine lines and the ninth one empty and no form on which anybody may be applied for, and a drain on the second terrace that was going to go in the third month and take a terrace's worth of seed with it, and a culvert four feet too low on a road that a bridgewright could say something about for nothing.

He had a sentence, and it was his own, and he had said it once in a witness room on an island in the growth month and it was not for writing down, and he had not said it since.

He had a sheet of his own handwriting, in pencil, on the back of his left hand: a count of two hundred and eleven sheets of a form in a cellar under a chandler's yard, and underneath it four days of a rate that had stopped falling. Those were the only two figures in this basin that belonged to him and neither of them was about anybody's future.

And his brother was going to be on that road in the morning with a bag of tools and a bill and no plan, and had told him, in a yard, in the cold, that he was not going to say the sentence there and that he was going to stand next to him while he said it and would not look at him while he said it.

He slept for about four hours, badly, in a rented room above a lockkeeper's shop in Tallow Ward, and got up at the third bell, and did not write to the harbour, because a letter costs a pound and takes six weeks and he had a morning of road and one sentence and a promise to stand next to somebody who would not look at him.

At the fourth bell the register opened in a shed eleven feet by nine, with about eighty people in the lane and a germination bench against the wall on the days the rota said so, and Teodor Lask read somebody's second line out loud twice at the ordinary speed, and the fifth line of the form was empty and the sixth line was empty and always would be, and a column on the wall list had nothing at the head of it, and a man with no office read out the fourth line, which was empty, twice, at the ordinary speed.

Then he took his coat off the nail, and put it on, and went up the road.
