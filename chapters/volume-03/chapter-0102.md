# Chapter 102 — Eleven Switchbacks

The road up to the Red Uplands has eleven switchbacks in it and takes a day, and it is built for carts, and Marek went up it on foot with a bag and a coat and no authority of any kind, and the four miles of it above the stone bridge are the part everybody complains about.

He left at the first bell and came into the top terrace at about the fourth bell of the next day, having stopped twice, once for water and once because a man he had met once was driving eleven ewes down in the wrong weather and stood in the middle of the road with an arm out until he had got past, which took a quarter of an hour and cost the ewes a quarter of an hour and neither of the two parties mentioned it afterwards.

Neris Kade was on the top terrace at the sixth bell with a germination bench out of a door and had been there since the fourth, and she had seen him come off the last switchback from about two hundred yards, and she did not come down.

"I know," she said, when he got to the bench. "I know who you are. I am not being pleasant, I have not got the time. Sit on the crate if you want to stand there."

There are four terraces and the fourth is the top and it is called Hartlow, and it is four miles above the stone bridge, and a cooperative's roll there has six lines on it: four of them women over fifty, one of them a child, and the sixth line carries a season examiner's name, and the examiner is not a member and does not live there.

"Thirty-nine lots on the second terrace," said Kade. "Nine of them are sand. You have heard that already, so I am not going to say it again slowly, and I am not going to say it for the fourth bell in six days to a man who is standing on a crate."

"I heard it at the stone bridge," said Marek. "From a haulier, and he heard it from a man with a lantern at the top of the road, and neither of them has any idea whether it is true."

"It is true. I have been up here since the fourth of the ninth month and I have put my own hand in nine trays of sand and I have counted them twice and they are the same nine."

Marek sat on the crate.

---

He had come up with a sentence. It was his own, and he had said it once, in a witness room on an island on the twentieth of the growth month, to a girl of sixteen who had asked him what a counterweight was called, and he had said he would say it once and it was not for writing down, and he had not said it since, and there was no form in this kingdom on which a sentence may be written down.

*A counterweight is a thing somebody is holding up. If the name is a person, the person is in the room, and being named is the only way of being in the room.*

It had got him eleven switchbacks and four miles above a stone bridge and it had got him nowhere yet, and the reason it had got him here was that in the ninth month of MR 413 a woman in the same room had written a different sentence on the fourth line of a wall in a shed in Tallow Ward, in her own hand, and had asked for it to be read out twice at the ordinary speed, and it was on the wall, and it said that a class is not a person and never has been one and cannot be asked what it will bear.

He got about four words into it.

"You have brought a good sentence to a bad morning," said Neris Kade, without looking up from the bench, "and I am going to tell you what happens if you say it to anybody on this terrace, and then you can decide what to do with the rest of your day.

"You say it to the woman standing behind me and she is going to agree with you. And she is going to agree with you because it is true and because you have said it well and because she is forty-one and has been up at four for eleven days. And then she is going to go and tell her sister on the second terrace, and her sister is going to tell the man who holds the season works, and he is going to write it on the works, and in about a month there is going to be a piece of paper in this valley with your sentence on it and no name at the head of it, and it will be in a book in a room in Bell Stair in nine months with somebody else's handwriting under it, and in two years a man in a good coat is going to read it and say *they said so themselves, in the valley, at the time.*"

She put a tray down on the bench and moved on to the next one.

"None of that would be a lie," she said. "All of it would be true. That is what I have learned about sentences that are true."

---

Janko Vrabec came out of the low door at about the seventh bell with a lamp in his hand that had a very small wick in it, and he was fifty-eight, and he was the keeper of the roll, and he had been told in the growth month of MR 413 that he was going to die this winter, and he had told exactly four people and one of them was a physician who could not help him and one of them was Marek, who was standing on a terrace holding nothing.

"Marek Vale," said Janko Vrabec. "You were in a shed in Tallow Ward in the ninth month and you read out a sentence of a woman's about a thing nobody had heard of, and I heard about it from Ruta, who heard it from the yard, and I have come out to say two things and the second one is the one I want.

"The first is that you are welcome and there is a crate and there is a bench and there is no work for a man in a coat, and if you have come to see nine trays of sand you have come the right way and you should not be asked for anything for looking.

"And the second is that I am not going to be the thing you came up here about." He said it in an entirely ordinary voice, with the lamp in his hand, and then he sat down on the step. "I have been told I am going to die this winter. That is a thing between me and a physician and it is a thing that happened in the growth month, and if you take it up and carry it down a road in a bag then you will have taken the only thing in this valley that is mine from me, because nobody else is going to have it. So I would like it said out loud, once, to my face, by the man it is going to be said about, and then I would like it left where it is."

Marek stood in the yard of a seed house with a bag at his feet and had no answer ready, and had four, and used none of them.

"You have said it to my face," he said. "I will leave it where it is. And I am not going to ask you anything about it, and if anybody else asks you about it I will say you told me first and gave the date."

Janko Vrabec looked at him for a while.

"That is the first thing anybody has said to me about it that was worth hearing," he said, "and it is not worth very much, and I would like you to go and look at the third terrace now while the light is on it, and then come back and eat, because Ruta has made more than there is going to be."

---

The second terrace is a day's walk down and a half day's walk back and Marek went down it and up it in one day, and the third terrace is below that, and what he saw on the third terrace was the thing that the whole of the tenth month of MR 413 turned on.

The third terrace has stopped reporting.

Not in any dramatic way. There is no hole in it and no family gone and nobody has been taken. **There are four people living on the third terrace and there were seven in the ninth month of MR 412, and two of the seven are in the town's second house and one is in the third house and one is dead, and none of that is this year's business.** The report is a folded sheet of paper with a figure on it that goes to the office in the hills, once, in the second month, and the third terrace has sent it every year for nineteen years, and this year it has not been sent, and there is no form in this kingdom in which anybody may be sent to ask why.

Nobody had sent anybody. That was the finding, and Marek stood on the third terrace at about the third bell of the ninth day and worked it out in about four minutes, standing up, in the cold, with nothing to write on.

There is a list in this basin called the season works, and it is not an office list and it belongs to no office. **The four terraces make it themselves, in the second month, out of what they can see from their own doors: nine lines. Nine is the whole of it.** Cutting is one. A drain is two. The shared threshing floor is three. A road is four. A water is five. Two store rooms are six and seven. A seed store is eight, and the ninth is the fourth-terrace store, which is the biggest and the only one with a key.

And the ninth line had a keyholder's name on it, because the ninth line had always had a keyholder's name on it, and the name was Janko Vrabec's, and he had held the fourth-terrace store for eleven years, and the store was full of the four terraces' kept-back seed and it was the only seed in the valley that would still be there in the eleventh month.

"I have not taken the key off the nail," Janko Vrabec said, when Marek asked him about it that night. "That is the thing to understand. I have not taken the key off the nail, because the day I take it off the nail the store is shut, and the store is not shut yet and it will not be shut while I am stood up.

"But I have told my son about it, and he is nineteen, and he is a person, and he is not a member of the roll because the roll is a roll of the cooperative and he is a man and there is no form for him either. And I have left the ninth line of the works ruled this year and I have not written anything in it, because if I write my own name in it and I am dead in the eleventh month then in the twelfth month somebody has to strike a man's name off a list, and there is no box for that, and the whole of that valley knows what a strike is and none of them will use the word in front of me.

"So there are nine lines on it and the ninth is empty and I have been the ninth line for eleven years, and I am not going to be a hole in a list, I am going to be an empty line with a thing that is due to stop, and there is nobody in this basin that can come and ask me about it, and I would like it written down by somebody who can write and I would like the person who is able to ask me to be somebody who eats at my table."

Marek could not write it down. That was the shape of the whole of the year, and it was the shape of that evening, and it was the shape of the morning he was going to have on a river road with a piece of paper in a coil.

So he asked the question the yard had taught him to ask, which was the only question he had, and he asked it in the words the person used, which cost him about a minute of standing in a cold yard being misunderstood.

"What were you told, and in whose words," he said. "About the season. Not about the store. About the sowing."

Janko Vrabec looked at the bench, and at the nine trays of sand, and at the road coming down off the top terrace, and for about nine seconds he did not answer, and the reason he did not answer was that he was deciding whether a man in a coat was going to use it.

"The rain came in the wrong week," he said. "I am going to say it that way because it is the plain way and I have not got a better one. It came six weeks before I was looking for it, and it was warm when it came, and it was a great deal of it, and the sowing went in on ground that had been warm for nine days, and then the cold came and the cold is the ordinary cold of that time of year and it is not anybody's fault and it is not a flood and it is not a storm and there is no name in this valley for what has happened to nine trays of ground.

"We asked for the sowing to come early. Do you understand that? We asked for it. The fourth terrace asked for it in the second month of MR 412 and it was sealed in the hills in about nine days, and the reason is on a paper you could read in a morning, and the reason is that the low ground's season was in the wrong order and that we could not get a week's difference out of the weather, and a sowing put in a fortnight late on the low ground is a sowing put in a fortnight late here as well, because we are one road and one weather and I have thirty-one years of it. And the sowing was done in the second month of MR 413, ten months after the paper came back, because that is when the ground was ready and not one day before."

"And I put my name to that. I did not have to. I am not a keeper of anything in this valley that anybody had to ask me about. I went to the hills and I put my name to a paper about a week of weather and I was asked for a reason and I gave a reason, and the reason was true, and the paper came back sealed and it worked.

"And the rain came in the warm week, and the low ground had the season it asked for, and we had a sowing on ground that had been warm for nine days and then we had the ordinary cold of the month, and **that is the whole of the season and there is nobody in it to be angry with. I asked for it. It arrived. I have been up a road and I have got nine trays of sand out of thirty-nine and I would do it again this year for the same reason and I would rather it had gone to somebody who was going to be able to use it.**"

The valley was very quiet in the way that four terraces are quiet at that hour.

"I am not a villain in this," said Janko Vrabec. "I want that said in whatever room you take it to, in my words, and I want it said early, and I am not going to be angry with the man who says it. I am a keeper of a roll of six lines and I have signed a piece of paper about a week of weather, and I have had a bad sowing, and I am going to die in the winter. Those are four facts and they are all in this yard and they do not need anybody to hold them up."

---

Marek walked back up to the top terrace in the dark and did not get there until the second bell, and Neris Kade was still at the bench with a lamp in front of her, and she had not gone in, and there was a room off the seed room with a notice nailed to the outside of the door that had been up fourteen months and that four people in this basin had read, and it said that anybody might come in and sit and look, and that nothing was charged and nothing was signed.

She had been in that room. He had sent nothing up the road and it had not been sent; it was on a wall in a shed in about four weeks' time, in her own hand, on the fourth line, and she had read it herself off a board in a room full of strangers, and she had asked for nothing and she was not going to.

"You went to the third terrace," she said.

"I went to the third terrace."

"And you came back and you did not say anything to me about it, which is new, and I am not going to thank you for it, and I would like to know what you are going to do now, because you have been up a road for two days and you have a bag and you have said about nine words that were not about weather."

"I am going to go down in three or four days and find out who cleared the road for the Crown March paper," said Marek.

"That is a man going to look for a room," said Neris Kade. "That is what you are. You are a man who goes looking for the room where the sentence was written. And I will tell you what will happen, and I have watched it happen twice.

"You will find a building. And in the building there will be a man who is going to be helpful to you, and he will be helpful because being helpful costs him nothing and because a man in a good coat who asks careful questions is the only kind of visitor a building like that ever gets. And he will tell you a form. And the form will be printed, and the form will be correct, and the form will have been printed before the four terraces had a bad year, and **you will go up that road again with a sentence about a form, and there will be one more season between the sentence and anybody's dinner, and next autumn I will be standing on this terrace counting trays again and it will be a worse number.**"

She put her hand flat on the bench, and it was the same gesture and it was the same reason as in a shed in Rainmarket about four weeks earlier, and neither of them mentioned that.

"Do not go looking for the room," she said. "There is no room. There is a form and a man and a road, and the road is open and the form is correct and the rain came in the week it was asked for, and the whole of what you are about is going to be a sentence, and I have read one of yours in a shed and it was the best sentence anybody has said to me in nine years and it did not come up here with you, did it."

"No," said Marek. "It did not come up here with me."

"Then do not say it here," said Neris Kade.

She went into the seed room.

Marek stood in the yard for about ten minutes with the bag at his feet and then he went and got it and put it on his shoulder, and on the way past the door of the seed room he stopped, because Janko Vrabec had put a lamp on the step and was sitting on the step in the dark with it beside him and was not going to bed, and had not been going to bed since the sixth of the ninth month, and the lamp was the only light in that yard and the seed store key was in the coat on the back of the hook behind that door.

"Sit down," said Janko Vrabec. "You are going to say the sentence in the wrong week and you are going to be right, and I have been a keeper for thirty-one years and I know what a right thing in the wrong week costs. Sit down and let an old man tell you what the room is.

"The room is the fourth terrace. There is no room. It is four lines of figures on a page in a book in a house on a main road, and whoever comes down that road will say the yes, and then they will say the order about the drainage, and then they will go, and they will not come back up here until there is a second harvest, and there is not going to be a second harvest on my ground.

"So there is no room and you were right to come up here first. And I am going to sit up in this yard with a lamp until the cold gets into me, and that is not being clever and it is not a speech. **I am the ninth line of a list of nine, and I am going to sit up in my own yard, and if I am not stood up in the eleventh month then somebody can come up this road and ask me whether I was right, and if I am stood up then nobody can come, and that is the whole of what a dying man can do with a form.**"

Marek sat down on the step for about twenty minutes and said nothing at all, which was the only thing anybody on the top terrace of the Red Uplands could have done with that.
