# Chapter 109 — A Brother, a Slate, and a Kitchen

The reckoning turned on the first of the second month and nothing in this basin marked it except a boy of sixteen who had carried the boards to the roof of the Tallow Gate yard in the rain for four days and had been paid, and a girl of nine who had asked, at the tail-board of a bridge span, what a date was for, and had been told that a date is for afterwards, and who had written on a slate, in a child's hand, *afterwards is us, so tell them the true day.*

Marek Vale walked out to his brother's house on the north lane on the evening of the second of the second month with a coat on and something in his mind that he had been building for nine days, and it fell apart in the first four minutes in a kitchen, in the way that a prepared thing does.

Tomas was at the table with his sleeves rolled and a lamp pulled close, doing the thing he does in the off-season, which is to take a joint apart on a board and put it back together slightly differently so that his hands are not idle. Bohum Ostrek, who is on that span and is not in this kitchen, lost two weeks of the middle of his hand at a counter-desk in the rains month under a clause nobody has ever questioned, and that is gone too and is not coming back. There is a hook on the wall where Tomas's own plane used to hang, and it has been empty for two years, and neither of them mentions either of those things and that is the arrangement in this house.

Tamsin was at the other end of the table with a coat of Marek's out of a bag and a needle, and she said good evening, and asked whether he had eaten, and he said he had, which was a lie of about nine hours' standing, and she let it go, and nobody in that kitchen said one word about a book.

"So," said Tomas Vale.

---

"I want to talk to you about something and I have thought about how to say it for nine days," said Marek.

"Then say it."

"I do not want you to go up the upland road."

Tamsin put the needle down. Tomas did not move at all, and did not look up, and after about four seconds he made a small sound that was not a word.

"Nine days," he said. "You have thought about it for nine days and what you have arrived at is a request, and you have come to my kitchen to make it, and you have brought your face."

"I have come to your kitchen because there is nowhere else in this city where I can say this to you without it being a thing that gets written down somewhere within a week," said Marek. "So let us have that on the table and then let us do it."

"Say the rest of it."

"Sabin Rell. Crown March granary. He came down the river on the nineteenth of the tenth month and he certified an underwriting on the four terraces on the twenty-fourth, and there is a clause in it, and I have been up the road twice and I have read the schedule and I know what is in the fourth column, and I know the answer, and I am telling you now because I think you are going to hear about it in a month in a yard and I would rather you heard it from me at this table."

"I have heard about it," said Tomas. "Sefa came down the road on the twenty-ninth with a cart and told Janko Vrabec's boy and the boy told the seed house and the seed house told me, because that is how things work in this basin. I have known since the twenty-ninth of the tenth month. I have had a month to think about it and I have thought about it in the only way available to a man who does not go up roads, which is not at all, so you may as well have it in one piece."

Marek sat down.

"And I am not going to ask you to go up the road," said Tomas, "because I have not said a word against your going up the road, and you have come here to stop me going up the road, and you are eleven years late, and there is a reason and the reason is that you are frightened of something and you have decided that the way to be frightened safely is to arrange for your brother not to be in the room."

---

The kitchen was quiet for a bit. The lamp hissed.

"Say what frightened," said Tomas.

Marek had four sentences ready. He had had them ready on the road and he had had them ready on the stair and he had had them ready for nine days, and every one of them was about the volume of work, and about a man being owed, and about there being no need.

He said the first one and then stopped, because it was a lie about a reason, and Tomas Vale had been a bridgewright for twenty years and had never once in his life been given a reason that was not the reason.

"There is a thing on the schedule in the fourth column," he said, "and the fourth column is a class of people, and a class of people is not a person, and that is why nobody can be asked, and I have a sentence about it and I have had it since the summer. And if I say it in a room on that road, then in three weeks a Crown March granary official will be in a room with the sentence in it, and he will say *go and find the room that signed the paper and not the room that is keeping the number*, and he will be right, and it will be the whole of the defence, and there will be nothing afterwards.

"And that is what I am frightened of. Not the road. **That I will say the true thing in the wrong week, and it will be true, and it will be the end of it.**"

Tomas put the joint down.

"Say it there," he said.

"That is what I—"

"You have been up there twice. You have been in a cellar and on a river stair and in a room over a shop and you have had a sentence in your pocket for nine days and you have not said it, and I want to know why, and I think you know why and I think it is that you have worked out that saying it is a thing you *do*, and that a thing you do, once, about somebody else's paper, makes you a man who does things about other people's paper, and then in about a year there will be a road with a drain on it and a man in a good coat up there with your name at the bottom of nothing, and somebody will say *he reads it out in a shed*, and that will be the whole of what anybody knows about you."

"Say the next part," said Marek.

"There is no next part. **That was the next part.** I have been in this basin thirty-four years and I have watched about nine of your sort and I can tell you what happens to all nine of them and it is the same thing and it is that they end up being the man who goes and says a true thing, and that is not a man anybody can help, and I have watched you carry a piece of Crown March paper up a hill in the inside of a coat and get thanked for nothing and say nothing about it, and I do not want to be the reason you stopped doing it, and I do not want to be the reason you did it either."

Marek did not answer that. Tamsin picked the needle up again, took a stitch, and put the coat down, and said: "There is bread in the cold box and eat it before you go up that road, because you never do."

---

The girl came in at about the seventh bell with the slate under her arm.

Lio Vale was nine and had been at the Tallow Gate in the rain carrying boards and had been paid, and she had heard most of this and had not come in for it, and she came in for the slate.

"What is a Crown March," she said.

"That is a region," said Marek.

"No, it is not that," said Lio, "because you said it the way you say *a thing a person is*, and you have said the word *a man* nine times in this kitchen and you have not said *a person* once, and I have got a slate here and I am going to put it on and you are going to tell me what it is and then I am going to go to bed."

Marek looked at the slate and did not know what to do with his hands.

"A Crown March is where the offices are," he said. "The offices that keep the accounts for the whole region, and the money for the roads, and the grain, and the seals. It is not a person. It is a place where people are."

"Which people."

"Clerks, mostly. And men who decide things."

"And when you said *it is not fair to Tomas*," said Lio, "was that about a person, or about a place where people are, or about a name at the bottom of a piece of paper that you cannot get into a room." She waited. "You did not answer my question on the tail-board either. You said the date is for afterwards. That was the second time somebody has said a true thing and then gone inside."

---

Tomas put his hand flat on the table.

"All right," he said. "I am going up the road on the fifth and I am going as a bridgewright and not as your brother, and I want you to hear the reason, and then I am going to bed and you can say what you like.

"The season works is nine lines and one of them is empty and I have seen a copy of it — Sefa had it, the boy copied it out for me, which he did not have to do. Line two is a drain on the second terrace and it has been holding a wall since the flood year and it is going to go in the third month and take a terrace's worth of seed with it when it goes. Line four is a road, and I have looked at the road in a book in a seed house and I can tell you from the description that the culvert is wrong and that whoever built it put the mouth four feet too low, and I would have said so for nothing, in a yard, for nothing, when I was twenty-two.

"So: I am going up there on the fifth, and I am going to say *this drain, this road, and I will do it for a bill and the bill goes on the works and the works is nine lines and the ninth one is empty*, and I am going to be right about the drain and I am going to be right about the road, and if they want a bridgewright they will have one.

"And there is not one word in that about your sentence, and there is not one word in that about a class of people, and there is not one word in that about a Crown March granary, and I want it understood that **I am not going up that road for you and I am not going up that road against you and I have not got a plan.** I have a drain and a road and I have been on this road thirty-four years and I do not need a plan for a drain."

"And Marek can come," said Lio, from the doorway, "if he is going anyway and if he does not talk on the road."

Marek said that was fair.

---

Tomas walked out into the yard at about the eighth bell and stood for a minute in the cold, and Marek followed him out and stood four feet off, which is a distance this family had used in a kitchen and in a corridor and on a bridge span for eleven years.

"You will not say it there," said Tomas.

"I will say it there."

"You will say it badly and late and at the wrong end of a meeting, because that is what you do, and it will still be better than nothing, and I will stand next to you when you do it and I will not look at you while you do it." He turned round. "And Marek. **I want to be told when I am being used.** Not a month afterwards. Not by a clerk in Bell Stair with a record in his hand. In the room. Before.

"Nobody has told me in eleven years. Not one time. And the whole of what has happened to us since the flood year is that men in rooms have decided which of my losses was worth writing down, and I have found out about four of them about a year after each, and the one I found out about soonest was the one you carried up a hill in your coat and asked nothing for, and I would have gone up that hill myself in an hour if you had handed it to me in the yard."

He went in. The door was not shut hard.

Marek Vale stood in his brother's yard on the north lane for about four minutes with a sentence in his pocket and a slate on the table inside and eleven years of being told things late, and he worked out — in about nine seconds, standing in the cold, the way he worked out most things — that his brother was not asking him to be a different man.

He was asking to be told first. In the room. Before.

And that there was no form in this kingdom in which a person may be told anything first, and that the register in a shed in Tallow Ward was the only place he had ever found where a man says a thing out loud in a room where it is written down, and that he had a duty there four hours a day, and that nobody had ever told him it was a duty to anybody else as well.

He went up the north lane at about the ninth bell with his hands in his pockets, and the fifth was three days off, and the register opened at the fourth bell.
