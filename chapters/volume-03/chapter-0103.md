# Chapter 103 — Sixty-One Trays

Hana Belic has worked sixty-one trays twice a day since the sixth of the ninth month, in the dark, by feel, in a shed with one window and a bench and a lamp that is not coming until the third month.

She is thirty-four. She is a member of the Elmsworth Seed Share in her own right and not through anybody, and she draws the eleventh tray, and the eleventh tray is one of the eleven that came up as sand, and she has known that since the ninth of the ninth month and has said it out loud to four people, one of them a physician, who wrote her out of four tray-days and did not write her out of the other seven, and one of them a reader in a shed, who wrote it down as it was said and could not do one single thing with it.

The seed house's lamp is the thirteenth of fourteen lines on a list in a building in Bell Stair. It is supplied in the third month and not before. The fourteenth line is a school off the north lane with about ninety-one children on its roll and about fourteen places, and nobody has ever sent anybody about either of them, because a cut is not a date and the lines fall when somebody sends somebody and this year nobody has sent anybody.

At the sixth bell on the twelfth of the tenth month, Mirren Fisk put both hands flat on her own bench and said the sentence she had been getting ready for since the sixth of the ninth month.

"I am going to move the germination bench into the Gate shed."

---

The Gate shed is eleven feet by nine and it is built for a seed bin, and since the eighteenth of the ninth month it has been a register of risk, and it is open four hours a day, and there is a queue in the lane outside it on most mornings that goes past the end of the building.

There is not room in eleven feet by nine for a germination bench and two trestles and about twenty-two people.

That is the whole of the arithmetic and both women knew it before anybody said it, and the argument that followed took about nine minutes in a shed yard with a tank in it and about forty people in it, and every single person in that argument was right.

"The bench comes first," said Mirren Fisk. "I have been a keeper for nineteen years and I have lost eleven trays off a bench that needed a lamp for thirty-seven days, and I am not going to lose a season off a bench to keep a room in a yard open. I said last year and I will say it now and I will say it in front of my own members: the bench comes first, and if somebody will not have the bench in that shed then the bench is in my shed and the shed is dark."

"And the register is a practice," said Aldis Kern, from the step, "and a practice with a room in it is a practice, and what it is not is a bench. That shed is a shed, and four hours a day is four hours a day, and if the bench is in it then it is a shed with a bench in it four hours a day and a queue outside it in the wrong place, and in about six weeks a man in a good coat is going to stand in that lane and point at the bench and say *this is a cooperative's work in a ward's room.* And he will be right. And the ward will not be able to say a word against it, because he will be right, and because the yard agreed in the seed month that the register is not a schedule and a schedule is a thing that has to be something somebody agreed to."

"I have been in your yard for about three weeks reading out a woman's sentence off a wall," said Fisk. "I have read it twice at the ordinary speed and I have not improved a word. I am not going to be told by a yard captain that the yard's rules do not reach into a shed with a bench in it. **The lamp on the water office's list is the thirteenth of fourteen and the fourteenth is a school and neither of them has been sent anybody and I have watched thirty-seven days of this season go out of a window.**"

"You have," said Kern, "and it is the worst thing that has happened in this basin this year and I have not said so out loud because I have been up a road. Thirty-seven days. Six of my own people are on that bench and one of them has a cough in the small of her back from it, and I am not going to pretend to a woman that her bench is a small thing. I am going to tell you the three reasons and then I am going to vote and then I am going to go and stand somewhere else."

He held up three fingers and did not use them.

"One. The bench is dark and that is the water office and it is on a list and nobody is sending anybody, and if I stand in this yard this morning and say the ward will carry a bench, then in nine months there is a ward that has promised a bench, and in eighteen months there is a ward that has promised two, and I have read the history of this city and it does not stop at two.

"Two. If the bench is in that shed, then the fourth line on your form is being filled in a room a quarter of a mile from the ground the rate is charged to, and I do not care how honest everybody in this yard is. I have watched this basin for nineteen years and I have learned one thing and it is the only thing I am qualified to say, and it is that **honesty in a room is cheap and distance is not.** Anything can be said in a shed. The trouble starts about a quarter of a mile away.

"Three. And then he stopped counting and said it flat, and he said it to Mirren Fisk and not to the yard. "And three is that you are the keeper of a seed house and you are not a ward and you cannot become a ward by being in a room, and if you are in that shed for six hours a day then the shed has a keeper in it, and a shed with a keeper in it is a room, and a room with a keeper in it is a place a man can come and ask what a thing is, and then in four years the whole of that is a thing that belongs to the yard and it will have started with me saying yes to you this morning, and I have said no to better offers than this in a worse month."

The yard voted, and the yard voted quickly, and the vote was eleven for the shed and about thirty against it and nobody counted out loud, and the yard said the count was not a count and had said for four months that a figure about a room is a number of people who agreed with something.

---

Then Marek Vale said the thing he had been carrying up and down a road for nine days, and he said it badly, and it was the only useful thing he said all morning.

"The fifth line," he said.

Nobody knew what he meant.

"The form has six ruled lines. Two is what the person behind it said, in their words. Four is the counterweight and the number of people who will bear it. **Five is the hours, and five has been empty since the eighteenth of the ninth month, and it is empty because the hours are nobody's paper.** And the hours are nobody's paper because nobody is the person who works them. Every hour of that register is worked by a person. Teodor Lask reads for four hours a day and there is no form in this kingdom that will take his four hours, and it is not his fault, it is a fifth line.

"And this morning I have been in a yard and a woman has stood in front of me and told me that she has worked one hundred and twenty-two tray-hours a day for thirty-seven days in the dark, and that is a person's hours, and that is the fifth line. It is the only hours in this basin I have ever heard anybody say out loud."

The yard went quiet in the way that about forty people in a yard go quiet.

"And I am not able to enter it," said Marek. "I want that said before anybody says anything else. I have no office and the form has no box for me and I cannot enter one word of that wall, and if I could I would not, because a line may be entered by the person behind it and about their own paper, and the bench is her paper and not mine and I have been up a road for nine days with a sentence in a bag that was not mine either."

"Then it is not yours to give away," said Fisk. "I have a keeper's name on a bench. I can enter a line about my own paper. Go and get Sedge and ask him whether the fifth line will take a figure and whether a figure can be a tray and not an hour, and then I will say it in that shed myself and I will not have it entered by a man who cannot enter anything."

"Bessok," said Kern, "go and get the clerk."

---

Sedge came down the hill in the rain with the form under his arm, because there was one form in this basin and it was the one he had cut, and he is a third-grade clerk with no service length anywhere and he said the sentence he has said before, more than once, and he said it again on the step of a tank in a yard in the rain.

"A form has to be cut by a man who does not know whether it is right. I have not been paid for this and I have not been asked and I would like it noticed that I came."

He looked at the fifth line for a while.

"It will take a figure," he said. "It is ruled and it is not a box and it is the hours, and a figure will go on it. It is a line and any line takes a figure and a figure does not have to be an hour. You could put a tray on it. You could put a day. You could put a person, and it would be the wrong word, and it would be a wrong word that somebody could read a year from now with no way of knowing that it was a wrong word, and I have seen four hundred forms in this city with a wrong word on the top of them and not one of them had anything to do with anybody's intentions."

"Then we do not do it," said Kern, from the step, and he did not use three fingers this time.

"We do not do it," said Kern, "and here is the reason and it is ours, and it is the reason we gave at this tank on the twentieth of the rains month, and Mirren, you will not like it, because it is your bench.

"**A round that repeats is the third box on the settlement desk's form.** A levy is a figure a body puts on itself in a room on a day in the words of the body, and the reason the yard refused the Compact's levy in the seed month is that a levy which repeats is a schedule, and a schedule does not have to be anything anybody agreed to. And if a hundred and twenty-two tray-hours a day go on the fifth line of a board in a shed in this yard, then next year they will go on the fifth line, and the year after, and the day a figure appears on a board the same way twice, that board is a roll. And this shed stops being a room where a person may ask what a piece of paper is, and becomes a place where the hours of a cooperative are kept, which is a different thing, and it is a thing a man in a good coat can produce at anybody in nine months.

"We would have fixed the empty fifth line," he said, "and we would have turned a yard's register into the ward's wage book, and it would have been the best-documented hour in this basin and it would have been the end of the thing that makes it worth anything."

Fisk stood in the rain at the step of a tank and did not say anything for about twenty seconds, and then she said the sentence that made it all worth the morning, and she said it to Kern and not to Marek.

"All right," she said. "Then where does it go."

---

The answer, arrived at by about forty people on a wet morning in about an hour and a half, and not arrived at by any of them alone, was this.

The bench goes into the Gate shed. It goes in from the fourth bell to the sixth on the days a rota says, and it comes out again before the register shuts, and the rota is a sheet of oiled board on the wall of the seed house, and it says which day Hana Belic's trays are in the shed and which day Wenna Drel's are and which day the two trays that are not anybody's work are in there, because the two trays that are not anybody's work are the two that came up and nobody wanted them.

The bench is in the shed for two hours of a four-hour day, and the lane is taken while it is, and there is no water at the Ropewalk standpipe on those mornings, and one of the two women who carries the yard's water has started coming down at the second bell to get it up before the queue forms, which is a solution that puts the whole cost of the queue on one woman's back, and everybody in the yard knows it, and nobody has found the other one.

And on the rota there is nothing at the head of it.

Not the day. Not the season. Not the Share's name, because the Share's name is at the head of the bench book and the bench book is a different book in a different shed. Not a keeper's name, because Fisk is a member and there are sixty-one members and which one of the sixty-one is on the rota is a question the yard has no standing to ask and the Share has no form to ask it with.

Hana Belic pointed at it herself. She had been in the yard from the fourth bell with a box of the eleven sand trays on her knees, and she had stood at the back through the entire argument and had said nothing, and at the end she came to the front and put her finger on the top of the rota.

"There's nothing on it," she said. "It just goes — day, day, day. And the day is a day somebody else picked, and the days are the days the shed is open, and so the rota is not ours, it is the shed's. **We have built a rota that belongs to a shed.**"

Kern looked at it for a while.

"So we have," he said.

"And I'm not asking for it to be named," said Hana Belic, "because I have read the thing on your nail and I have worked sixty-one trays twice a day in the dark for thirty-seven days and I have not got the breath for a column. **Leave it bare. Put the days on. I will come in when the rota says I am to and I will go out at the sixth bell and I will not be thanked for it, because the day it is on a board with a name at the top of it, somebody is going to work out that the trays are a resource and the days are mine.**"

Kern said that he would like it noticed that the fourth voice of the morning had come from a woman with a box of sand on her knees, and that nobody was going to be told what it was about.

---

They carried the bench up the lane at about the first bell the next morning, in the rain, in eleven pieces, with about nine people, and it went in and it came out at the sixth bell and it went in again the next day, and on the fourth morning there were about eighty people in the lane and the bench was in the shed and the two trestles were against the wall and a reader of nineteen was reading somebody's second line out loud standing up, with his back to a germination bench, and a woman of thirty-four was standing in the corner with her hands in a bench frame waiting for the fourth bell to pass.

Then the reader stopped in the middle of a second line, because the bench frame was in the way and he could not see the board, and he said so out loud, and about nine people laughed, and the yard laughed, and the woman of thirty-four said she would hold it higher from tomorrow and be quieter about it, and nobody thanked her for that either, and the register ran forty minutes over on that morning because a bench had turned up in a shed built for a seed bin.

The rota on the seed house wall had days on it and nothing above them, and the fifth line of the form was empty, and the sixth line was empty, and the column at the right-hand end of the wall list had nothing at the head of it and was not going to get anything, and the lamp on the water office's list was the thirteenth of fourteen and was coming in the third month and not before, and Hana Belic went home at the sixth bell down a lane with no water in the standpipe and had not been paid for one hour of the day and had not asked to be.
