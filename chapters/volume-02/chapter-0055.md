# Chapter 55 — The Kitchen With the Second-Hand Plough

The house is one room and a lean-to on the low ground, four hundred yards off the road, and there is no gate because a gate on the low ground is a thing you build when you have stopped expecting the water.

Marek got there at the quarter after the second bell, in the last of the light, because Neris Kade had said there would be an asking in that kitchen tonight and had not said whose, and because he had spent the sixth at a desk working out that he could not stop, and because a man who cannot stop doing something is at least entitled to find out what it is.

There was a lamp in the room. There was a woman on a barrel with a ledger open on her knee and a pen in her hand and a good coat over a working woman's sleeve, and there was a man of about sixty standing in front of her with his hands behind his back, the way a man stands when he has decided to sign and wants to be able to say afterwards that nobody made him.

And there was a girl of nineteen at the back with a bowl in her hands, not doing anything with the bowl.

He was thirty feet away, in the dark, on the wet side of a low ground field, and he could not go in, and he stood where he was for about an hour and forty minutes.

He heard most of it. The room is one room and the night is quiet and a person does not have to strain.

---

The woman's name was Ilse Ferrow and she said it herself, twice, because she did the asking properly.

"Mr Orleb. I am Ilse Ferrow and I underwrite for the house over the chandler's, and that is the whole of what I am. I have written the number on the top of this page and I am going to read it to you and then I am going to answer anything you ask, and if there is a thing you want to know and I have not offered it, you ask me and I will tell you or tell you I do not know."

Then the figures, and Marek had them in his list and heard them said out loud in a kitchen for the first time.

Seed for the low ground at the February price, ELEVEN HUNDRED. A share of it if you take only what you can stand in one season, SEVEN HUNDRED AND FORTY, and that is a bad year and Fisk will tell you it is a bad year. The paper is THREE HUNDRED and the paper is not a loan and there is no name on it that can be chased, and it is a rate and not a sum, and she said the word *rate* three times on purpose.

"And the plough," said Orleb.

"The plough," said Ferrow, "is forty. The plough is not on the paper. The plough is the money you have, and I would take the money first, because a house with seed and no animal is a house that will borrow again in August at a worse price than mine, and I have watched that happen and so have you."

"You have been in this room before," said Orleb.

"Twice. In the autumn and in the bad spring, and I did not sell you anything either time, and there is a reason for that which I will give you if you want it."

"I want it."

"You did not want it," said Ferrow. "You are sixty-one and you have had one bad autumn and you did not want it then either, and I did not sell you, and I would like that in your head while you are reading the number, because a man who thinks he has been sold something stops listening to the price."

And the girl at the back said something with the bowl in her hands, and it was quiet, and Marek did not catch it, and the man said, "She goes."

---

The question came about twenty minutes in and it was not a question about the price.

"Who pays the other year?"

The room did not answer for a moment. There was a chair moved and a kettle moved and outside a frog, and Marek stood in the wet field with his hands in his coat and understood that a woman of nineteen in a low ground kitchen was about to learn the answer to the only question she had asked in four years, from a stranger who was paid to be honest.

"Everybody signs the fourth clause," said Ilse Ferrow, "and four fifths of the people who sign it have not read it, and I am not going to stand in a kitchen and say they have. So I will tell you what it says in words. It says that if this season is favoured — if the sowing goes in the ground and takes and the weather comes up right — the cost of that favour does not go into the air. It goes against the district named in the schedule. The schedule names this low ground. So it goes against this low ground, and it comes on the district, and the district means the rate."

"And the rate is the water and the levy on the water and the school on the water."

"It is."

"So when the rate comes on us, we pay it out of the crop, and the crop is the favour. We pay for the good year with the good year."

"Yes."

"Then who is the other year for?"

Ferrow took her time about it, and Marek thought: she is not enjoying this, and she is also not sorry, and both of those are true and one of them is on the card in her coat pocket.

"The tower books the failure," she said. "It does not have anybody to give it to. It has a place to book it, and the place is the district, and that is not a word I have got any better than, and I have been doing this eight years and I have never found a plainer one. A district is a box. A box does not get asked. So the other year is the low ground's, and yours, and the neighbours', and the year after that if it needs to be, and the rate is how it arrives, and I have seen it arrive." She closed the ledger on her knee. "I am not going to tell you the other year is a stranger's, because it is not. That is the part I will not sell, and I have turned down about four times the price of a night's lodging over it."

"And if we do well," said Orleb. "Say we get a year like the year before the flood. Does it come now, or does it come then?"

"That is the right question and I am going to answer it exactly, and you are going to dislike it." Marek heard the chair again, and the ledger shut flat on a knee. "It does not come in the good year. There is no such thing as a good year on a district that has been named. In a good year your ground is up and the threshing share comes in and the rate is paid and the account is square, and the failure has not gone anywhere, because it was never in your ground to begin with — it was in the weather of a year you did not get. The district is still named. So the second year the curtain goes to the ridge, and the low ground's rate goes up, and it goes up by more than the good year was worth, because the good year paid for it."

"So it is twice."

"It is not twice and I will not have it said twice in a kitchen," said Ferrow. "It is once. It is bigger in a good year than in a bad one. And the only thing a good year does for you is buy you the time in which to be charged for it."

Somebody at the back of the room said something very quietly that Marek did not hear, and the girl with the bowl was the only one who did not move.

"And I sign it anyway," said Orleb.

"You sign it because the seed is eleven hundred and you have ninety and the girl goes or she stays." She uncapped the pen. "I am not going to say that makes it right. I am going to say that I have put it to you in words and you are the only person in this room who can say no, and if you say no then in a fortnight there will be a young man at this door with no licence and a different number, and that is true, and I would rather you heard it from me."

He signed, and Marek heard the pen, and the sound of a pen is not a small sound on a quiet night.

---

Ferrow came out about the hour after that to empty a pail into the gutter and found a man standing in her neighbour's field in the dark.

She was not startled. She set the pail down, straightened up, and looked at him, and Marek understood that she had known he was there for the last twenty minutes and had spoken the answer with a man outside listening on purpose, and that he was going to be told so, and he was.

"Mr Vale. You are the recertification and you have no office, and you have been standing in a field for an hour and forty minutes because you cannot make yourself walk away from a thing." She wiped her hands. "I was told to speak plainly to anybody who came. It costs nothing and it is honest and there is no reason to do it quietly in a lane where everybody can hear the rain."

"I heard the whole thing."

"You heard the whole thing," said Ferrow. "Then you know that the only question she asked in four years was who pays the other year, and that I answered it, and that the man is sixty-one and has one bad autumn behind him, and that he signed because the seed is eleven hundred and he has ninety." She picked the pail up. "And now I am going to tell you the thing that will spoil your month, and I am going to tell you at the top of my voice on purpose, because you have heard everything else and you may as well have this in the same key."

He waited.

"He signed the assignment himself," said Ilse Ferrow. "In the autumn. On the fourteenth of the second month, at the water office, on a form with a number on it, and he put his own hand on it, and it was signed and witnessed and it is a proper assignment and it is eight months old." She tipped the pail. "This house in Morrowfen is nine years old and this paper is four days old and not one of the fifty-two of them was made by anybody in this room. He is not being cheated tonight. He is being paid, at a discount, for something he signed in the autumn, and he knows exactly what he is being paid for, because I read it to him in the autumn as well and he did not want to hear it then either."

She went inside. The door stayed open about a foot, because the room was hot and the lamp was low, and through the gap Marek could see the barrel, and the ledger on the knee, and the girl at the back still holding the bowl.

He stood in the field for another half hour and then walked back up the causeway with his hands in his coat, and the case he had been building for five days came apart somewhere between the third stile and the bridge, and it did not come apart with a bang. It came apart the way a shoe comes apart. He had been holding a shape where a careful old man had been tricked, and there was no careful old man, and there was a form with a number on it signed in the autumn by a man with ninety in the house, and behind it fifty-one more, and behind those a district.

And there was a counterweight sitting in a cooperative's own instrument in its own fourth clause, in its own witnesses' hands, under the name of the low ground where the seed was going, and no one had lied about anything anywhere, and the only thing in the whole business that could not be argued with was the fourth clause, because it was correct.

He got up the hill in the dark and the Tallow lamps were lit along the Gate and the board at the tank had four columns and about two hundred names on it, and there was a Trust notice on the hall door that nobody had signed, and he stood under it for a while with the card in his coat that had a figure on it and an address, and thought that in five days somebody was going to ask him to put his eye on the whole of it.
