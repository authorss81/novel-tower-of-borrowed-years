# Chapter 61 — Forty-Three and Some Months

The carriers' row is four sheds and forty feet of broken stone between the Tallow Gate and the north road, and it exists because the Gate is the lowest flat bridge in the city and anything heavy that comes into Morrowfen has to be dragged across it by somebody who is paid to be there when it is not.

Reisz had the third shed and a plate on the door with a number on it, and the number was on the district roll, and everybody in the row knew that and nobody had ever asked what it meant.

Marek got down there at seven on the morning of the thirteenth, in boots that were a size too big, with a boy of twenty-one beside him who had not wanted to be there and had said so twice on the way down.

"You can say it a third time at the cart," said Marek. "I am not going to be offended and I am not going to argue with you about it."

"I have said it twice and I meant it twice," said Ondra Kelb. "A man who said a number out loud in a room on the eleventh of this month and then went to bed has not bought the right to a morning off. That is all I am saying. I said it and now we are here."

The rain had stopped about four and the stone was drying in patches, and the row smelled of tar and wet rope and the fish off the barges, and by the time they got to the third shed Kelb had stopped talking, which Marek took for the better.

The first thing was not the man. It was the plate, which was a rectangle of painted iron about the size of a hand, and the number on it, and a line under the number in a smaller hand that said the district.

That was it. That was the whole of it, and it took a man who is good at documents about four minutes to see it and about six weeks to be able to say out loud what it was.

"You are going to tell me that is a rate," said Kelb.

"I am going to tell you that is a ratepayer," said Marek, "and that the plate is how a yard is a ratepayer, and that it is the only way. I have been looking at that plate since we turned into the row and you have not looked at it once."

"I have looked at it. I have been looking at it since the fourth shed." Kelb's left hand went up and touched the paint and came away, and he looked at his own fingers and then wiped them on his coat, which was a thing a boy does at twenty-one when a hand has not always been his. "Do not make anything of that."

"I am not making anything of it. Put your hand on the cart and we will count it out, because we are going to do this in a yard and not in a room and I would rather we had it right before a man with a cart looks up."

---

They put the flat side of a handcart's shaft down on the wet stone and Kelb counted on it with the tip of a piece of chalk he had brought for exactly this and had not admitted to.

"Five," he said. "Five households out of fifty-two on the schedule, each one a rate period and a sum and a discount and a date and a signature, and every one of them old. The oldest is eight months and the newest is four days." He drew the first line. "They are the same five I said on the eleventh, in the same order, and one of them is a man of sixty-one who signed in the autumn at the water office with his own hand, and I would like everybody in this city to stop telling me he was tricked."

"Six," said Marek. "The low ground's returnable sum. What is it."

"It is the flood quarters' uncollectable rate for the low ground, in two parts, on two dates, and it went into the fifth box on both." Kelb drew a longer line. "Seven is the house. That is the Compact's house, and the house is a holder of a sum now and not a holder of a paper, and there is a difference that about forty people in this basin are about to be asked to care about."

"And eight."

Kelb put the chalk down.

"Eight is a carrier at the Tallow Gate," he said, "and it is a rate arrears of forty-three and some months, and there is no box for the carrier's name, only for the house's, and the sum is under fifty and it is uncollectable and it is private, and there are three places in this city a thing like that can go."

He held up three fingers, which was a thing he did when he was reading a rule.

"A fund. An estate. A ratepayer." He put one finger down. "There is no fund, because there has not been one in three hundred years that would take a sum of forty-three. An estate is a dead man's property and it takes a year and a clerk and a seal, and nobody is doing that for forty-three. So it goes on a ratepayer, and the ratepayer has to be one whose rate is charged on a district roll and is not a household's rate, and there is one kind of that in the whole of Tallow Ward and it is a yard with a plate on the door."

He put the second finger down and left the third one up, and looked at it.

"That is the rule and it is not the Compact's," said Kelb. "There is no form anywhere that says the Compact may do it. The house has a licence to buy claims, and a district may lawfully hand over a returnable sum at a discount, and both of those are older than the house, and a sum that is under fifty and uncollectable and private has nowhere else in this city to be put."

"And nine."

"Nine is a district," said Kelb, and did not finish the sentence, and put the third finger down and picked up the chalk.

Marek finished it for him, standing in the middle of the carriers' row with a handcart shaft on the ground, because somebody had to say it once and because a rule with no person in it is a rule nobody has to be angry about.

"Nine is Tallow, and Tallow is the last of it, and Tallow is a district account, and nobody in this ward has been asked anything at all."

---

Reisz was forty feet off, under his own shed, hoisting a load onto a two-wheeled cart with the shafts off it, because the shafts are for men who walk and this one had come out of the barn for a horse that was not there.

The load was a churn. Not a milk churn, which is a thing a man carries in one hand, but a tallow churn, oak, banded, half the size of a door, and there were four of them, and they came up the north road from the tanneries in the wet season and went on to the coast road, and a man who took them was paid at the gate and at the gate only.

Marek had never in his life been on a carriers' row and he had about nine seconds to decide what a man doing that for thirty-one years would think of two strangers from Bell Stair standing in his stone looking at a plate on his door, and he got it wrong.

"You are not from the water office," said Bartel Reisz, without stopping, "because the water office sends a boy with a slip and the boy does not stand about."

"No," said Marek. "I am a clerk of the Chamber and I have no office, and this is Ondra Kelb, who is a counter-clerk and who counts twice."

Reisz got the churn up onto the bed and turned round. He was fifty-eight and had a plate in his hand and a wedge of chalk behind his ear and the loose kind of authority that comes with being the only man on a road who can be relied on.

"Then you are not the water office either, because the water office knows where I am." He looked at the number on the plate above his own head, which he had not needed to look at in a working day since he was twenty-seven. "What do you want."

"I want to ask you a question and I want to tell you why I am asking it, and I am not going to pretend to you that the reason is good, because it is not."

"Ask."

"There is a sum of forty-three and some months that has been set against this yard."

---

He did not put the hammer down.

That was the part Marek went back over on the causeway afterwards, because he had expected the hammer to go down and it did not, and he had built his whole approach on the hammer going down. Bartel Reisz, fifty-eight, thirty-one years with a plate on a shed door, stood under his own shed with a tallow churn on a cart and a sum of forty-three hanging in the air between two men, and he did not put the hammer down and he did not get angry.

"Forty-three," he said.

"Forty-three, and some months."

"Whose forty-three."

"That," said Marek, "is exactly the question, and I cannot answer it, and I have been walking about for two days working out that I am not going to be able to answer it, and I would rather you heard that from me than from a slip with a district's name at the top of it."

Reisz put the hammer on the bench. He did it slowly, which meant he was thinking.

"I have been on this row thirty-one years," he said. "I have carried for the tanneries, for the seed house, for two hospitals, for a chandler in Rainmarket who has been shut four years, and for a man with a white beard who brings fruit up from the coast in the warm and who has never once had a plate. I have paid the water office every quarter of my life at that counter and I have never had a bad word off a clerk."

"Yes."

"And there is a sum of forty-three against my yard."

"There is. Not yet, and I have to be exact about the not yet, because it is the whole of what I know and if I let it go soft I will have told you a thing I do not know." Marek took the flat board out of his coat — he had the recertification list and nothing else, and the list is a list of twenty-two files and it is not evidence of anything — and then put it away again, because there was no figure on it that would help. "It is on a paper. The paper is a lawful piece of paper and every signature on it is a real signature and the office that keeps it has told me the figures are right. What has not happened is that anybody has come to this door to ask you for it. That is what is not yet, and I am telling you the thirteenth and I do not know what the fourteenth is."

Reisz looked at him for a while. There was a barge come up under the Gate and the man on it was shouting at somebody about a rope.

"Then say the part you have not said," said Bartel Reisz.

"The part I have not said is that there are eight or nine steps between the Share and this shed, and none of them is a person asking you anything, and every one of them is a date and a signature and a rule, and the last of them is a district, and the district is four hundred and six people who have not been asked a question by anybody in their lives."

"Forty-three is not nine months' water for this yard," said Reisz.

"No."

"Forty-three is two months of a bad month," said Reisz. "It is what a carting man loses in a fortnight if the north road goes. I can pay forty-three. I have paid forty-three in a week twice in my life and I did not have to sell anything to anybody to do it." He said it without any defiance in it at all, which was the thing that undid Marek. "I am not asking which of yours it is. I am asking whose it is, because I would like to be able to say the name of a man I have carried for."

"That I do not have."

"Then I will wait for the slip," said Bartel Reisz, "and when the slip comes I will pay it, and I will pay it to whoever brings it, and I am telling you now, standing here, that if it is a bad thing I will still pay it, and I want that written down by somebody."

Kelb took out a small book, because he was twenty-one and carried one, and wrote it, and then stopped and looked at the page and put the book away.

"I have no office either," he said. "It is in a book with no letter beside my name and there is nowhere in this city to put a sentence a man wrote down in a yard."

"Then write it in your own book," said Marek, "and I will tell you what to do with it, and what you will do with it is nothing, and I have spent a month learning that nothing is the correct outcome and it is the only one on offer."

---

They walked back up the row and out onto the north road, and about two thirds of the way there Kelb said the thing about the list.

"I cannot tell you how many you finished."

"I know you cannot."

"It is a column with your name in it," said Kelb, "written down, and a column is not a number. There is no total on that list and there is not going to be one, because a total would be a claim and a name is a hand, and I have thought about it for two days and that is as far as I have got." He turned the piece of chalk over in his right hand and then put it in his left, which is not a thing a man does on purpose. "You told Sedge on the twelfth, standing up, in nine seconds, in a corridor."

"Nine seconds."

"He is going to do it badly on purpose. He said so to me at the fourth bell yesterday, in about so many words, and he said it the way a man says a thing he has already decided to live with." Kelb kicked a stone off the road and watched it go. "And I sat there and let him say it, and then I came down here with you this morning, and I have been thinking for two hours that I would have been the second pair of eyes on that paper eleven months ago and I would have signed it and I would have been right to."

"You would have been right to sign it," said Marek. "That is the part that is not going to get better. I have been at this for five weeks and it has not once been the alternative to being right."

"Then what is it the alternative to."

"To being the only one," said Marek Vale.

---

The low ground road was four miles and a causeway long and it was on the way back, and a man with a flat board and no office does most of his thinking on the causeway in this city, and Marek did most of his at the first stile, standing still in the wet with his hands in a coat that had a card in the inside pocket and a folded list with a pencil line on the back of it.

The ninth link was a district, and it had always been the last, and he had known that on the eleventh and decided not to do anything about it that night. What was different on the thirteenth was that a man in the third shed had said he would pay it.

A licensed carrier with a plate on his door and thirty-one years of a road and no household behind him, who had never been asked for anything by anybody, and who would pay a stranger's forty-three out of a bad fortnight because nobody had told him it was a stranger's. And a thing that has a man willing to hold it can be given to him, and a thing that has a yard on a road can be collected out of a yard, and the whole of the market's advantage is that a sum nobody will pay for can be put where there is a door.

There was a room over a seed shop on the low-ground road that had come free, and four a month, and a long table in it, and a lock on the door that Marek Vale would have been able to shut on the four ruled sheets in a flat board with oiled paper over them and on the little book of promises he had made his mother and on the pencil line he has carried since the ninth. Somebody at the seed house had told him on the ninth that a room over the shop on the far side of that road was going to be let and the Share had not decided, and he had asked about it once and had been told to ask again in a week.

He was a man with no office and no work and a bed above a lockkeeper's shop in Tallow Ward, and the room was four miles from that bed, and a man who lives where his work is can get up in the dark on a day when the work is at a desk.

He got to the seed house at the quarter after the second bell and put his hand on the door, and Mirren Fisk had the third tray open in front of her and did not turn round.
