# Chapter 71 — The Line in the Ditch

The frost came in off the ridge in the night of the twenty-fourth and by the sixth bell there was a skin of ice on the standing water in the low ground's ditches that a boot would break and a cart wheel would not.

It came in the night, on the seed that had gone into the ground on the twelfth, twelve days earlier, in nine hundred and forty acres of it, and it finished the second crop before the second crop was a thought anybody had. Sera Holt had said it would come in the last week of the month and had said she did not know which week and had not been asked, and it came in the first, and nobody in Rainmarket was surprised, and everybody lost something.

Fisk sent a boy up the causeway at the seventh bell with three words on a bit of card, and the three words were a name and a field and *come*, and the boy was fourteen and had done it before.

---

The field belonged to a household whose name was on nothing in this book yet and which the yard would have called the Trevans and which the low ground called the Under-Row end, and it lay at the bottom of the slope with nine hundred yards of the Share's stock in it and a ditch down each long side that had been there so long the banks had gone the shape of shoulders.

Marek was on the low ground road by the second bell and Neris Kade was in the field already, standing in the headland with her coat open, and Mirren Fisk was at the far end with a stick in the frost and no gloves on, because gloves are the first thing a seed keeper loses.

They walked the length of it together, from the near ditch to the far one, at the speed of people who are reading ground.

"Here," said Fisk, at about two hundred yards, and stopped.

The crop in the strip in front of them was a fortnight up on the crop at either end of it. Not a shade better. A fortnight, in a field sown on the same day, out of the same sacks, by the same four men with the same drill, on the same ground, under the same water.

"Nine days," said Neris Kade, and then would not say it again, and wrote nothing down, and stood there with her arms folded looking at a strip of ground about nine feet wide that ran the whole length of the field and lay along the ditch.

"The bench says twenty-one days on river sand at this temperature and we are at day twenty-two," said Fisk. "And that is up. So either the bench is wrong or the field is, and I have been turning sixty-one trays twice a day for eleven years and the bench has not been wrong in front of me before."

"A field is not a tray," said Marek.

"A field is not a tray," said Fisk. "You may put that in a book and put a date on it. A tray has a mark on the end of it and a woman knows it by the mark and she can turn it. This is nine hundred yards and four strips and no mark on the end of anything, and I have got no way of coming back tomorrow and turning this field, and if I had I would not know what turning it meant."

---

They went along the ditch. It was a drainage ditch, four feet at the top, going slack in places, and it was full of black water and frost at the edges, and about a hundred yards in from the headland the ground on the low side of it changed and both of them noticed it at the same moment and neither of them said anything for about ten yards.

The high side was a hand's breadth higher than the low side and the soil on the high side was a different colour, and it had been that for a long time, long enough that the difference had a name, which was that it was the high-rate side.

"It has been there since before I came," said the man who owns it, who was forty-one and had come out to them at the top of the field and had not been asked to come out. "My father's. The ditch is the boundary. It is in the survey and it is on the rate map and it is the reason my half is at the top rate and his is at the lower and that is the whole of why anybody ever dug it."

"For what," said Marek.

"For the sluice." The man said it the way a man says a thing he has said to a clerk four times. "Twenty-two years back there was one sluice and it was cut into the high side and the low side got nothing. The high side is at the top rate because it had the water and the low side is at the lower rate because it did not, and that is not an assessment somebody made about the ground. That is an assessment about the ditch."

Kade said, "Who charged that."

"Every year the water office, out of the low-ground roll, and the roll has a rate period on every line and the rate period on those two halves is not the same and has not been the same since before either of us was born." He looked at the water in the ditch. "They restated the whole low ground on the eighteenth of last month. I went and read my own line at the counter because a woman told me I could. It is the same rate it was on the fifteenth and I have got four hundred yards either side of that ditch and the only difference between the two halves is a line somebody dug."

---

Fisk put her stick in the strip.

"Here is what I am going to say and I would like it said where the three of us can hear it," she said, "because I have got four facts and I am not going to let anybody in this basin pick one of them up in the ninth month and walk off with it.

"One. A shower went over the ridge on the ninth and it missed this ground by four miles. Two. The stock that these eleven trays came off was out of a shed that was shut up wet in the flood year and was dried at the wrong end of the year. Three. There is a ditch with a rate in it on each side. Four. The district's own flood-quarters rate was restated on the eighteenth, on a rule that was correct, by an officer who had it open again in two days and has told nobody that four households lost a fortnight while it was shut.

"That is four. I have got no fifth one, and I have got no way of getting a fifth one, because there is nothing in this city you can put a field in. You can put a tray in a bench book. You can put a body in a clinic. You cannot put nine hundred yards of ground and a fortnight of growth in anything, and that is the whole of what I have been trying to say to men from Bell Stair for three weeks and it has taken me a field to say it."

"Any of those four could do it," said Marek.

"Any of those four could do it and I could not tell you which and neither could she and neither could the man who signed the instrument in the bad autumn, and neither could the two witnesses who were not paid by us and one of whom is dead." Fisk turned the stick over in her hand. "And the fourth of them is a rate, and everybody in this city is going to reach for the rate because the rate is the thing you can write down, and I am telling you now that a rate is a line in a ditch. That is all a rate has ever been. It is a line in a ditch and a number against it, and the ditch is the oldest thing on this ground and nobody dug it to be a rate, it was dug to be a boundary, and the rate came afterwards and was written down to agree with the ditch."

Neris Kade had not moved from the strip.

"Do you understand what you have just said," she said, to Fisk, and her voice was not loud and it was not warm either. "You have said that the ground knows what the paper is charging it for. And that is not a fact about ground, that is a fact about a roll, and if the roll is right the ground is only standing where the paper puts it."

"I have said a field does not remember," said Fisk. "A ditch does. And a roll remembers, and I have not got the roll and I have not got a form for asking to see one line of it, and neither has he." She nodded at Marek. "You can read a rate at a counter. You cannot have a copy of it. That is a rule and the rule is correct and it means that the only people in this basin who know that a line in a ditch and a line in a roll are the same line are the people who are standing in this ditch, and that is us, and we are three people and one of us is a man with no office."

---

They came back to the headland at the fourth bell and the light had gone that hard winter way it goes, and the man who owned the top half of the field was still there, and he wanted to know one thing and he wanted to know it plainly.

"Is my rate going to change," he said. "Because the strip is up and the rest is not and there is more of the ground coming this year than there was last year, and I have got a rate that is the same as it was on the fifteenth, and I would like to know whether the office can see a fortnight."

"No," said Marek.

"No," agreed the man. "That is what I thought. I asked the counter girl on the twentieth, nice of her, and she read me my own line and told me the rate period and the rate, and I said the ground has come up early this year, and she said, she said, that is not a line on this roll, and she was right and she was not being unkind, she was reading a roll."

He put his hands in his coat.

"My half will be charged the same as his half in the ninth month," he said, "and his half has got nothing up and mine has got a fortnight, and I have had one good fortnight in my life in this ground and I am going to be charged for the whole of it, and I would like somebody to tell me what the difference is worth, because I have got a feeling that somebody in this city knows."

Nobody answered him, and that was correct, and Marek stood in a wet headland and understood the entire shape of the next five months in about nine seconds and could not do one single thing about any part of it.

A counterweight is not a switch. He had known that for three weeks and had said it out loud twice and had believed it the way a man believes a rule. Standing in a ditch, he understood that it was a different and worse thing than that. A switch is put somewhere and somebody's hand is on it. A counterweight is a rule about what happens to a district, and the district has no hand on anything, and the rule does not need a hand, and it will do exactly what it was written to do in the ninth month, in the same room, in the same words, with nobody present and nobody able to object and nobody even able to hear about it.

There were four facts in the field and no way to choose between them, and there was a strip of ground a fortnight up, and a ditch, and a man who would be charged the same as his neighbour, and not one box in the city in which any of that could be put.

He got his boots back into the road and walked up it with both of them, and neither of them said anything for half a mile, and at the top he took out a folded list of twenty-two files, and on the back of it, where there were already six words and a full stop in pencil, he did not write anything at all, and put it away, which was the first sensible thing he had done since the ninth.
