# Chapter 74 — Four Bells

The yard on the low-ground road where Petar Duss kept his carts was forty feet deep and open at the front and had a forge-fire in a shed at the back that was not his, and there were four other yards on that road within two hundred yards of it, and on the eighth of the growth month it was the second bell and there was a man from a factor's house in Crown March standing in it with his coat off, waiting.

He had come for the cart. That was the whole of what he had come for, and he had come at the second bell because the instrument said the second bell, and the instrument said the second bell because it was written by a clerk in Ostrand and Bell's counting room off a form that had a box for every part of it and had been used for that purpose for thirty-one years.

The form is a strong thing to look at. Five parts on one sheet, a need, a pledge, a scope, a witness, a settlement, and under each of them a line with a date, and every line filled, and the witnesses in the room and not paid by the borrower, one a farrier and one a lime merchant who had both put a thumb on a witnessed copy in a counting room in Crown March in the winter, and the settlement in the fifth box saying in the ordinary words of the trade that the second cart and the gear on it stood against the house of Ostrand and Bell until the season's work was paid, and could be taken at any bell after the second on the day the instrument named.

Nothing in it was false. That is the part that has to be said, because it is the part that everybody in a room like this already knows and nobody ever puts in a letter.

Per Vahl, who was thirty-eight and had been a collector of pledged goods for six years and was not a hard man, stood in the middle of a cart yard at the second bell and looked at the cart and then at the man holding the horse.

"How is the horse," he said.

"Lame in the near hind and no better and no worse," said Petar Duss. "It has been like that a fortnight and I have been paying the farrier for it and I have not been hiding it off you and I would not have told you unless you asked, but you have asked."

"Have you got another horse."

"I have got a man who owes me for four days and I have got a cart with a lame horse and I have got what I have got."

Per Vahl nodded. He was not enjoying it and he was not pretending to, and he said the sentence he said, which is the sentence of that trade, and it is not a cruel sentence, it is a professional one.

"Then it stands," he said. "You know the position. It is not that I am taking it off you and it is not that I want it. It stands against the house until the season's work is paid, and the season's work is not paid, and I am the man that goes and gets things, and I would rather take a cart from a man I have had a cup of tea with in a counting room than take it out of a shed in the dark."

"I know the position," said Petar Duss, "and I do not want to argue with you in my own yard in front of a stranger."

---

Ondra Kelb was in the yard because he was there to measure the cart.

He had a rule of his own in his coat, a yard-wide rule of wood with a brass edge, and he was going to measure the bed and the axle and the two wheels and write four figures on a slip for the factor, because a distraint of a cart is measured before it is taken and the figures go into the factor's book, and the factor's book is in Crown March, and a Crown March book is a thing offices are obliged to answer. He had done it twice before since the spring and both times it had been for a man who could not pay and neither time had he been asked anything about his hand.

He measured with his left hand, because his right is nine years old in the sense that his right does fine work at the age of nine and has not got any of the rest, and the rule lies flat on the near rail of the bed and his left hand holds it down and his left hand reads it off the brass.

"They will take the whole bed," he said, after a while. "Not the wheels off. The wheels are not the pledge. The pledge is the bed and the gear and the pledge is a bed."

"You are telling me the wheels stay."

"I am telling you what the fifth box says and it says bed and gear," said Kelb, "and I have read it twice, and I have not been asked to be fair."

---

Marek had come into that yard at the second bell with nothing, because the eighth of the growth month was the ninth day of having nothing, and there is one road in this city where everything happens in the open and it is the low-ground road, and he was walking up it with his hands in his pockets.

He stood at the back of the yard for about a minute and a half and watched a man in a good coat with his coat off tell a drayman that his cart was going to be taken, correctly, in the words of a form, at the second bell, and he watched the drayman take the halter off the horse and hand it to his boy and stand there with his hands at his sides, and he had been in a city for nine days watching people be told what a rule costs them, and something happened in him that he did not have a name for and did not examine.

He did not decide to do it. He wants that understood, and it is the only thing he has got out of it. He did not stand there and weigh it up and choose. He put his hand flat on the sheet on the tail-board of the cart, which is where Per Vahl had left it after reading the fourth box out loud, and he held the settlement — the event, the taking of the bed and the gear, the thing that was about to happen at this bell and not one minute later — and he held it for four bells.

He could not have held it longer. That was not a decision either. It was a thing about how long a thing can be, the way a man cannot hold his arm out past a certain point and it is not a matter of will.

---

The first bell of the hold was the easiest and it was also the moment in which the whole thing became real, because nothing at all happened.

A hold does not stop a room. A hold stops one event and the room carries on in the ordinary way and the people in it do not know that anything is being held, because there is nothing to see. There is no noise and there is no mark on a cart and the horse is still lame and the light in the yard is the same light.

The boy took the horse's water in. The fire in the far shed went on being somebody else's fire. A woman came up the road with a hoop in her hand and asked the drayman whether he had still got the little barrow and he said he had and she said she would bring it back in the morning and did not say thank you and he did not expect it to. A man from the forge next door crossed the yard with a bar in his hand and stopped, saw the man in the good coat with his coat off, said *o* — and meant it kindly, and went on. The rain started at about the fourth bell and was the ordinary rain of the growth month, which is to say it was not doing anything remarkable at all.

Marek stood in the yard with his hand on a tail-board for four bells and the only thing that happened was that nothing happened, and after about two of them he understood what he had bought, and after three he understood that he could not give it back, and by the fourth he had stopped thinking about it and was thinking about the rest of it, which is what a person does when they have been handed a thing with no instructions.

---

There is a vault book of marks on the second floor of the Chamber, and it is kept by a man called Osk, and there is no fee, because the tower never decided a person should be charged for being damaged.

That is the whole of what a person in this city is told about what a hold costs, and it is more than most people get, and Marek had been in the room with Osk once in the rains month with a junior clerk of the Chamber who had been hurt in the course of duty, and he had been shown the book and had not been shown a line out of it.

The entry for the eighth of the growth month, at about the sixth bell, would be made by a man with a pen who does not know the person it is for, and the book does not ask the person either, and that is the practice and it is the correct practice, and Marek was not going to be the one to write it down and he was not going to be the one to go and ask about it afterwards, and the reason he was not is not a rule. There is no rule. It is that a man who goes and looks at his own line in that book has started keeping a record of what he costs, and a man who keeps a record of what he costs begins to spend on purpose, and he had not decided to spend on purpose and he was going to be careful to go on not having decided that.

He also could not read it. That is the other half and it is the half that got him through the afternoon. Whatever the book says about him on the eighth of the growth month is in a shorthand that Osk writes for a clerk who has been hurt at work, and it is not a number and it is not a name and it is not a shape anybody could carry in their head and look at on a wet morning, and whatever it says, it does not say *four bells*, and a man who is handed four bells can give them away on purpose tomorrow, and a man who is handed a mark cannot.

---

Petar Duss used the four bells, because of course he did, and he used them well, and Marek would have been ashamed of himself if he had not.

The barrow was out of the shed by the second-and-a-half and the horse was harnessed with a rope on the near hind and not one single thing about the arrangement was sound, and the drayman went up the low-ground road with a load of quicklime from the kiln at the second-and-a-half, and it was for the Ropewalk standpipe, which had been short for five weeks, and which was a job that four men had refused in the ordinary way because there was not four men in Morrowfen who would come out for a standpipe, and which was the smallest job in the whole of the district, and which is exactly the kind of work that a man with a lame horse and a good cart and four bells of nothing will do at no profit whatever because it is the only work there is.

He came back at the fifth bell with the barrow empty and the harness wrong and the horse's breathing wrong, and he unhitched it and watered it and sat on the tail-board, which is where Marek's hand had been, and did not know that anything had happened to him at all.

He told Marek, without being asked and entirely pleasantly, that he had been in the yard three weeks now and the standing of the district's rate on the low ground had been restated on the eighteenth and he had been down to the counter to read his own line like everybody else on that road, and that he had a rate period and a rate and a district on it and that the sum of what he owed the district for the flood quarters was on the second quarter of the year and not the fourth, and that he had not signed anything and had been asked by a man in a lane in the spring whether he would, and had said he would think about it, and had thought about it, and had said no, and that he had said no to a woman in a lane which was a thing he was not proud of and that his wife had told him he was a fool.

"That is not a fool," said Marek. "That is a man who has read a schedule."

"I have not read a schedule," said Petar Duss. "I have never seen one. I have a cart and a horse that is going and a house, and I would like it understood that I have not refused anything out of any principle. I refused because I did not want my name on a line and then find out in a year what the line was for."

---

At the sixth bell Per Vahl took the bed and the gear off the cart in about eleven minutes, in the rain, with his coat back on, and wrote the four figures Kelb had given him into a book, and asked the drayman whether he wanted the gear or the bed, and gave him the gear, which is the smaller thing and is the correct answer, and put the bed on a handcart of his own and took it up the road himself and the man helping him was the boy from the forge next door.

Then he said, to nobody in particular, in the way of a man finishing a piece of business properly, "Four bells late is nothing. It is in the fifth box and it says any bell after the second, and if I had gone at the second it would have been exactly as good and I would not have got wet, and there is nothing in that paper about four bells. You are welcome."

Marek said nothing at all, and Kelb looked at him for slightly too long and then went back to rolling up his rule, and said, in the ordinary voice of a young man with nothing to say,

"You were stood in that yard for four hours, Mr Vale."

"I was."

"There's four loads of lime gone up the Ropewalk and the standpipe's not short any more," said Kelb, "and that's a real thing, and I'd not be telling you it if it weren't. And I don't know how you came to be stood in that yard, and you're not going to tell me, and I'm not going to ask, and I want you to know that I'm not going to ask and that it is not because I don't want to know."

Then he put the rule in his coat and went out into the rain and up the hill.

---

What Marek had learned by the seventh bell on the low-ground road, alone, with the rain coming down in the ordinary way, was the thing that the volume had been telling him for a week and that he had been refusing in the only way a clerk can refuse anything, which is by looking for a defect in it.

There is no defect in that form. Not one. A need, a pledge, a scope, two witnesses who were not paid by him, and a settlement, and the settlement was due and it was taken and the man lost his bed and got his gear and did not know why he had had a morning. A hold delays perfectly. It is the best tool in this building. There is nothing it does not do exactly, on time, to the letter.

And on the eighth of the growth month, between the second bell and the sixth bell, the whole of the market that has been eating Rainmarket for a month went on eating it, and not one single part of it noticed.

A boy of fourteen came up the lane at the third bell with a list for a queue that had not reformed since the window reopened, and stood at the end of it for forty minutes, and then went home with it. A woman from the second row of houses went into the shed on the low-ground road at the fourth bell and asked to look at a line, and Signe Alve wrote in a book what the woman said, in a second hand, and the column at the end of the page had nothing at the head of it and had nothing at the head of it at the fourth bell and nothing at the head of it at the sixth. The water office charged the low ground's third quarter, four miles off, with nobody present, as it does, and the sum went on a roll and the roll was a string on a counter and the roll did not know or care that there was a cart standing in a yard on the low-ground road.

Four bells. He had bought four bells of a man's working day at the price of an entry in a book on the second floor that he will never read, and every part of it was perfect, and he had given four bells of a man's life to a man who did not need them, in order not to have taken a cart off a man who is going to lose it anyway in the ninth month.

He walked up the causeway in the wet. And the thing he could not get out from under, for about a mile, was not the cart and was not the book: it was that there had been a moment in that yard when a delay was the only honest thing he had, and he had used it, and it had felt like nothing at all, and he had not once, in four bells, wished for it.
