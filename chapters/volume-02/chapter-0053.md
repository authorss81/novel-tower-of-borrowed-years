# Chapter 53 — What a Counterweight Is For

There are two men in Bell Stair who have to know what a counterweight is, and one of them was Marek, and the other was not going to find out from him.

He put the file on Ostrowsky's desk at the first bell of the fourth, opened at the fourth clause, with a pencil laid across the page so that Ostrowsky would not have to touch it to see how far down to look.

Ostrowsky read it standing up, because he reads everything standing up; nineteen years in the levy office and a bad hip and a habit that has outlived the hip.

"A counterweight," he said. "Right."

"I need it explained as though I know nothing and want to know everything, and I want it explained by somebody who has sealed a great many of them, and I want to be told when I have it wrong."

"That is a good way to ask." Ostrowsky moved the pencil, read to the end of the clause, and then did not say anything for about half a minute, and Marek understood in that half minute that Ostrowsky was about to find out whether he knew, and that this was the moment at which the whole morning would go.

"I have sealed seasonal instruments since the year the flood office opened," said Ostrowsky, "and I have never had a supervisor ask me that, and I have never had a second clerk's eye ask me that, and I want to say first that this is not a complaint about you."

"I know what it is."

"It is that a second eye is a person and not a procedure, and a second eye is on a file because somebody upstairs is worried, and worry does not go round asking the man who has been doing the sealing for eleven years what the words mean." He squared the file. "First thing. It is the thing on the other side. A sequence loan moves the order of events — that is all it does, it does not invent anything, it changes which of two things that could both happen happens first — and whatever does not happen does not stop being possible, it goes somewhere. The tower books it. If you move a shower off a low ground, then some other day is a day with no shower on it, and that day belongs to somebody, and somebody has to be entered against it. Otherwise the arithmetic does not close, and it is not a metaphor, it will not close."

"And the second thing?"

"And the second thing is that it is a second pledge."

"That is not right."

"I know it is not right, I have told you it is not right, and I am telling you the wrong thing because it is the wrong thing that people believe." He put his hand flat on the file. "Nobody gives it. That is the difference and it took me twenty years and a supervisor's retirement to see it. It is not a pledge because a pledge is given by a person who has been asked and who said something. It is entered. It is booked against a name the office has got, and if the name is a person then that person is in the room with a witness, and if the name is a yard or a house or a ward or a district then it is a box the office can write in." He took his hand off. "A district is not a person. I have known that since I was thirty. I have never once had it put in front of me in a place where I had to say so."

"Then the fourth clause is a box the office can write in."

"Yes."

"And the district named in the schedule is the low ground, and the Share's seed is going into the low ground, and the threshing share comes off the low ground, and the whole instrument is correct."

"Correct," said Ostrowsky. "I will go further. It is better drafted than a good many I have sealed, and the schedule is named, and the naming is the part they care about, and I have never in eleven years had reason to think about the part they are not talking about." He looked at Marek. "I have been wrong about a thing for eleven years. I would like that to have been useful to somebody and I do not know yet whether it has been."

---

"If you were allowed to write in the margin of that file," said Marek, "what would you write."

"I have thought about it for four minutes and I have got an answer and it is no use to anybody." Ostrowsky put the pencil across the file again. "I would write *the fourth clause is correctly drafted and the named district is not a party and has no officer who can be asked*. Twenty-two words. It is a true sentence. It is the kind of sentence that gets a clerk noticed, and the word that gets a clerk noticed is *distracted*."

"And you would not write it."

"I have written nothing in the margin of a settlement instrument in nineteen years," said Ostrowsky, "and I am not going to start on an ordinary afternoon for a man who is not the clerk of the day. There is no place to put it, which is the difficulty and is the whole difficulty, and it is not that nobody will read it. There are four hundred people in this basin who would read it and understand every word of it, and there is nowhere in the world to put twenty-two words where any of them would come across them."

He looked at the file for a moment longer.

"It wants a form. That is all that wants. Somebody with authority has to sit down one afternoon and invent a place on a piece of paper where a clerk may put a true sentence about a thing nobody has asked about, and it has to be a box, because if it is not a box then it is a letter, and a letter is an opinion."

"And nobody is going to invent it this year."

"No," said Ostrowsky. "Nobody is going to invent it this year. Next year there will be a different problem and this one will be in the second file behind it."

He went up the stair at the third bell to get his coat off the peg by the landing window, and the landing is the one place in that building where nobody goes for a quarter of an hour at a time, because there is nothing on it and the bell rope is on the other side.

He had the file under his arm and he was tired in a way that had nothing to do with the morning, and he put his hand flat on the cover.

The ink came up on the paper in sparse lines, the way it always came up, and it was not much, and it was not a sentence anybody had written.

> The counterweight is named.
> Behind the name there is nobody.

Then nothing, for a moment, and then one more line, which was the part he would think about for the rest of his life and which he would not be able to explain to anybody:

> Bring nothing to the low ground. Count.

He took his hand off the cover and sat down on the stair, because the stair was there, and the headache came up the back of his neck the way it does, and the light in the stairwell went narrow at the edges and stayed narrow for about nine seconds and then let go.

*Behind the name there is nobody.* It was not a lie. That was the thing he sat with for the length of a bell. Not a lie, not an omitted page, not a figure that did not balance — a true sentence about a file that was entirely proper, and there was no such thing in the world to do with a true sentence of that kind except wait for somebody to fill the gap, and about nine hundred people in this basin were in the business of filling gaps for money.

He could not produce it. He had a book with four things in it and none of them was worth anything, and the five uses of that power had cost him a parcel he ate and could not remember, a quarter of an hour, an hour with a man's voice in it, and a mark on his forearm. He was not going to spend the sixth on a landing to be asked about in a room by a man who could sign.

So he went back down and worked the twenty-two files from the third bell to the sixth, and he did not say one word about the landing to Ostrowsky, or to anybody, or in the book at the back of his own, and that was the first thing he did in Rainmarket that he would have to do again.

At the sixth bell he asked Ostrowsky for the walk.

"The clerk of the day may not leave the hill without the leave of the desk."

"I am not the clerk of the day."

"Then you have nothing to be given leave by," said Ostrowsky, "and if you go out on the low ground alone on a working afternoon there is nobody to say that you went, and a man who is nowhere is the exact shape of a man doing something he should not be doing. I will not help you be that man and I will not report you. Sit at a desk."

"I am going to sit at a desk," Marek agreed, "and on the ninth I am going to be somewhere else, and you are not going to know that from me, and I am telling you now so that when it happens you can put it down to being told."

---

Tamsin was at the door with her coat off and a lamp in her hand, and she is a seamstress and there is a customer's coat on the table behind her that she has been in the middle of for four days.

"You said you would come at the second bell," she said.

Marek stood in the passage and looked at her.

"I did not," he said, and then stopped, because he could hear the shape of the sentence he was about to say, which was *I did not say that*, and it was going to be a lie, and she was going to let him say it, and he was going to hear himself.

He stood there for about four seconds.

"I have lost the middle of today," he said. "I remember standing at the desk with the file and I remember the lamp on this stair and there is nothing between the two of it."

Tamsin put the lamp on the table beside the coat.

"You have not eaten," she said. "There is bread and there is a pot scraped and I have not been in the room since the fourth, because a woman of forty-nine is not going to sit and watch her son not eat." She picked up the coat and went back to the table. "I am not going to ask you what it is. I have had thirty-one years of a house where nobody asks the question, and I am telling you the reason for once, and the reason is that when I am asked I have to answer, and there is one answer I am not giving you in the next ten years."

"You have never asked me what I do all day."

"No," said Tamsin Vale. "You have worked out for yourself that I have never asked you what you do all day, and that is the correct conclusion and I am glad you got there by yourself."

He ate the bread standing up. Then he took the list out of his coat and laid it flat on her table beside the customer's coat, which she moved out of the way without being asked, because the table is for work and he has been putting work on it since he was nine.

"The fourth line," he said. "The Share. I have to go out to the low ground and stand in it and look at the ground, and I cannot tell anybody that I am going, and I cannot take a paper out there, and if I do not go and look at it in the next five days then the seed goes into the ground on the twelfth and nobody will ever know what was in the ground."

She did not ask what he was going to look for.

"Then go on the ninth," said Tamsin, "because the sixth and the seventh are the days the men from the yard come about the seed carts, and there will be six people in that shed asking you what you are, and you will not be able to say one true thing."

"How do you know what days the seed carts come."

"Because Lio has been going down there every day since the second and comes home and tells me, and because I am her mother, and because she is nine and nobody has thought to tell her that there are things she is not supposed to be interested in." Tamsin took her needle up. "Go on the ninth. And eat on the road, not in a lane, because a man who eats in a lane is a man who has nowhere to sit."
