# Chapter 87 — In Parts

The register was a shed, and the shed was the Gate shed, and the yard had said it could be used and the yard had said it on the condition of the three things that were on the back of its own refusal with a string through them, and nobody had charged anything to anybody for the use of a shed in Tallow Ward and nobody ever has.

On the twenty-first day of the harvest month, from the second bell, the door was propped open and a counter was two trestles and a board, and there was a queue in the lane, and about sixty people were in it by the fourth bell of a morning on which the whole of the contents of the register was one entry.

Nobody in that shed was paid. The levy had not been agreed and would not be for three more days. The reader was nineteen, and had read a yard's refusal out twice in the seed month, and had read the four lines off a nail exactly once, because it took him forty seconds and there was nothing in it that wanted a second time. The second person was a rope-maker of thirty-nine out of Ropewalk who had not asked to be anything and had said in the yard that she would be asked herself, and she was there, at the trestle, with her own sheet in her hand, which was not the register's sheet and was not going in the register.

And a man with no office stood behind the board and read out, and could enter nothing.

---

Oren Pike's house went in first, and he insisted on that, and he gave a reason that was about the market and was not a good reason and he gave it anyway.

"I go first because I am the one who can afford to be first," he said. "Anybody can be second in a room where the first has made a fool of himself. If I go second and somebody in this lane says *the factor waited*, then in a month there will be a line in this room with a person's name on it and the story will be that a factor was careful, and the story will be true, and the room will be about the story. So I go first and I am careful in the open."

He had it in a leather case and he opened it on the trestle himself.

It went in as its parts. Six lines that had to be six lines, and the third of them is the one the whole of this month has been about, and Pike read them in the ordinary voice of a man reading a price list, and did not improve one of them, and did not say a single word of explanation before or after, and the room listened to about two hundred and forty words.

Then the second person at the trestle asked him the second question, which was: is that your line, and is that the person behind it, and is it your words.

"It is my line. It is the person behind it. It is my clerk's words and not mine, and I have told him to stand there while I read it, and he is a man of twenty-six and he can afford to be a person behind a line and I would not."

Sanne Droll wrote it in the words it was given in.

---

Ondra Kelb came up the hill at about the third bell with a slate and his own rule, and nobody had asked him to and he said so four times, and then he counted.

He counted it the way he counts everything, which is twice, once down the columns and once from the back of the sheet in a different order, with his left hand, which is a nine-year-old's and is not his and is better at figures than the hand it replaced. It took him about eleven minutes and at the end of it he said the sentence he had said in the counting room in the seed month and had been ashamed of ever since.

"It balances," said Ondra Kelb. "Every part against every part. There is no invented line in it and no second hand that should not be there and no figure that has been put in twice. There is nothing wrong with this entry and nobody in this shed has done anything wrong, and I have counted it twice and I am saying so out loud because I am the only person here who can, and I would like it understood that I have been relieved again and that the relief is worse the second time than the first."

"You have been relieved," said Pike, "at the prospect of a man with no office putting a figure in wrong."

"I have," said Kelb, "and I have not got a way of saying that in a room without sounding like a man who wants the work to be dishonest, and I have got one now and I am going to use it: I was relieved because it is not mine. In the seed month I found that nothing was wrong with a file and I was the only person in Bell Stair who could look, and I have carried that about for two months like a stone in a boot. It is not mine. Ondra Kelb is not a party to a thing in a shed in Tallow Ward. Put that in the second column of your entry if you have got a column for it."

"You have not got a column for it," said Sedge, from the board, with the form in his hand, "and that is the sixth time somebody has asked me for one this month and I have cut six lines and I am not cutting a seventh, because a seventh is a column and a column gets a name and a name gets a heading and I have been in this building long enough that twice in it and I know what happens."

---

The man who came in at about the fourth bell had come in because a woman in a good coat had come to his door in the seed month and said his own name out loud, and his name was on the schedule of fifty-two, and he had never seen it and could not have read it.

He was about fifty. He had a bad cough and he had come down the causeway on a bad morning for no reason he could give anybody except that a rope-maker in his lane had said there was a room where you could find out, and he had not believed her, and he had come anyway.

The second person asked him the second question.

"Is that your line?"

"That is my line."

"Is that you?"

He looked at the board for a while.

"That is my name," he said. "I did not sign anything. My wife signed it. She is not here. She is at the tank at the Gate twice a day for the water and she is fifty-one and she cannot get up the causeway in this cold, and I have been to that woman's door in the seed month and I was told my own name out loud in my own doorway by a person in a good coat who had a book, and I said *that is me* and she said *that is you* and that was the whole of it and I have been not knowing what it is ever since."

Nobody in the shed said anything, and the lane outside got louder for about four seconds and then stopped.

Sedge had the form in his hand and it was a form with six ruled lines and no boxes and the second line wanted a person, and he read it twice to himself and then put it down on the trestle, flat, which is what a clerk does with a thing he is not going to do.

"I cannot fill that," he said. "I can put your name in it and I can put what you have just said in it, and then it is a line with a man's name on it and the man's words, and the person behind it is his wife and she has not been asked anything, and in four years somebody will read that and say the man knew.

"And I cannot leave the second line empty, because an empty second line is the piece of sack in Fisk's house, and you have just heard a woman of forty-six say that out loud in a yard, and she was right, and I cut this form and I am not going to be the man who puts a thing with nothing at the head of it in a shed in town."

---

Marta Brel, who had been down off the causeway twice in a week and had said on the first occasion that she would come again, stood up on the step of the shed and said the thing that made the room able to carry on, and she said it without being asked and without any particular feeling at all.

"He can bring her," she said. "Or she can come herself. That is the rule and it is an old rule and it is not yours, and you printed it on the ninth of this month and you have not noticed that you have been working to it all day.

"The keeper and the reader, and a second person can come with you and is named at the door and does not get a hand on anything, and what is in the room is what the person said and not what the second person said, and if the second person is lying then the reader does not know that and cannot find out, and everybody in this lane has been living with that since the rains and about forty of us can say it in words.

"So the second line takes his words. And the fourth line is for anything anybody else has to say about it, and the fourth line is where his wife goes when she comes, and the two are printed one above the other and are not joined, and the room does not know which of them is the line and is not going to guess."

Sedge picked the form up off the trestle and wrote.

"Two lines and a rule I did not cut," he said, "and I would like it minuted somewhere that the form is not the whole of the thing and the man who cut it does not know all of what it is, and that the difference between those two states is a woman of forty-six at the weighbridge who cannot read."

---

They asked Sanne Droll at the sixth bell, because the yard had said in a yard on the ninth of the month that the person who keeps it was to be asked like anybody else, and the yard was not in the shed and had not sent anybody and would not have sent anybody.

The four questions were the four questions. She answered them in her own words and they went on a sheet on the wall with the rest, and the fourth answer was about the days, and she said the days were not going to be entered because there was no box for them, and she said that in the voice of a woman saying a price of rope, and the room wrote it down and did not improve it.

And then, because the fourth answer was the cost and the room had not yet heard the cost in anybody's own words, she said one more thing and then sat down, and what she said was:

"You have all been very careful about the hands on this. The yard has been careful for two months, and there is not one man from a hall in this room and I am going to say that out loud because it is a thing that ought to be said in a room. I have been careful too, and I am the second person at this counter, and I have not been careful about my own household's days, and they are eleven, and my daughter is fifteen and she is not on any roll of anything and she will read this about the yard in a year's time, and that is my answer and I have not got a better one."

Nobody thanked her. She did not look round the room to see whether they did.

---

They shut the door at the seventh bell because the shed is not a warm building, and the register had one entry in it, and the entry was a grain factor's own book entered as its parts in the words of his own clerk, and it balanced, and nobody had lied, and it was the whole of the counter-move that the year had produced so far.

Pike stood in the lane afterwards and let about sixty people go past him, and about nine of them did not look in, and he noticed all nine and Marek noticed that he noticed, and neither of them said one word about it that evening.

"She was asked her own four questions at a counter," Pike said, eventually, "in front of about forty people, because her yard said so. That is the single best thing anybody has done in this basin this summer and I would like it recorded that it will cost that room about a fifth of the people who come to it inside a month, and I have no idea which nine, and neither does the room, and I am not going to guess in a lane."

"You think that is the reason."

"I think it is a reason," said Oren Pike. "I have got a figure for what a reading is worth and I have not got a figure for what a room is worth, and the difference between those two numbers is the whole of the trade I am in, and I have been in it thirty years and I have never once been asked for the second one."

He got into a cart and went down the low-ground road, and the register had one line in it, and about nine people in Tallow Ward had decided something that afternoon and had not told anybody, and the sixth line of the form was ruled and empty, and it was ruled empty because a man in a copy room who was not sure of the form had cut it for a thing that did not exist yet, and nobody in that shed could say what the fifth line was for either, and the reader wrote nothing about it, because there was nothing to write and no day to write it on.
