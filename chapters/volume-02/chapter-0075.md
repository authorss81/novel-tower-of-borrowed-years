# Chapter 75 — The Under-Row

The fourth line of the schedule was not a household in Rainmarket.

It was forty-one acres on the middle terrace in the Red Uplands, four miles below Hartlow, and it was called the Under-Row, and the man who kept it was forty-nine years old and had come out into his own yard on the first of the growth month to ask a stranger from the low ground whether he knew the price of lime in a hard year, and had asked it because there were three loads on the road behind him and a man standing in his gateway, and a question is cheaper than an explanation.

Kelb had not wanted to give the address and had given it. That was the third time in a month that somebody had asked Ondra Kelb for something and got it, and he had done it in a book with no letter beside his name, and he had said, standing in the corridor with the book shut, that a man who is asked for a thing by a person with no office is going to be asked for a thing one day by a person with an office, and that he would do it anyway, and that Marek was not to come back.

Marek came back the same afternoon, because the road up is the same road up and it takes a whole day and there was no other way to do it that did not involve telling somebody he had been asked.

---

The holding went up the terrace in three strips with a grass lane between each, and about two hundred old apple trees on the middle strip, and a press at the top end under a lean-to of tarred board, and eleven hundred bushels of windfalls in a heap going soft behind it. There was a frost still in the shadow of the terrace wall at eleven in the morning.

The man was Josip Malnar and he had a basket under his arm, a wide one, willow, old, with a strap mended twice, and he was filling it off the heap a third at a time and carrying it down to the press himself, and he was doing it that way because his two men were down the valley cutting a hedge for a neighbour and because he had been doing it himself since the ninth and had got used to it.

"You will take one," he said, when he saw the coat. "No, you will not. I have four of them and a woman in the house. You will take one and you will put it down where I put mine, there, by the wall, and not on the heap, and I will not have a stranger standing in my heap looking at my heap."

His name was on the fourth line of a schedule in Bell Stair and he did not know it, and Marek had said nothing about it, and had asked instead about the lime.

---

They sat on the press bench with a basket each. It was the fourth bell and the light was going and the terrace below them was the colour of a wet road and the terrace above was in the sun with a cloud on it, and the old woman inside had put a lamp in the window and shut a shutter against the draught, and neither of them could hear what the other was saying very well and both of them shouted slightly, and it turned out to make the conversation better.

"You have come up here for a piece of paper," said Malnar, about an hour in.

"I have come up here to ask you about one."

"That is a piece of paper with a coat on," said Malnar. "Ask."

Marek asked.

The ground rate on the Under-Row had gone into arrears in the flood years along with everybody else's on that terrace, and the flood years' assessments were for years that did not happen and were not collectable at any price, and a Crown March house out of the Crown March road had advanced the holding money on them in the second month of MR 412 and taken an assignment of the collection right, which is a lawful thing and had a box for it because that house cut its own form and its own form had seven boxes, and the assessment had been made in the wrong year by somebody who had left, and the payment had been made by nobody, and when the house found out in the bad spring that nothing was going to be collected off it at all, they sold the paper.

"They sold it in a yard," said Josip Malnar, "to a man with a table and a stool, and he was honest with me, which is the thing I cannot get past, because I have been sitting here for ten months trying to work out how I was cheated by a man who told me the truth.

"He said: this is the right to collect the flood quarters of the low ground off one ratepayer, dated, at a discount, and it is a good price because everybody knows the low ground will not pay. He said: it is not a great thing, it is a piece of a thing, and pieces of things are cheap and sometimes they are not. He said: you will not get it in a year and you will not get it in three and you may get a bit of it. He said all of that and he did not once tell me a false thing, and I gave him four hundred and ten pounds on the strength of it, and it was our own money and it was the money for my daughter's wedding, and she is married and it was a good wedding, and I have not regretted the wedding and I have regretted the four hundred and ten pounds very nearly every day since I paid it."

He put the basket down.

"He told me the claim was made of fifty-two lines," he said. "He said, there are fifty-two of them, out of a cooperative of sixty-one, and yours is somewhere in it, and here is the schedule and you may look at it. And I have got it in a chest in the front room and I have not read it, and I am telling you that in my own yard before you ask me, and I am fifty years old next spring and I have been in two wars' worth of somebody else's arithmetic and I did not sit down and read a schedule about other people's business in a bad year when I had trees to thin."

---

Marek did not go and get it. That was the first thing and he did it on purpose and it was the hardest thing he did that month.

"No," he said. "Do not read it. And I am not going to tell you to read it, and I am going to tell you why, and I am going to be the only person in this city who says this to you, so I would like you to sit still for it.

"You have been sold a piece of a thing. What a piece of a thing is made of is a list of fifty-two lines, and the list is a description of a claim and not the claim, and a description of a claim is a fifth box on a form, and the fifth box is the oldest box on the form, and nobody who cut the form had ever heard of what was going to go in it. And I know what is in it, and I have read it, and I cannot tell you who your line is, and neither can the man who sold it to you, and neither can the house in Crown March, and the reason I cannot tell you is not that I do not want to.

"A rate may be read at a counter and a copy of a rate may not be produced. You have been told that already, by somebody, in a courtyard, and you did not believe it, and it is true. What you are holding is not a rate. It is a claim on a rate and a claim is not a rate and there is no box anywhere in this city for a claim, and a man with a seal can read a rate to you for nothing and cannot give you a paper with the figure on it, and that is not a trick, it is the shape of the law, and it has been the shape of the law for longer than either of us has been alive.

"So there is no way for you to prove what you bought. Not this year. Not to a court, not to a factor, not to the office that took your money. And the reason is the good reason. Because if a man could produce a copy of a rate, then a rate could be sold twice, and the district would be pursued by two parties for one sum, and everybody in the basin would learn in about a year that a roll is a thing you can hold in your hand and take to market. It is deliberately built so that you cannot."

"Then I have paid four hundred and ten pounds for a thing I cannot prove I own," said Josip Malnar.

"You have paid four hundred and ten pounds for a thing that may well be worth four hundred and ten pounds," said Marek, "and you have paid it honestly and you were described it honestly and there is nothing wrong with anything anybody did. And I am not able to make it worth anything. That is the honest end of it and I would rather say it in your yard than in a room in Bell Stair with a form on the table, because in a room in Bell Stair I would be able to do about a tenth of what I would look like I could do."

---

The wind came over the top of the terrace and went over them both, and the lamp in the window moved, and the old woman inside did not come out.

"Now say the other thing," said Malnar, "because you have been not saying one thing for about ten minutes and I know what your face does, because I have watched a man from the low ground read a schedule out loud in a kitchen on this road in the spring and it was the same face."

"Two things."

"Take them in order."

"The first is that the low ground's ratepayers have assigned their collection rights to somebody else since you bought that paper, and the somebody else has bundled it, and the bundle is being sold in this city to houses that do not buy Morrowfen paper. Which means the thing you bought has been sold out from under you by people you have never met, lawfully, and you cannot stop it, and there is nobody you can complain to, and it is not fraud and it is not even anybody's fault, and I have wanted to say the last part of that sentence out loud for a fortnight."

"And the second."

"The second is that you are part of something. Not as a wrongdoer. As a piece of a thing. Your paper is a step in a line of steps that starts with five households in Rainmarket and ends somewhere I am not going to say, and I have counted to four and I have stopped at four and I am not going to count further in a year. And I am telling you that because you are fifty years old and you bought a thing you could not read and somebody ought to have told you what you had bought, and nobody did, and I have not got a way of telling you that makes it better, because the thing I would have to tell you with is a schedule and you have not read the schedule and I would not hand you mine either."

Malnar looked at the terrace for a while.

"Then what do you want of me," he said. "Because you have come up a road that takes a day to say two sentences and you have not asked me for anything and you have not put a paper in front of me, and I am a man who is used to being asked for things."

"I want you to know one number," said Marek. "Forty-one days. Is that what you have pledged this year?"

Malnar put his head back against the press frame.

"Forty-one days of my own pruning," he said, "to Harrow and Sons out of the Crown March road, to put a crew of four on this ground in the last ten days of the growth month, which is the only ten days a year a graft goes in properly, and the settlement is the forty-one days and they are due on the eleventh of the growth month and collected out of my own year, and the witnesses are the miller at the bridge and a man from the low ground who sells lime and did not get paid by me, and it is a good contract and it is the only money in this basin that has come to me in three years and I got it because of the trees and not because of anything else."

"Do you understand the settlement," said Marek.

"I understand that I have sold forty-one days out of my own back and they want them between the eleventh of this month and the second of the next," said Malnar, "and that if I die in the meantime they want them out of my estate, and that the factor has been perfectly clear about that, and wrote it down twice, and that I have read that page twice and the rest of it never, because the rest of it is the part about a crew and a graft and I know what a crew is."

---

They put the baskets down. The frost had gone off the terrace wall and the lane between the strips had gone the colour of a road.

"Malnar," said Marek, "I am going to do a thing in ten days that I have no business doing and that nobody has asked me to do, and I am telling you about it in advance, and I am not asking you anything, and I am not going to be able to tell you afterwards either, and I would rather you heard the shape of it from me now than found out about it in a yard from a stranger.

"I am going to try to hold a settlement for a few days. Not your forty-one. Those are paid and the work is done and a contract you have kept is a contract, and there is nothing in it to hold. Something else, on this ground, on the eleventh, and it will not be about the trees.

"And when I do it, you will lose what you lose, and I will not be able to put any of it back, and you will not know that I was anywhere near it, and I would like you to be in a position where it never occurs to you to look for me, because if you look for me and I am not there, that is a different kind of harm to a man with four hundred and ten pounds in the wrong thing than the four hundred and ten pounds is."

Josip Malnar looked at him for a while.

"You will not get it back," he said.

"No."

"Then why are you telling me."

"Because a man who has been bought ought to be told which way the water is running before it arrives, and because I have decided I am not going to do it quietly, and I would like one person in this basin to know in advance that I am going to try, and I have decided that it is going to be you, and I am aware of what that makes you, and you are the fourth, and I have told you that and I have not told you the other three and I am not going to, and you can ask me in a year."

He did not say anything else. He picked the basket up and carried it down to the press and put it on the heap and came back and sat down again, and after a while he said, in the ordinary voice of a man with a hundred and forty trees and a factor to see on the eleventh,

"You will be here on the second of the month, then, if you are going to be here at all, and I would not send you away, and there is a room over the press and it is not clean and the bed is a bench. Bring boots. You will not need a coat on this road in the last ten days of the month, you will need to be up before the light."
