# Chapter 58 — A Shower in the Wrong Place

The shower came over the ridge on the ninth of the month at about the fourth bell in the afternoon and went down the high ground in one long grey curtain, four miles off, where a man could stand and watch it arrive, and by six o'clock the ridge was the colour of a wet road and the low ground was the colour of a beaten dish.

That is all it was. There was no second shower. Nothing fell on the low ground on the ninth, and the low ground had been dry since the twenty-eighth of the rains and the seed was in the ground in nine hundred and forty acres of it, and a district could be favoured and disfavoured in the same season by the difference of four miles and a line of country, which is not a mystery and is not a crime, and is the thing the tower spends.

Marek walked out of Rainmarket at first light with a pair of boots he had borrowed from Tomas and no paper at all, and he was on the low ground by seven, and the ninth was the day he had been told to come.

---

Kade had been there since the fourth.

She had the whole of the Share's membership out by the second bell, not as a roll but as sixty-one columns in a wet notebook with a date and four marks in each, and she had done it in the ordinary way: on the ground, at the door, in the rain, one household at a time, four questions and no fifth question.

*How much of the seed did you sow. How much of the seed came up. What did you sow in the ground under it. And who in this house signed the paper, and did you know that was what it was.*

She did not offer him the notebook and she did not let him hold it, and when he said, at the eleventh, that he could take the columns down, she said:

"No."

"I am not the ward's method and I am not the Chamber. I am a man with no office and a wet book."

"You are a man who has been let into this work for five days because you are useful, and there is a paper at your place with your name on the second-eye line, and if one of these sixty-one women puts a figure in that book that a hall has been holding, then a hall has been holding a figure that was taken in a kitchen door by a man with no office." She put the notebook inside her coat. "I have been doing this for six years and the only rule that has never failed me is that the person who asks is never the person who keeps it. I learned that in a village where a clerk's boy wrote down what a woman's neighbours told him and the neighbours found out in a day."

At the second bell they were at the bench.

---

Nine trays.

Mirren Fisk had them on the long bench under the oiled paper and the paper was off, and there was nothing in nine of them but river sand, and the smell of it had gone from sour to sweet, which is the smell of a tray that has failed and has stopped being interesting to anybody. And all nine of the nine were off the same stock, which is the oldest kept-back seed in the basin, and it came out of a shed at the back of the seed house that was under four feet of water in the flood year and was dried out afterwards by being shut up in June, and that is not a mystery and Fisk said so before anybody asked her.

Fisk went down the line with the marker and touched the end of each tray and said the number out loud in the ordinary voice of a woman doing a job at the end of a day: *four, eleven, nineteen, twenty-six, thirty-one, thirty-three, forty, forty-four, fifty-one.*

"Sixty-one trays," she said. "Nine of them."

Sera Holt had come out from the low ground clinic at the second bell because Fisk had sent for her at the seventh and Sera Holt has never in her life not come when she is sent for. She stood at the end of the bench with her hands behind her back, in the way of a physician who has decided not to touch anything.

"Nine out of sixty-one is one in seven," she said. "Say that again in a different shape, because that is the shape that is going to be repeated in every kitchen between here and the ridge inside a fortnight. One household in seven. It is not one household in seven, it is nine households, and there is no number in the world that makes it better, and every one of them knows today which nine."

"And the other fifty-two."

"The other fifty-two have seed that will come up." She put one finger on the end of tray nineteen without touching the sand. "And the nine will not, and I want the next thing said carefully, because you are all going to want to make these nine trays and this afternoon's weather into one fact, and I do not yet know whether they are one fact or two, and neither does she." She nodded at the seed house. "A tray of seed does not fail because a shower went over a ridge four miles off. A tray of seed fails because somebody shut a shed up wet in the flood year. Both of those have happened to this Share in the same nine days and nobody in this basin will ever be able to take them apart, and a man in a good chair in a room in the ninth month will say which one it was, and he will say it with a figure in front of him and not with a tray." And the difference between those two sentences is a whole season, and the season is not a feeling, it is a date, and the date is when you may sow a second crop, and the second crop is not a rescue of the first, it is a different crop with a different risk, and there is a frost at the end of this month that has not arrived yet and that will arrive in the last week regardless of anything anybody signs."

She said the next part to Marek and not to Fisk, and she said it quietly.

"Now tell me what a household that planned on sixty-one shares does when nine of them are sand. It does not lose nine parts in sixty-one. It loses the threshing share, because the share is a sixth of what comes off, and a sixth of nothing is nothing. It loses the labour it hired and paid for out of a share that will not come, and the labour was four men for nine days and they were paid on the tenth. It loses the second sowing. And it puts the household on the district for the difference between what it spent and what it will get, in a month when the district is already short, and the district's answer to that is a rate." She took her hand off the tray. "That is a body's calendar, Mr Vale, and not a season's. A man who has sowed a second time on the twelfth of a month and a year is nine years away from a bad knee has a bad knee nine years earlier than he was going to have it. I have been writing that sentence for twenty-six years and I have never once been able to make anybody hear it at the rate call."

---

Kade came back at the sixth with her notebook still inside her coat and her face doing something Marek had not seen on it before and could not read at first.

She had found Ansgar Drel's household at half past two, at the top of the rise, on the high ground, where a man of seventy-eight had come out into his own yard to tell a woman with a wet book that he had not signed the paper, and had said why, and the why was that he had taken a hundred and forty off a factor for a quarter of his kept-back stock in the bad spring and had not signed a thing since and was not going to. And when she said the word *stock* he had asked her, without any particular bitterness, whether she had heard about the nine trays, and then had not waited for the answer, and had gone back inside, and she had stood in his yard for a minute afterwards and had not been able to think of one question to put to a man who had just lost the oldest line of seed in the basin and had not signed a piece of paper that was offered to him in a lane in the rain.

"The one house in the whole Share that did not take it," said Kade, "has seed in the ground that is up. All of it. Ansgar Drel's ground is on the high side of the rise and the high side of the rise got the curtain at the fourth bell and his is the best in the district." She was walking fast and not looking at him. "I have been walking for two days waiting to find a household that is better off for not having signed, and I found it, and it is a man of seventy-eight who sold his seed to a factor for a price that was fair when he sold it."

"Say the name of the household that took it," said Marek, because he wanted to hear whether she had got to it.

"Iren Orleb's," said Neris Kade. "Two hundred yards from where we are standing, on the flat, with the gate he cannot afford to have mended, and the curtain went over the ridge and not over him, and it will go over the ridge next season as well for as long as his ground is the ground the instrument names. I have been pleased for forty minutes and I am not going to be pleased. Being pleased about that is the same as being pleased about a good year, and I have watched men in a lane be pleased about a good year."

She stopped at the top of the rise, where you can see the whole flat mile of it and the ridge behind it with the weather still standing on it.

"Now I have to say the rest of it," she said, "and I would rather say it here than in front of a woman of thirty-four with nine trays of sand behind her. The paper did not do that. The paper has been out four days and it did not call that shower and it could not have, and I have been wrong this week and I have been wrong in the way that people are wrong when they have been right about something for six years. That clause is four years old. Two witnesses signed it and one of them was a seed merchant's widow who is alive and lives in the third house along this road. The Share's own minute book records the clause being read out and carried on a count of hands, and forty hands went up for it, and nineteen of the sixty-one were not in the room to put a hand up at all, and every hand that went up was a hand the Share had asked to put somebody else's season behind."

"Who signs for a cooperative?"

"Every member signs for the cooperative if the cooperative is a party to the instrument, which it is, because the low ground is named." She said it patiently. "Sixty-one names on one seal, Mr Vale. That is not a smallholder's contract. That is a body corporate pledging a thing on behalf of sixty-one people who were in a room when it happened."

"Then nobody hid it from anybody."

"Nobody hid it from anybody," said Neris Kade. "That is the part I have been walking two days about, and it is worse than the other part, and I do not have a technique for it, and I have a notebook with sixty-one columns in it and no question left to ask any of them."

They stood on the rise. Down below, four hundred yards off, a woman of thirty-four was marking nine trays with a piece of chalk so that a stranger in ten days would know which ones had failed, which is the practice at that bench, and which the tower has nothing to do with.

"Vale. What is a counterweight called when nobody is named?"

"You have said this to me once."

"I have said a version of it to you once, on the ninth, in a shed, and I said then that I had not said it out loud to anybody and would rather you did. You did not." She looked at him steadily. "So I will say it in full, once, and then I am not going to say it again this season. It has been called a district for three hundred years. Every season loan in this basin with a sequence clause in it has called it a district, and the district has never once been asked, because there is no box. And the people who drafted those clauses were not crooks, and the people who signed them were not fools, and the clerks who sealed them were not careless — they were all doing the correct work of a rule that puts the cost in a place that cannot object." She took the notebook out of her coat and put it back. "Somebody has to be the first person who says the word out loud in a room. That is all. It is not a programme. I have watched men with programmes."

Marek had no paper on him, which had been the plan and which was now the whole difficulty, because he wanted to write it down and the only thing he had was the inside of his own coat.

He took out the recertification list, folded it once, and on the back of it, in pencil, in the standing of a man who has no form and no office and one list that is a fortnight old, he wrote four words and a full stop.

*A district is not a person.*

Then he folded it again and put it away, and it was in his coat that night and the four nights after that, and it was not evidence, and it was not a document, and there was no box in the city for a sheet of paper with four words on it and a man's hand at the bottom, and he was aware of all three of those things while he was doing it, and did it anyway, which was a new thing, and it did not feel like anything at all.

Behind them, on the high ground, the curtain had gone off the ridge and the sky over the low ground was the colour of the inside of a dish and was going to be that colour for another nine days.
