# Chapter 79 — Nobody Is Holding It

The twentieth day of the growth month, and a girl of sixteen came up the low-ground road at the second bell with a letter in a flat board of her own making, because it had come out of a hired basket with a crease in it that went through the word *cooperative*, and she had a coat that was her mother's and a hood she did not need and had been given anyway.

She asked for Marek Vale in the seed house yard, and Fisk said he was not there and had not been there since the sixth, and the girl said she knew, and had been told.

"I have got a thing to do and I would like you to come and stand in the room," she said. "That is all. You do not have to say anything."

"Where."

She told him, and she told it in the wrong order. The wrong order was that there is a room in the Red Uplands four terraces up where a man stands in it and answers questions in words and not out of a paper, and nothing is charged and nothing is signed, and two of them had been in it in the second week of this month, and anybody may go in and sit and look.

---

The Threshold is a hall with six intake windows and four rooms behind it, and the third of the rooms has been the third of the rooms since before the causeway was built, and it is eight feet by fourteen with a table planed soft down the middle and a rail round three sides and a window with a wire in it and a bell on the keeper's side that is not rung for time.

Sabina Krela was fifty-one and had kept it twenty-nine years and had a face like a woman who has listened to a great deal in a small room and got economical.

"Terms first," she said, to the room, and then said the whole of it again slower to the girl, which she had not been going to do, and Marek watched her decide to. "Anything brought to be sealed, sealed at the front table and after the bell, and the two witnesses stand on the rail and do not speak while the terms are read. Anything said on the record is said after the bell and read back. Nothing in this room is sworn, because there is no oath in this room and anybody who tells you there is has been in a different building. And a person is not charged to come into it."

The girl put the letter on the table and did not let go of it.

"I want this read," she said, "and I want something else. I want it written down that I have been on a rate since I was born, and that it is on my mother's ground, and that nobody has ever asked me whether I mind."

Krela did not write anything down, and that is the first thing and it is the thing the whole chapter is.

"Right," she said. "Now three things, and you will hate the second one, and the third one is not mine, it is the law, and I have been saying it in this room since before you were born.

"First. I can write that you said that, today, in this room, at the third bell, in front of me and the man on the rail, in your own words. That is a true thing that happened in a room. It goes in the day book, and the day book has no heading on it, because it is not a heading, it is a list of what people came for.

"Second. I cannot write that you have been on a rate. A room witnesses things that happen inside it. Your hand on that table happened in this room. A rate that was entered in a shed four miles off in the seed month did not happen in this room, and no man alive can make it happen in this room, and if I wrote it down you would have a piece of paper with a figure's worth of words on it and not one thing in it that anybody would have to answer to. You are angry, and you are entitled to it, and it will not make this room better, and I have had a man of sixty sit exactly where you are sitting and say the identical thing to me about a roof, and there was nothing wrong with his roof."

"Third. Do you want it read back."

"Yes."

Krela wrote for four minutes in a hand taught in a school that no longer exists, and read it back twice, the second time slower. Wenna Drel corrected it twice, both times by saying *that is not what I said*, and Krela made the corrections in the margin in a smaller hand and initialled them, which is a thing a witness room does and no other room in the city does.

Then she rang the bell with her thumb.

---

"What is it called," said Wenna Drel.

"Called what."

"The thing that is doing it. The letters say counterweight and do not say it in a way anybody can say out loud. So what is it called, when nobody is named."

Marek Vale stood on the rail with a queue of nine in the passage behind him and understood that this was the only room in the city that would ever ask him a question he could answer, and that there was no form on which the answer could be entered, and that every other person in this hall had a better reason for being here than he did.

"I am going to say it once," he said, "and it is not for writing down, and if that bell goes while I am saying it I will stop."

Nobody rang it.

"A counterweight is a thing somebody is holding up. That is the whole of the word. It has meant that for nine hundred years and it will go on meaning it in a bay full of mud.

"You have got a sequence and the sequence is wrong. The season that was favoured was the wrong season, or the wrong thing was favoured, or somebody moved the order of one thing past another thing and did not tell the person it moved past. To make the arithmetic come out you have to hang the wrongness on something else, so the books balance and nobody has to admit to a mistake. And to hang it, it has to be up there. Somebody has to be holding it up.

"And if the name is a person, then the person is in the room. You have to name it to them, and that is the whole mechanism and not a courtesy, because being named is the only way of being in the room, and being in the room is the only way anybody can refuse, and a refusal is what voids the instrument. That is the design. It is a good design. It is the only reason nobody in this basin has ever been ruined by a thing they did not know about.

"And a thing nobody is holding does not come down."

He stopped there. The window with the wire in it had a fly in it and the fly went up and came down and went up.

"That is what a counterweight is when nobody is named. It is not a weight. It is a thing up in the air with no hand on it. And it has been up since before this city had a hall, and it will be up in the ninth month, and in the ninth month after that, and there is no office in this kingdom that can put a hand on it, because the only thing in this kingdom that can put a hand on it is a person, and a person is what has been left out of the box."

Nobody said anything.

"And I have been looking for the word since the fourth of this month and I want to give you the one we have all been using without noticing. We have been calling it the charge. Harrow said it in a room on the twenty-third of the seed month. You have all said it. The four words in the boxes on that form are four words for a charge — a levy, a schedule, a posting, a return — and a fifth has been going into a fifth box for eight years because there was nowhere else to put it. That is five words in this city for a thing a person is supposed to be holding up, and not one of the five is a word for a person."

He put his hand flat on the letter, on the crease, which was not a thing you do to somebody's own paper, and the girl let him.

"So when you ask me what it is called, the honest answer is that it is called a charge, and the whole difficulty of this month is that a charge is a word for something somebody has to have paid, and nobody has to have been asked, and there is nowhere in this city to write down that a person was asked and said no."

---

The keeper wrote the girl's own words in and nothing else, and did not touch the rail.

Marek did not say the sentence again. He had said it once and it was in the room and it was going nowhere, and that was the arrangement.

Then he did the thing he had been not doing all month, which was to think about the shed on the low-ground road and about a bench along a wall and about a column a hand's width wide with nothing at the head of it, there for a month, over which a man with a licence had sent two painters home rather than put a wrong word on.

He could have walked forty feet down that lane in six minutes and put a heading at the top of it, and it would have been a good heading, and it would have been true, and about two hundred people a day go past that board who have never been asked anything by anybody and would have read it in four seconds and gone on to the thing they came for.

He did not do it, and he had gone over the reason nine times between the causeway and the room and got the same answer every time, and the answer is not modesty and it is not that he has nothing to put in it.

A heading is a promise to the people who read the words under it: whoever comes after me will know what these are about. And you may only make that promise if you were there on the day the words were said, and if there is one person alive who remembers the day.

And there is no day. Forty hands went up in a shed in the bad autumn for a thing that had not been read, and four hundred people in a yard said no to a number they were not shown the working of, and the Share wrote fifty-two letters last week that nobody is obliged to answer, and the second time round the doors a household that said yes five weeks ago said no in the doorway before either of them had asked anything. He was not on the day. Neither was the yard. Neither is anybody in Bell Stair.

"There is a bench in a shed on this road with a column on it with nothing at the head of it," he said, because she was the only person in the room who had asked him a question. "It has been empty a month. I know what goes in it. I am not going to be the one to write it, and here is the reason and it will not sound like anything: there is no day. A heading is a thing you may only write if you were there, and I was not, and that is the whole of it, and I have been carrying it about for a month as though it were a thing that needed somewhere to go."

"Then it can stay empty," said Wenna Drel, which was not an argument and was not meant as one, and Marek understood that a girl of sixteen had disposed of six weeks of his thinking in nine words and had not known that was what she was doing.

---

They read the letter out. It took nine minutes and Krela read it in the words of the Share and did not improve one of them, and two men who had come in to bond a debt to each other stood at the rail and listened to the whole of it, because there was nowhere in eight feet by fourteen to stand that was not in the way of the reading.

When it was finished the girl put her thumb on the crease.

"That is a true thing, and it is nineteen lines, and it is going to go yellow and go soft in that basket, and I do not know how to ask anybody for a book."

"You can have a copy of your own words out of my day book," said Krela. "Free. It will be ready on the second day of next month with your name on it and the day on it, and you may do what you like with it, and it will not be worth anything in a room."

"I know."

"You do not know that and I do not either, and the day I find out which of us was right will be the more interesting of the two." She closed the day book and put a weight on it, which is a thing a keeper does. "I will tell you what a day book is, because you will think it is more than it is. It is a book about a room. It is my book about my room. It shows that on the twentieth of the growth month a person said a thing in here at the third bell. It does not show whether the thing was so, and it never will, and in about sixty years it will be a shelf of books nobody can get anything out of except me and whoever has my job."

"Then what is it for."

"It is for the person who said it," said Sabina Krela.

And Marek understood, standing on the rail of a room with a bell that had been rung once that day, that this was the only answer available to him anywhere in MR 413, and that it was not enough, and that it was not nothing, and that those two are not the same size and that he would have to carry both of them into the ninth month.

---

They went back across the causeway in the rain, the ordinary rain at the end of a growth month, and the girl had her hood up and said nothing for two hundred yards.

"You said it once and you did not say it twice. Why."

"Because a thing said twice in a room is a thing being taught, and I am not a man who teaches."

"Which of the five is the right one."

"I do not know," said Marek Vale. "And it will not matter, and that is what I have had to learn in six weeks. If the fifth box is a return, then it is a return, and there is a form for a return, and it will be administered correctly and the woman will be handed a figure on a piece of paper by somebody doing their job. If it is a charge against a district, then there is no form and there never will be one, and that is worse, and it is the same money."

"Which is it."

"Both. It goes in that box on the footing that it is a return and it does the work of a charge on a district, and no office in this city has it as its job to notice the two things in one box."

He said that to a girl of sixteen on a causeway in the rain, and then stood there and worked out, about four steps later, that he had just said the truest thing he had said in six weeks to the one person in Morrowfen who could not take it into a room, which was the Tuesday again, the third or fourth of them, unarranged and nobody in it meaning it.

---

At the low ground they came past the shed lit inside, and there were nine on the lane at the seventh bell and about six of them had come to find out whether a letter had come for them, and it had, because Merrow took the fifty-two on the nineteenth and entered the fact of them in a second hand in a book, and the seventh column was still bare at the head of it and about four people were standing in front of it not knowing what they were looking at, which is the only condition that has kept it bare for a month.

Marek did not go in. He walked past it with a girl who had come four miles in the rain to be told a thing could not be entered, and at the end of the lane he said that he would be at the seed house in the morning, and that he was not doing anything else for three days, and did not know what for.

He had said a sentence out loud in a room. It was in a book, in an ordinary hand, with the day on it, and under it in the margin in a smaller hand and initialled was a correction made by a girl who said *that is not what I said*.

There was no office under it, and there would not be one this year, and nobody had thanked him, and the ninth month was five weeks off, and on the morning of the twenty-first he was going to stand in a seed house yard and watch a rate go onto eleven pieces of paper with nothing at the head of them, and there was not one thing in the city he could do about that, and the sentence he had got would not touch it, and he had known that before he said it out loud, which was the first honest thing about the whole of the two holds.
