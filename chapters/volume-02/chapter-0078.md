# Chapter 78 — Forty of Forty-One

The Share met on the seventeenth of the growth month at the second bell, in the seed house, standing, because there are forty-one people in that building and there are eleven chairs in it and the chairs are for the old and the bench is for the sacks.

Nobody had called it. It was announced in the shed on the ninth, on the board over the door, in the ordinary way the Share announces things, and the announcement said *the question* and then set out the question in four lines, and the four lines were on a slate and the slate hung on a nail by the bench and about four hundred people came up the low-ground road to look at it over two days, and none of them were members, and the yard that keeps it is the yard it has always been.

The question was this.

*Whether the Share shall write to each of the fifty-two members who have signed an assignment of a rate arrears, telling them in the words of the instrument what they signed, and asking them to do whatever they think is right about it.*

Fisk put it up, and the four lines were hers, and she had spent two days getting them to that length, and there were four things it deliberately did not say. It did not say *withdraw*. It did not say *buy back*. It did not say *the Compact*. And it did not say *repay*.

---

The argument took an hour and it was not about the letters, because everybody in the shed had understood the letters by the eighth minute and the whole of the rest of it was about a sack.

Bessok Tan was twenty-nine and second-generation and had been the Share's secretary for four years and kept the roll, which is a piece of oiled board with sixty-one lines on it and a line of nails at the top, and he had brought the board and a length of string and a slate, and he stood at the bench end with the string in his hands for an hour while forty people argued past him.

He asked his question at the end of the hour and it was the only thing said in that shed that day that had not been said before in one of the other rooms in this basin, and it is a question about arithmetic and it has no feeling in it at all.

"Whose hand is a hand," he said.

Somebody said it was his.

"It is mine and it is not the question. The question is: we carry on a count of hands, the instrument was carried on a count of hands, and the fourth clause of the instrument names a district, and nobody in this shed thinks that is the same thing, and I want to know whether we can say that out loud or whether we have just been lucky." He held up the string. "This roll has sixty-one lines on it. Every line is a household. There are four hundred and something people in those households and about a third of them are over twenty and are not the person whose name is on the line, and two of the households on that board are a woman and her mother and one of them is a mother and a daughter of sixteen, and the daughter is on the board because the mother is, and she has drawn seed and filled cones since the third of last month and has never once put a hand up in this room in her life.

"So when we count hands in here, are we counting households, or are we counting people, and does the instrument know the difference, and did the instrument care?"

Nobody answered for a while. Fisk said, "A member is a household."

"Then a member's hand is a household's hand and there are sixty-one of them and the Secretary is one, and I have counted the hands in this room for four years and I have said sixty-one and I have said it out loud, and I have never once said *households*."

"You have said members," said Fisk.

"I have said sixty-one," said Bessok Tan. "And a number is a thing you can carry into a room by somebody who was not in the yard, and I would like to be in here when somebody says the number."

---

And then a girl of sixteen at the back, near the door, where she had been standing the whole time because she always stands by the door, said:

"Have any of you read it."

The shed went quiet in a particular way, which is the way forty people go quiet when they have all been asked the same question at different times and have all given the same answer.

"I have not," said Wenna Drel. "I have drawn seed off you since the third and I have filled cones and I have been in this shed every day of the sowing and I have never read the paper and I did not know until the ninth of last month that there was a clause in it, and then a man from Bell Stair read a bit of it out loud in here and told me that it was not the threshing share."

Somebody said, "It is not the threshing share."

"Then what is it," she said, "and how many pages, and is it in this building."

"It is five leaves and a schedule and it is folded twice and the clause is on the back of the second leaf and the schedule is at the back of the fifth," said Fisk, "and it is in that chest and it has been in that chest since the bad autumn."

"Then you have all read it."

"Forty of us have read it in the last month," said Fisk. "I have read it four times. I did not read it until the third of this month and I have kept this house for eleven years."

"That is what I would have said," said Wenna Drel, and stopped, and stood by the door with her hands behind her back, and did not ask for anything, and it was twenty minutes before she asked for anything, and what she asked for was whether the fourth line of the schedule was the one with the receiving party on it, and Fisk said she did not know, and the girl said she had heard a man from Bell Stair say there were four, and Fisk said, in the voice of a woman who has decided to answer a question rather than not answer it, "There are fifty-two, and I will not look at it in front of you, and that is the honest answer and it is not a good one, and the reason I will not look at it in front of you is that the minute I open that book in this shed I am doing it because a girl of sixteen asked me to and not because the Share decided to, and I would like it to be the second thing."

---

The letters were argued about for another half hour, and the argument was not about the letters, and at the fourth bell Bessok Tan read out the one paragraph that the letters all say, and there is one paragraph, and it is nineteen lines long, and it is a good paragraph and he wrote it and nobody improved it.

*I am a member of the Elmsworth Seed Share. I signed a line assigning the collection right in a rate arrears. The line was one of fifty-two. Behind those fifty-two lines there is an instrument sealed in the bad autumn, and in the instrument there is a clause that names the Rainmarket low ground as the district that bears the failure of a season favoured. I have had this explained to me and I am setting it down in my own words and you may check every word of it at the seed house or at the shed on the low-ground road lane where anybody may ask and nothing is charged. I am not telling you what to do. If you want to know what you signed and nobody has told you, come and ask and we will read it to you. If you do nothing, that is a thing you may do and nobody in this shed will say a word about it to you.*

That is nineteen lines and it is the whole of what a shed can make, and Fisk said so before anybody else had worked it out, because she had been the one who refused the room for two days.

"You may read that back to me," said Iren Orleb, who was sixty-one and had been a member since the founding and whose gate he could not afford to mend, "and I will say one thing before you do, and then I will vote, and I will vote the same way either way so you may as well hear the thing.

"My line is dated the fourteenth of the second month. I know that because I have the counterfoil and I have had it since the bad spring, and the counterfoil is the only paper in this whole business that anybody ever gave me, and the counterfoil has got a date on it and I have looked at it about a hundred times and I worked out what the date meant about a month ago and I have not told anybody because I could not think of what anybody could do about it.

"The house in Morrowfen that bought my line did not have a house in Morrowfen on the fourteenth of the second month. There was no such house. There was a man with a ledger going about the low ground and a woman sat on a barrel in my kitchen and I signed where she put her finger, and she told me the truth, and I have had eight months to think about whether she did, and I have decided she did, and I would like somebody in this shed to understand that I have thought about it for eight months and I have not found the lie and I have stopped looking for it, and it is the not finding it that has cost me the season.

"So I am against the letters. Not because the letters are bad. Because they are good and they are the only good thing anybody has done about this and there is nothing on the face of the earth that obliges one person in one household to answer one letter, and I wrote my name on a line in the second month and a girl of sixteen has just told this shed that she has never read the paper, and if a letter does not get an answer, then in four years this shed will be forty-one people in a room with a piece of paper and no way at all of making anybody do anything, and that is not a thing you are going to fix with nineteen lines and my name at the top."

He voted against, and he voted against with a reason that nobody in the shed could improve on, and afterwards, in the lane, four other men said the same thing in about four different ways and it was not copying because nobody had heard each other.

---

The count was taken standing, the way it is always taken, and Bessok Tan had the string and the slate and the roll, and it is not a vote in any sense that the city would recognise and it is the only instrument this cooperative has ever had.

One hand against.

Forty hands for.

"There is forty," said Bessok Tan, "and there is forty-one in the room, and I am going to write it on the sack and I am going to write *of forty-one in the room* under it, and I am going to write the number of lines on the board that are not in this shed, and it is twenty, and I am going to write under that the number that were not in this shed the last time this instrument was carried, and that is twenty-one, and I am going to write both numbers because one of them has gone down and one of them has gone up and I have not got a way of saying what that means and I am not going to pretend I have."

He wrote it on the side of a seed sack in charcoal, the way the Share writes everything, and the sack is a thing anybody can pick up and turn round, and there are two sacks in the seed house and the number is on one of them and it is still on it.

"Forty is not a majority," he said, to nobody, writing. "Forty is forty hands in a shed. If somebody asks me in a room in the ninth month what the Share decided, I am going to say that forty people in a shed with one hand against decided to write fifty-two letters, and I am going to say what the letters say, and I am not going to say fifty-one to sixty-one or four in five or anything of that, and I would like it minuted that I said I would not, and the minute is the bench book and I keep it and it goes in the chest and nobody reads it."

---

The letters were written out over two days, nineteen lines each, on the Share's paper, and there were fifty-two of them and about four of them were written by Ansgar Drel, who is seventy-eight and takes four hours over a thing, and there was a hired basket and a boy with a cart.

And on the second day, at the fourth bell, a man in a good coat came up the low-ground road and stood at the side of the seed house with his hands behind his back and said that the shed on the lane would take the fifty-two letters and post them at the house's own cost, and would enter in his register, in the second hand, that a letter had been sent to each of the fifty-two on the seventeenth of the growth month and what it said, and that anybody could come and ask, and that nothing was charged for that either.

"Why," said Iren Orleb, from the step.

"Because in eight days' time fifty-two households are going to receive a letter from a cooperative they are members of, and forty of them will not have any idea what it is and eight of them will not be at the house they live in, and there is a bench in my shed with a column on it for exactly that," said Ovid Merrow, "and a person who cannot find out whether a letter came for them is a person who cannot find out what they signed, and the whole of my trade is that a person who cannot find out what they signed is a person who has already signed whatever it was."

Ilse Ferrow, who was standing in the lane with her arms folded and had not said anything for about four minutes, said, "That is not approved."

"I know it is not approved," said Ovid Merrow. "I have known it was not approved since the twenty-second of last month and it has cost the house a clerk's time and I have decided it is worth a clerk's time, and you are welcome to say so in the minutes of your own, and I would ask you to."

The shed agreed to it, and the fifty-two letters went into the shed on the lane in a hired basket on the nineteenth of the month, and the register has the fact of them in it, in a second hand, in the words of the Share.

And what the shed produced was fifty-two letters that nobody is obliged to answer, and one number in charcoal on the side of a seed sack, and a fact in a rival register that the Share could not have entered anywhere itself, and a rule's own office turned out to be the best office in the basin for this work, and not one office was closed and not one licence was withdrawn and nobody was convicted, and a shed is a place where a count of hands is a count of hands, and outside of a shed it is nothing at all.
