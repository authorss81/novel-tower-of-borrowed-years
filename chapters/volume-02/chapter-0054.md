# Chapter 54 — Ovid Merrow's Morrowfen House

The Sable Compact's Morrowfen house is one room over a chandler's shop on the Rainmarket side of the bridge, and the chandler moved out in the second year of the tenancy and the room kept the smell, which Marek noticed and Ovid did not, and the two clerks on the floor below it were the only people in the building who worked in it and neither of them came up.

Ovid Merrow had a good table under the window, three chairs at it, two against the wall, and a window that looked down the long flat mile of the low ground to the silt bank and the ridge, and the whole of the flood, and eleven weeks of a city that had gone back to being a place where things grew.

"Vale," he said. "Sit down. You have come on purpose and I have not got anything else this afternoon, which is a thing I am permitted to say because it is true and because you will find out the alternative from the man in the lane."

He was forty-seven. He offered a cup of something hot and did not press it. He had a licence on the wall, framed, and Marek read it from the chair because he could not help himself: the Sable Compact, house of Morrowfen, assignment and bundling, granted in the ninth year of the house, renewable at the year. Nine years. The same nine years the reserve carry had been going into a district account unapplied, and Marek noticed himself noticing and made himself stop, because a man who sits in a lender's room and starts seeing the lender everywhere has stopped looking.

"Two hours," said Merrow. "Ask me anything in the order you want it, and I will not manage you, and if I do not know I will say that I do not know, and you may write any of it down that you have a right to write down."

"That is a remarkable thing to offer a man from Bell Stair."

"It is a cheap thing to offer," said Merrow, "and it is cheap because I am not the only one who will tell you how this works. I am simply the only one in this basin who will tell you how this works while you are sitting down. If you had come in a fortnight there would be a young man here with a different version of the same method and no licence at all, and he would be right about most of it."

Marek had come in with four questions in the order he wanted them and had them in his mouth in about nine seconds, and he found that he had none of them.

"Start with the fourth clause," said Merrow, "because I can see it is the fourth clause."

"Section four recites a counterweight. The district named in the schedule bears the failure of the season favoured."

"It does."

"And a district is not a person."

"No." Merrow said it without any pleasure at all, which was the first thing about him that Marek had not expected. "It is not, and I am not going to stand here and pretend to you that I have invented that. I did not invent it, and I have not been able to get rid of it, and I have tried. Nine years. The practice is not ours. The practice is older than the practice, and it is written into the form, and it is very good drafting, and the reason it is good drafting is that a named counterweight is the only kind the tower will accept at all — an unnamed one is a gap and a gap is a shortfall and a shortfall lands on a district whether anybody named it or not. The drafter of that clause did the city a service. It moved the cost from nowhere to somewhere. And somewhere turned out to be a box with the word *district* in it, and there has never been a form on which a district is asked whether it minds."

"You have talked to an officer about this."

"I have talked to eleven officers in nine years," said Merrow, "and two of them told me that a district cannot be asked, and nine of them told me that a district is an administrative division, and the two who told me the first thing are dead or retired, and neither of them told me the first thing in writing, which I would have paid for."

"So the city hands you a district."

"The city hands me a district and then charges me for the water in it." He turned his cup round on the table without drinking from it. "I will make it plain, because you will not get a plainer version anywhere else in this city and I would like you to have it once from somebody who is paid to want you to have it. I do not lend to people. I do not lend to the Share either. I buy claims, and I put claims together, and I sell the putting-together. The Share's instrument is a good instrument. I could not have written a better one. Two witnesses, neither of them on my side or the Share's, and a pledge out of the members' own labour and their own seed, and a settlement out of a harvest that has not happened yet and which therefore costs nobody anything today." He stopped. "The bundle is not a loan. There is no borrower in my house. There is a schedule of fifty-two assigned claims with an aggregate on it and a rate, and a household that would rather buy the rate from me than go to the water office and be told it cannot pay one, and the schedule is a page and the page is one line, and that is the whole of the product."

"And the cost."

"The cost is the counterweight," said Merrow, "and the counterweight is the low ground, and the low ground is named, and the low ground is the ground the seed is going into, and I have never in nine years had a single member of the Share ask me about it, and not one of them has been kept from asking."

"Then you have told me the product has a cost your customers do not hear."

"I have told you the product is a line of paper with a rate on it, and I have told you the fourth clause of the instrument underneath it says that the district named in the schedule bears the failure of the season favoured, and I have told you that the schedule names the low ground, and the Share's secretary reads that clause out of the schedule at every meeting." He sat back. "Do you know what I do for a living, Mr Vale? I explain. There are four pages in front of a person in a lane and none of them says what is in the other four, and I can say what is in the other four, and I say it in the plainest words in the basin and I do it for nothing, and then I sell them the cheaper of the two prices. That is the business. If somebody else in this city had the same product and said it as plainly, I would be out of business in a year, and I have been out of business in a year before and I am still here."

He was not lying. Marek went over the causeway at the sixth bell and checked that four times in his head on the way, and the four checks all came out the same, and the thing that was waiting for him at the foot of the hill was not anger.

There was a card in his pocket. It had been in his pocket for two hours and he had not decided to take it, and Merrow had put it on the table beside the cup and said the ordinary thing about it: the house takes a clerk, not a man with an office, and a man with no office is not a man with nothing, and the work is the reading, and there is a figure, and the figure is more than the hall pays and will be more next year.

"I have not taken anything," Marek said at the door.

"You have taken a piece of card with an address on it. That is not taking anything; it is being given something that costs me fourpence. You will not come, and I do not expect you to come this week." Merrow stood up. "One thing, since you are about to cross that bridge and go up that hill and be the only man on it without a letter beside his name. I read the finding of the seventeenth. It is public; I buy them."

"I know."

"The clerk of the counter-desk is not you."

"No."

"You said so in the room and it is in the finding, and the finding also says that you signed a certificate that made the settlement due on the peak night, and that is a different paper in the same folder." Merrow put his cup down. "I am going to say the part that nobody in Bell Stair will say to you, and then I am going to let you go. A certificate is not a lie. Every office in this city issues certificates, and a certificate says *this is in force*, not *this is true*, and the whole of the honest machinery is built on that distinction, and there are men who work it and never think about it and they are not all cowards." He waited. "I do not say that to be kind. I say it because in about six months somebody is going to offer you the argument that you were only following a form, and it will not work, and you will know it will not work, and I would rather you heard the shape of it from me than from a person who wants something for it."

"Can I ask you something that is not about the fourth clause."

"You can ask me anything at all and there is no charge for it, and the ones I do not answer I will tell you I do not answer, and there is one of those and I will come to it if you get there."

Marek took his hand out of his coat pocket and left it there.

"You have a licence to buy claims," he said. "Who do you buy from?"

"Offices, estates, funds, and about forty households a year who would rather not sit in a queue." Merrow turned his cup round again. "And in nine years I have made two offers for the oldest reference volume in this city and both have been refused by the office that keeps it, and I am going to tell you why I am going to keep offering, because it is the only thing in this conversation I have not had and it is the reason I came to the window today rather than to your file."

"You cannot find a holder."

"I cannot find a *holder*," said Ovid Merrow. "That is the whole difficulty and it has taken me four years to say it in one sentence. A claim is a thing that somebody stands behind. That is what a claim is: a person, a house, an office, a fund, a district with an officer who signs for it. I do not care how old it is. I do not care how good the paper is. I cannot buy from nobody. There is a first page in that case with a single ruled line on it and nothing at the end of the line, and I have had a clerk look at it twice under a lamp and describe it to me, and the line has nothing in it, and so there is no one to sell it to me, and no matter what I offer there is nobody to accept it." He looked out of the window at the low ground for a moment. "And the office's own standing order says that no clerk may copy that page, so there is not a second one of it anywhere in the city either. It is the only document I have ever wanted that there is no second instance of."

"And the seals at the bottom of it."

"The clerk described them and I do not know what they are," said Merrow, "and I have had nine years to find out and I have not, and I have stopped asking, because every person I have asked has said one of two things: *I do not know*, or *you are not the sort of person that page is for*. Both of those are answers."

He turned round.

"Now. The reason I am telling you this is not a courtesy and it is not a threat, and I would like you to hear it as neither. There is a public register on Bell Stair and anybody may read it, and it has had a page in it this month that the public may read, and I do not know what is on that page and I have not asked and I will not, because I would rather have it in a year. But I will tell you what is going to happen to it, because it is a thing that happens and I have watched it happen to three other documents in this city and I have never once been able to stop it.

"A page anybody may read is a page somebody will copy. That is not a prediction, it is a habit: people copy what they are allowed to see, and they copy it into a place where other people may see it, and then there are two of it, and then the office that made the first one has a problem it cannot solve with a form. And when there are two of it, a claim that had nobody standing behind it has a piece of paper. And a piece of paper in a public book, with a line in it that has nothing in it and a name at the foot of it, is a thing I can put in a schedule." He put his hands flat on the table. "I do not know whose name is at the foot of it. I do not know whether it is a clerk's. It will not matter when it happens, and in about nine months it will matter more than anything else in this basin, and I will buy that page in a morning, and I will pay over the odds for it and I will not feel bad, because I will be the only man in the city who can."

The room was very quiet, and out of the window the low ground lay flat and grey under the weather coming off the ridge.

"The order for it has been nailed to the Tally Hall door since the fourth of last month," said Ovid Merrow, "and it is sealed, and the duty clerk reads it out twice, and about thirty people stop on the steps to hear her, and not one of them will say afterwards that they have read it, and I have watched it for a month and I could not work out whether that city is honest or frightened. I am not going to insult you by asking you to tell me what is on the page. I have said the thing I came to say."

Marek stood up and got as far as the door, and then stopped in the frame with his hand on the rail, because there was one more question in him and he had been in rooms where he did not ask it and he was not going to be in one again.

"What would you do with it."

Merrow looked at him for a moment and then stood up too.

"I would not sell it. I have thought about it for four years and that is the first honest answer I have given anybody about it, and you may write it down if you happen to have a form for it. I would enter it in a schedule as a claim with no counterparty, which is the only kind there is that nobody can audit, and then I would be able to point at it and say that the city owes it and that the city is the holder, and in about eleven years a man in a better coat than mine would take it and set it against a district." He picked his pen up and put it down again. "That is a worse thing than selling it. It is a much worse thing, and it is the reason I would rather not, and I have not been able to make myself not do it, and I would like you to take that as well, because you are the second person this year I have said it out loud to and the first was a chandler who told me I was mad."

He left the card on the table. Marek walked up the causeway with it in his hand instead of in his pocket, and read the figure on it three times on the bridge, and it was more than the hall paid, and there was no office attached to it, and he put it in the inside pocket of his coat where he kept the little book of promises he had made his mother, which was the worst place in the world to put it and which he chose on purpose, and told nobody, including himself in any words he would have to write down.

Then he sat on the step above the Tally Hall door, under a notice of the Trust's that has been on that door since the fourth and that about thirty people have read and no one will admit to, and he argued with himself for about a quarter of an hour.

The argument was this: a district is not a person, and every true thing the man had said in that room was in the instrument, and the instrument had been drafted by two witnesses and a cooperative of sixty-one people and a seed merchant's widow who cried about the February price, and the man in the good chair had not put a single false sentence in front of him in two hours.

And the answer, which he could not get round, was that a man who explains a thing honestly to a person in a lane has still made the other thing worth more, and there was no form anywhere in the city for saying that was wrong.

He got up and went in, and the letter beside his name was not there, and the list of twenty-two files was on the desk where he had left it, and the fourth line was the Share.

He had six days.
