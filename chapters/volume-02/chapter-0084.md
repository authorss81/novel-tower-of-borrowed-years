# Chapter 84 — Who May Write In It

The argument about the register took two days, and on the first day it was about who was allowed to look at it, and it took forty minutes, and the forty minutes was the shortest forty minutes of Marek Vale's summer.

The Gate yard on the fourteenth day of the harvest month, at the first bell, about a hundred and forty people, standing, in the cold, with the shutter off the tank because the shutter had a chain on it and the chain had been broken since the ninth of the seed month and nobody in Tallow Ward repaired it.

"Anybody may come," said Milan Vray, inside a minute. "Four hours a day. Nothing charged. Nobody asked to sign anything to come in. That is Merrow's rule and it is our rule and I did not get up this morning to spend four hours arguing about it."

Four hours a day was agreed. Nothing charged was agreed. Not being asked to sign anything was agreed with a small argument about whether a person who wanted to be asked should be written down as a person who wanted to be asked, and it was decided that a person who wants to be on a list has to be on a list, and the list is the yard's own, and it is not the register's, and the register has no list.

Then somebody at the back said the thing that turned the morning.

"The woman at the weighbridge," he said. "Brel. Forty-six. She cannot read and she cannot write and she has been to a water office counter twice this year with her husband and both times he has said the figure out loud for her, and he is dead since the flood year. What does she do when she is standing in front of her own line."

The yard went quiet in the way it did about four times a summer.

---

Marta Brel came up the causeway at about the third bell on her own, which nobody had asked her to do and which Sanne Droll was later quite clear she should not have done, and stood at the back of the yard with a shawl over her head and let somebody else speak for about a minute and then said it herself.

"I can tell you what a rate is," she said. "I have been in this yard four times and I have answered their four questions and I have written the answer on the sheet with a charcoal, because the keeper held it and I put my thumb where she said. That is the whole of what I do and I have never once pretended to be able to do anything else.

"So if somebody reads my line out to me in words I will know what it says. And I cannot write it down, and I cannot write my name, and I have been in this city thirty-one years and I have never been asked anybody's name so that I could write it down later."

"Who will come with you," said Kern.

"Nobody," said Marta Brel. "That is the whole of the trouble and it took me three months to be able to say it in a yard."

Somebody said, in a voice that the yard recognised and would remember: *Then somebody has to be able to come with you.*

And Aldis Kern said, "That is the yard's rule and it has been the yard's rule since the fifth month of the year and it is on the sheet you printed on the ninth, and the rule is that a sheet does not go to a third person's house.

"And the point of that rule is not that we do not trust the third person. The point is that a sheet in a third person's house is a sheet somebody can come and take, and there is no telling afterwards who read it. So a second person may come into the room with her and be named at the counter, and the second person is not the keeper and does not get a hand on anything, and if the second person is lying about what they were told the reader does not know that and cannot find out, and that is a thing we have known since the rains and have not found a way round, and I am not going to invent one at a tank."

"Then how do I do it," said Marta Brel.

"You do it exactly the way you did it at the trestle," said Kern. "You say it, somebody holds the charcoal, and what you said is what goes down. And the rule that stops anybody improving it is the yard's rule and it is the second line of the thing you printed, and it is the only line in it that anybody in this city has ever been able to enforce."

---

There was a man at the back of the yard with a blank sheet of Chamber paper and a carpenter's pencil, cutting as people talked, and he had been cutting for an hour and had said almost nothing, and by the fourth bell he had four ruled lines on it and no boxes at all.

His name was Sedge and he was a third-grade clerk and he had been on that hill long enough that twice in it, and he had come down the hill on a morning when he was not clerk of the day for anything, and Marek Vale had not asked him to come and Sedge had not said that he was coming.

"I am going to say what I have done and what I have not done," Sedge said, to the yard, "and then I am going to ask one question, and then I would like to go back up the hill.

"Lines, not boxes. That is deliberate and I will defend it and you may argue with me. A box is a thing a person can be put in. A line is a thing a person has to write in. Every bad instrument I have ever had to file in this building got filed in a box, and every bad instrument anybody has ever put in front of me this summer had a box on it with a word at the top of the box, and the word was a kind, and the kind was chosen by whoever cut the form and not by whoever is standing in it.

"Four lines. The first is the thing. The second is what the person behind it said, in their words. The third is who holds the other end and since when. The fourth is the words anybody else had to say about it, and there may be nothing on the fourth, and that is correct.

"And I have not put a heading on it, and before somebody says it, that is not a protest. The heading is going to be the tank and the date, because the yard has told me in writing that if there is no name at the foot of it then nothing can be traced to a hall, and because the yard wrote those four lines at that tank on the twentieth of the rains month and I am not going to cut them into a form and pretend to a person in a room in town that I thought of them."

"You have copied them," said somebody.

"I have copied them," said Sedge, "and I have never met the yard, and I did not know they existed until the sixth of this month, and I want that on this sheet in my hand and not in yours, because in a year somebody will ask who wrote the rule and there are going to be two answers in this city and I would like the smaller one to be true."

---

The question he asked took about four seconds and it stopped the yard cold.

"The second line is what the person behind it said. Singular. It says *the person*. I wrote the word and I chose it and I am not sure it is right and I have not got anybody to ask."

Nobody had anybody to ask.

Then a woman at the back said, "The roll has a mother and a daughter on it and the mother's name is on the line and the daughter does the days."

Sedge looked at his own sheet for a while.

"That is one person," he said. "That is one person and a ground and a house and a woman who signs where the mother signs, and I have got four lines and one of them wants a word in it and the word is *person*, and I cut it that way in about nine seconds this morning and I have been not-thinking about it for four hours, and somebody has now made me think about it and I would like to go back up the hill."

"You have not answered the question," said Neris Kade.

She had been at the back of the yard for the whole of it and had not been in it, and she said so before she said anything else, in the voice she used in rooms.

"I am Rainmarket's examiner for the season works. I am not a member of this yard, I am not a member of the Share, I am not a member of the Fund and I am not a member of anything, and I cannot vote on any of it and I have a sheet under my arm with nothing on it. I have had a sheet under my arm since the fourteenth of the seed month and its only function is to hold four figures in the order they are called out, and nothing has been called out, because that is a different account in a different ward and this is not it."

"And you will not hold one of ours," said Droll.

"No," said Neris Kade. "I will not, and I would like it said why in front of these hundred and forty people rather than at somebody's door in a week. If the examiner holds a sheet in this, then in a year there is a column in it with my name over it, and an office somewhere will say the examiner took it, and in ten years a person will say the examiner agreed. I have watched that happen to a village four terraces up. The two of you hold both of your sheets between you, and neither of you lets go of either, and I will do the thing I am for."

"Which is," said Kern.

"Standing where the asking is," said Neris Kade, "and not being able to help, which is the first time in four months and which I remain extremely good at."

Nobody thanked her, and she had not expected to be, and Marek Vale did not thank her either, because he had noticed that a man who thanked an examiner for not holding a sheet in a yard has understood nothing about what she has just done.

---

The second day was in the seed house at Rainmarket and it was about who may write in the thing, and it took most of it, and the answer was one line.

"Anybody may enter a line about their own paper," said Sedge, with the draft in his hand and Fisk behind the bench and about thirty people in the building and sixty-one trays of river sand under oiled paper along the wall, "and no person may enter a line about anybody else's paper. Not a factor, not a house, not an office, not a man with no office, and not me, and I have cut a form and I cannot enter a single line on it and I have wanted to since the fourth of the seed month.

"And an office may enter its own lines, and a house may enter its own lines, and a person may enter their own, and where the person who will answer to a line is not the person whose name is on it, the register enters the line and the answer side by side and does not join them, because joining them is the third box and I am not going to cut a third box and hand it to a man with no office to see what he does with it."

"Iren Orleb voted against the letters," said Bessok Tan, who was twenty-nine and had been the Share's secretary for four years and kept the roll on a board with a line of nails at the top. "So Orleb has a line in the thing and Orleb has answered one question about it and has not answered forty-nine, and I have carried a string and a slate for four years and I have said sixty-one out loud in this shed every month of my life, and sixty-one is households and it has been households the whole time, and I have never once said the word.

"So the second line of that form wants a word and the word I have been not saying is *household*, and if that is what goes in the box then a girl of sixteen who is on the roll because her mother is has an answer in that room that her mother gave, and nobody ever asked her, and there is no way in the world to know from the outside which of those two it is."

Fisk said, from behind the bench, without turning round: "Put *the person*. And leave it empty where it is empty, and let it be wrong in public, which is the only kind of wrong anybody in this basin can be trusted about."

"That is not a rule," said Sedge.

"It is the only thing I have got," said Mirren Fisk. "I have kept this house for eleven years and I have a bench book and a room and a yard, and I have not got a rule, and I have got four hundred and sixty-nine lines in that book and a rule would be a thing I could have used in the bad autumn and did not have."

Sedge wrote the four words in. Then he stopped, and took out a knife he had brought for the purpose, and cut the sheet off his own pad, and put it on the bench in front of the keeper of a seed house.

"Two more lines and then I am going," he said. "Hours, which are four a day and which somebody is going to have to pay for and I have not got a figure for it and neither has the man in the seed trade. And the cost, which is not money, which is that a person who comes stands in a room for about eleven minutes and loses whatever they were doing, and there is no box for the days, and I have written the word *days* on the sixth line and I have not got a word for the days and the days will not be entered and that is going to be the biggest hole in this thing and every person in it is going to find it in about a week."

He put the pencil down on the bench.

"And that is the form," he said. "Six lines, ruled, no boxes, a heading that is a tank and a date, and one word in the second line that I chose in nine seconds this morning and that I am not sure about, and I have to take it up a hill this afternoon and have it cut, and when it comes back it will be a form in the copy room with a number on it and it will be the fourth form I have cut and three of them are for things I do not agree with, and I would like the yard to understand that the man cutting it does not know whether it is right and is going to cut it anyway, because that is the whole of what a form is in this basin, and if a form has to be right before anybody will cut it then there has never been one."

---

Marek Vale walked up the causeway behind him for a piece of the way and then stopped and let him go, because a man with a form under his arm does not want company on a hill.

At the top of the causeway, at the second bell, the duty clerk on the posting desk had heard about the yard from a boy in about nine words, and she was sixty in the year and had twenty-two years in the copy room and was the posting desk's woman and had read out the figures on a hand-copy in the open in the seed month in front of two people, twice, the second time slower.

"Mr Vale," she said. "I am going to say the thing I said on the eleventh of the seed month, and I would like it noticed that I have said it once already and that nobody in this building has written it down anywhere either time.

"Reading is not entering. And the register's page is not the Trust's page, and a man with no office may enter nothing, and that has not changed because a yard printed four lines on a sheet, and it will not change in a month, and there is nothing anybody in the ninth month can do to it.

"So if the yard has given you a standing to read it out, that is the yard's business and it is theirs to give and nobody can take it and nobody can enter it either, and the register's page will say in its own hand on such a day *read out by a person with no office* or it will not say it, and either of those is correct and I could not tell you which is better before it happens.

"And the other thing, and then I have got a tray. If you read it out, then in about a year somebody in a room is going to ask you whether you checked the figures. And you will have to say no, and they will not like it, and they will be right not to like it, because reading is not checking.

"And there is nobody at that counter who checks. There is a yard's second person and she is not a reader and she is not a keeper and she is the only reason I have stopped worrying about it, and I have not met her, and I would like somebody to tell her that a woman at the top of this hill thought so."
