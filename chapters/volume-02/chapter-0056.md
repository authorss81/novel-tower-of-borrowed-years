# Chapter 56 — It Balances

The settlement desk's counting floor is one long table with a rule down the middle of it and a beam scale at the end, and eleven clerks sit along the north side on stools that have been worn into a shape, and the south side is bare except for a bench for the people who bring the work in.

Ondra Kelb had the seventeenth bundle of the day on the table in front of him and a pencil in his left hand, and the left hand was nine years old, and the hand-book was laced on the right and doing nothing at all.

He is twenty-one. He has been in the post eleven months. He came to the counter-desk in his fourth week and countersigned a total with a pencil in good faith because the figures in front of him balanced and nobody asked him who had produced them, and a clerk with no office held a settlement in place for six bells looking for a lawful alternative, and the chain walked to the counter-signature, and the hand on the north side of the desk is a hand that lifts a cup at sixty.

He looked up when Marek came onto the floor and did not stop, and he said:

"You are not to help."

"I know."

"You are to watch and not to help, because if you touch the sheet the sheet becomes yours and if the sheet is wrong it is your sheet, and I have spent eleven months finding out what happens when a young clerk is given something that is his." He moved a column two places along. "And you are the second eye on the fourth line of a list of twenty-two, and the fourth line is the only one in that list that is a bundle, and I have had it since the third bell yesterday, and I have been on it since the first."

"How many sheets."

"Nine, plus a schedule of fifty-two lines and a cover. Nine sheets and a cover is what a bundle of that size comes in as, and the schedule is not one of the nine, it is the back of the fifth, and the cover says what the thing is called and not what it is." He put the pencil down. "That is legal. The cover is allowed to say what the thing is called. There is no form in this city that makes a man say what a thing is."

Marek took the stool at the south end and put his hands flat on the table.

"Then what do you want me to do."

"I want you to watch me count and to not say one word for about four hours," said Kelb, "and then I want you to have an opinion about whether I did it correctly, and you are the only person in this building I would want that from, and it is a horrible thing to want from a man who did what happened to me, and you may put that down as well."

---

He counted until the third bell.

It is a particular kind of work and there is no way to do it quickly. Every line of the schedule against the fourth line of the instrument against the cover against the office's own received entry; then every rate on the schedule against the district roll for the month; then the aggregate against the aggregate in the instrument's recital; then the total against the total on the cover; and then, because the settlement desk's practice is that a number which has been agreed twice is a number which can be signed, the whole of it again, in a different order, from the back of the sheet forwards.

Marek watched. It was four hours. Kelb's left hand did the fine work without hesitating and his left hand is not his, and he did not say anything about it, and at some point in the second hour he began to whistle through his teeth and stopped again as though he had been caught.

At the third bell he put the pencil down and squared the bundle and said:

"It balances."

"Say that again."

"It balances. To the penny. Nine sheets and a schedule of fifty-two lines and a cover, every rate correct against the district roll, the aggregate correct against the recital, the total correct against the cover, and the fifty-two assignments each dated, signed, and witnessed, and each one of them a form with a number on it that says what it is." He pushed the bundle a foot towards the south side of the table. "There is nothing wrong with it."

"No false term?"

"No false term. There is no invented reserve. There is no page that was not served. There is no cause anywhere in nine sheets, because a cause is a thing that goes on the form and this thing is not on a form, and I have been looking for one for four hours because that is what I do now: I look for the sentence that is true and cannot be acted on." He was not looking at Marek. "And there is no chain. There is nothing to aim. You could put this bundle in front of the whole of Bell Stair and every person in it would say that it is a bundle of proper claims, properly assigned, properly aggregated, and they would be right."

"And the fourth clause."

"The fourth clause is correct," said Ondra Kelb. "A named counterweight. If it were not named it would not be accepted at all, and if it were named wrongly it would be a gap, and a gap is a shortfall, and a shortfall lands on a district anyway, so the man who drafted that clause did the city a service and wrote it in six lines." He looked up then. "You have been in Rainmarket for three days looking for a lie. There isn't one. Go home."

---

"I found one," said Marek.

"I know you did. I have read the finding of the seventeenth. I was the boy in it." He drew the bundle back to the north side of the table because it was his again. "I have had five weeks of learning what it is to be the reason a file is wrong, and I want to say the next part in the first person, because you are the only person in this building who would understand why I am going to say it and you are also the man who did it.

"Eleven months ago I could have read this bundle and found nothing wrong with it in twenty minutes, and I would have countersigned it, and I would have been right to, and that is the horror. I am not a cleverer clerk than I was. I am a damaged one. Somebody held a thing in place for six bells and the cost came down the instrument to the counter-signature and it took my hands, and the only thing it bought me is that I now cannot stop looking for a false term when there is no false term, and so I have spent four hours proving that the worst thing that has ever happened to me has nothing to do with me."

"That is not the same as it being somebody else's fault."

"I know. And I have been relieved." He put both hands flat on the bundle, the wrong one and the right one. "I have been relieved all afternoon and I could not tell you why until now, and the reason is that the last thing that happened to me was a lie, and if this is not a lie then I was hurt by a man doing the correct work of a bad rule, and that is a better thing to have been hurt by. Do you understand what I have just said to you? I have been comforted by the discovery that a thing which costs people their hands and their futures is *proper*."

Marek sat on the stool at the south end of a long table and had nothing to say that would be worth saying, and he was aware, in a way he had been aware of once before in a room with a man in custody, that the correct thing to do and the useful thing to do had come apart from each other and that he was only going to be able to reach one of them.

"The number," he said. "Say it out loud twice more."

"With a witness?"

"With a witness. Not because you are wrong. Because in nine months somebody with my face and a better coat is going to stand in a room and say that you found something, and there will be no way in the city to check him, and the only thing that will be in the record is a number said by one clerk in a hall with a door on it." He stood up. "I am not able to enter anything in that record myself. I have no office. So the number has to be in a room, said by a person, with a second person in it, and I am the second person, and that is the whole of what I am good for."

---

The woman from the posting desk came up at the fourth bell because somebody had told her there was a thing to be read out, and she is fifty-one and she has read a notice out twice for twenty-two years and she has never once written down that she does it.

"Twice," said Kelb. "You read it twice, and the second time slower, and I will write both of them down and neither of them is a copy of the other, they are two readings of one number."

The schedule's aggregate is one thousand nine hundred and sixteen, in fifty-two assigned claims, out of sixty-one members of the Elmsworth Seed Share, and the nine who are not in it are the nine with nothing to assign.

He read it twice. The second time she was slower. The floor listened, all eleven of them, without looking up from their own columns, because this is what the floor is.

"Thank you," said Kelb, and the woman said, "It is the same number twice. That is what twice is for."

"Who else has read it today?" Marek said.

"Eleven of us off this floor, and every one of the other ten would have told you the same and not one of them would have told you about the box, because the box is not a figure. The figure is right everywhere. The figures are not merely right, they are right in the way a sum is right when it has been made by somebody who knew the answer and went backwards." Kelb wiped the pencil on the cloth. "I have known for eleven months that a clerk who finds something in this building has nowhere to take it. That is the whole of what a hold taught me and I have hated it every day, and now I have said a number out loud in a room with a second person in it and I still cannot do anything with it, and I would like that noted, because I have been putting off believing it on the grounds that this once would be different."

"It is not different," said Marek. "It is the same size as everything else, and I have spent a month finding out what size that is."

Then, while she was still on the floor, because she had asked what else he had found and had expected to be told about a false term and had got a number instead, Ondra Kelb said:

"There is a fifth box on the settlement desk's forms."

Marek looked up.

"There are four boxes," said Kelb. "One for a levy, one for a schedule, one for a posting, one for a return. And there is a fifth at the foot, under a rule that says a district may hand over a returnable sum to another party at a discount, and it is the oldest box on the form, and it is the only one of the five that is not dated in this century." He put his thumb on it. "There is no box for an assignment, because the form was cut before anybody was doing it. And so for eight years, everything that is an assignment in this city has been entered in the box for a return, and it is not an illegal entry, because a district may lawfully hand over a returnable sum, and a rate arrears is a returnable sum, and so it is not merely legal. It is the correct box."

He stopped, and then he said the part that Marek went out into the rain that night and could not put down.

"And the fifth box goes back a long way further than eight years. There is a hand in it I cannot place and it is in the same ink as a district's ordinary return, and I have not told anybody that I have noticed it, and I am not going to say any more about it in this hall, and if it is in your book, Mr Vale, it is in your book and not in mine."
