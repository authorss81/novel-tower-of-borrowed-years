# Chapter 60 — A Copy in the Open

The copy room on the night of the eleventh had the lamps going along the north wall and a wet wind on the sills, and Marek Vale had the Share's instrument open on the long table with his own copy paper, four ruled sheets, a pen, a blotter, and about four hours of lamp in it, and he was not the clerk of the day for anything, and there was no form anywhere in the city on which a man with no office did what he was about to do.

Ondra Kelb came up at the sixth bell, when the copy room shut and the lamps were lit along the north wall, with his coat on, and said, "Which of the four?"

"The fourth," said Marek. "I want you to count with me."

"You want me to count with you and I am the second eye on it, so you are not asking a favour. Say the rest of it."

"I am not going to enter this anywhere," said Marek. "I am going to make it by hand, in this room, with the door open, and you are going to say the figures out loud as we go, and at the end I am going to write my name and the fact that I have no office under it, and it is going to sit in a drawer in a house on Sill Row where two people live and neither of them is a clerk."

"All right," said Ondra Kelb, and took off his coat.

---

It took four hours and most of a pot of ink.

The instrument is one page folded twice, and the fold is where the first hour went, because the crease goes through the recital and the fourth clause is on the back of the second leaf, and a hand-copy has to say that a crease exists or somebody in six months will read a complete document that was never complete.

He copied the head, the need, the pledge, the scope, the two witnesses by name and by trade, and the settlement, and he copied the fourth clause word for word because word for word was the only way it was any use at all:

*Fourth. The season favoured is the season of the sowing. The district named in the schedule bears the failure of the season favoured. Named: the Rainmarket low ground, as per the schedule at the back of the fifth leaf.*

Then the schedule. Fifty-two lines, each one a household, a rate period, a sum, a discount, a date, and a signature — and no rate box, and no name box for anybody except the assignor, and the assignor's box was headed *assignor* and the heading is not old and everything else on the form is.

He copied it in columns and got the columns wrong the first time and did it again, and the wrong first attempt is on the sheet underneath the right one because he could not lift the grain off a page he had already got wrong, and Kelb read the figures out loud as they went and stopped him twice, and both times he had been right.

About two hours in his right elbow began, the same elbow as the longhand in the rains, and by the third hour the hand had gone and the figures on the copy came out in a way he did not like and he did not correct them, because correcting them would have meant a second night and there is no second night.

At the last of the light he wrote at the foot of the last sheet, in the standing that a clerk uses and that a copyist is taught:

*Copied longhand from the instrument of the Elmsworth Seed Share sealed the ninth day of the bad autumn, MR 411, and from the schedule at the back of the fifth leaf. Copy made the eleventh of the seed month, MR 413, in the copy room at Bell Stair, in an open door, with a second person present who read the figures aloud as they were written. The figures agree.*

*Written in full. M. Vale, clerk, no office.*

And then he put the pen down, and did not eat anything although there was bread in the room because he had a page unfinished, which is a habit and has never once been a virtue, and the lamps along the north wall went on doing their business.

---

The woman from the posting desk came up at the evening bell because somebody in the hall had heard a lamp going and the lamps are not supposed to be going.

She read it out. Twice, because her practice is to read a thing out twice, and the second time slower, and both readings were of one set of figures and neither was a copy of the other.

"Will it go in the public register?" she asked.

"No."

"You have not asked anybody."

"I am asking you," said Marek. "You are the only person in this building who can tell me. May a man with no office enter a copy in the public register?"

"No," said the woman on the posting desk. "You may not. The register's page is the Trust's page, and an entry is made by a clerk of the Chamber against a letter beside a name, and there is no letter beside your name, and if I entered it I should be entering a thing in the register of an office that has not been asked to carry a page for an unassigned clerk, and I have done this for twenty-two years and I have never done that, and I would like to keep the not doing it."

"Then you have read it out."

"I have read it out," she said, "and reading is not entering, and I want it written down that I said so, because somebody is going to come into this room one day and ask whether the finding of the seventeenth is in the public register, and it is, and it is there because a clerk signed it and gave it up in a room. It is not there because two people read it out at the end of a night."

---

Kelb did not go home at the sixth bell. He sat with his hands in the sleeves of his coat at the end of the long table and looked at the four sheets.

"You are not going to be able to use that," he said.

"I know."

"It is not the only copy. There is the original and the office's received entry and a bound duplicate at the house and the cover in the house, and a bundle of that size is in four places and probably five. You have made a fifth and it is the only one with your name on it." He said it without any unkindness at all, which was worse. "What are you going to do with it?"

"Read it out to people who cannot get to a register," said Marek. "That is all. There is no form for that either."

Kelb nodded, and got up, and went to the door, and stopped with his hand on it and said the thing that had been sitting in him for two days.

"There are nine links."

Marek did not move.

"The assignments," said Kelb. "I have been through the schedule at the back of the fifth leaf the way you copy a thing, and not the way you certify one, and there is a second date on about a third of those lines. There is a box on the form above my line and it is not for the assignor, and nine of the fifty-two carry a figure in it, and a figure in that box is not a household and it is not a person." He was still holding the door. "Then the low ground's uncollectable rate for the flood quarters went back into a district return in the fifth box on two dates, and a district return is a sum and a sum can be bought, and the house has the sum. And then the house set it against a rate arrears of a carrier at the Tallow Gate, and I do not know the man's name and there is no box for the carrier's name, only for the house's, and it is a sum of under fifty. And then it is in a district, because an uncollectable private sum of under fifty has nowhere else in this city to be put, and you know that, because you sat at a table with a board on it for a month."

"How many links."

"Nine," said Ondra Kelb. "Five households, the low ground, the house, the carrier, and a district. The carrier's is the second from the end and it is forty-three and some months, and the ninth link is a district account, and the ninth link is Tallow, because Tallow is the only account in the basin that can take a stranger's uncollectable money without anybody's name going anywhere."

He went out. The door stayed open for a while, the way it does in a room where somebody has just left.

---

Marek wrote the date on the sheet at the top, in the right-hand corner, because a sheet without a date is a sheet that can be dated by whoever finds it, and then he sat for a quarter of an hour and worked out what he was going to do about the ninth link and decided he was not going to do anything about it that night.

The reason he wrote down for himself, in the words he actually used, was this: that he had no office, and that a man with no office who turns up at a tank at midnight is a rumour, and that in the ninth month this office would come to that tank with a form and a district's name already written at the top of it and ask the ward to show it, and the ward would show it, and the ward would have four hundred and six people in it and three hundred and seventy-seven of them had never been asked a question by anybody in their lives, and he was not going to walk in there tonight and start something he could not finish and could not record.

Then he put the four sheets in a flat board with a piece of oiled paper over them, and put the board in his coat against his chest, and went down the hill in the rain at the last of the light.

---

He told Sedge at the eighth bell on the twelfth, standing up, in the corridor outside the settlement desk, and he had rehearsed it four times on the stair and it came out in about nine seconds and none of the four rehearsals was in it.

The card was in his coat. He did not take it out, and Sedge watched him not take it out, and neither of them said one word about it, and that was most of the length of the silence that followed.

"I am not recertifying the fourth file."

Sedge did not say anything, and Marek found that he had been bracing for a question and not for a silence.

"Why?"

"Because I cannot be the second clerk's eye on a paper that does not say where its cost lands, and because if my eye goes on it then for eleven days there is a document in this city with two sets of eyes on it and both of them official saying that the cost is a district." He heard how that sounded and went on anyway, because he had come to tell it and not to be told he had done well. "And because the second eye is the only thing that kind of paper has ever had. It does not need a person who is angry. It needs a second pair of eyes that is in the building."

"That is a good reason," said Sedge, "and it is not a reason that stops the work."

"I know."

"It has to be done by somebody, and it will be done by me, and I will be signing it, and I have read the fourth clause this morning, which I had not read in nineteen years." He looked at the desk. "I want you to hear the shape of what I am about to say. I am doing it because you did not want to, and not because you were right, and if this is ever looked at, the two of those are going to be in it together and I am not going to be able to separate them and neither will you."

"It should have been you that read it," Marek said. "You have read it and I have not, and that is the whole of the difference between the two of us and it is not a matter of reading."

"It is a matter of reading," said Sedge. "And I have just read it, and I am going to sign it badly on purpose, because I have not been sure of anything on this hill since the sixth bell this morning and I was sure of things this morning." He picked up the file. "I have been on this hill for nineteen years and twice in that time somebody has not wanted to sign something and the thing was signed by the supervisor, and one of those was a roof in the Rill yard and the family is still in it, and the other one is not a story. You have moved it off a man with no office and put it on a man whose word will be believed. That is the whole of what you have done. Go and count your other eighteen."

"I know," said Marek Vale. "That is the part I am going to have to carry."

"Nobody in this building will help you carry it, because there is no box for it," said Sedge, "and you have spent a month learning that, and I am not going to pretend the lesson came cheap for either of us. Go and count your other eighteen."

---

Nim Rook came up at the sixth bell that evening and stood in the copy room with her hood down and her hair wet, because she is a night courier and Rainmarket is a long walk from anywhere and she had come up the hill for a different reason entirely and had decided, on the stair, that this would do instead.

"You have had nothing for a month," she said.

"No."

"You have had nothing to carry since the twenty-second, and I have come anyway, twice, and the second time I stood on this landing for a quarter of an hour and you were not here and I did not leave anything and I did not take anything, and I have been thinking about the price the whole time." She looked at the board on the table, which was flat and had nothing on it. "I never said what the price was. Everybody assumes it is the information. It is not the information."

"What is it?"

"It is that you would say so," said Nim Rook. "That is the whole of it. You would have said *I have nothing* and I could have gone down the hill. Four people this month had better than a clerk's word, Mr Vale, and I would like it on the record — and there is no form, and I know there is no form, and that is what I have been thinking about on the stair."

Below them, on the Tally Hall door, the Trust's notice of the fourth was still nailed up with its seal, and the duty clerk had read it out twice that day, and about nine people had stopped on the steps to hear her, and not one of them said afterwards that they had read it.

The public register was on the table by the window with a new page in it, and the page said what the oldest page says, and beneath the empty line it said a signature written out in full, letter by letter, and the words *written in full*, and a date, and a man's name with no office after it. Anybody who came up the hill that week could stand at that table and read it, and something over forty people had stood at that table in a month, and there is no register of who they were.

And on the low ground, in the seed house, a woman of thirty-four turned forty-one trays twice in the day with a piece of chalk on the end of nine of them, and at the Tallow Gate the lamps were lit along the wall and the four yards were drawing off the tank on a board with four columns and about two hundred names on it, and the wage on that board was coming out of a line of forty thousand nine hundred that the water office had asked one question about ten days ago, and the answer was due in the ninth month.

The seed went into the ground on the twelfth.
