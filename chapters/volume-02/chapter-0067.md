# Chapter 67 — The Last Line

Rustam Vey reopened the Rainmarket credit window at the first bell on the twentieth with a board, a brush and a pot of white, and he did it in the ordinary way, in the lane, in front of whoever was there, because he is a man of fifty with nineteen years in the post who has learned that a shutter with a queue behind it is his to stand next to.

*Seed advances resumed, 20th. District assessments of the flood quarters restated, 18th. No advance may be made against a district whose rate is uncollected.*

The restatement came out on the eighteenth, which is four days earlier than his clerk thought it would, and it is the whole of the reason he had gone back inside on the tenth of the month instead of doing the thing he said he would not do, and the lane emptied by about the second bell, and by the fourth there were four people at the trestle opposite and not forty-one, and a boy of fourteen was still holding a list for a queue that had stopped existing, and he stood there holding it for another hour because nobody had told him he could stop.

"That is your rule," said Marek, standing at the edge of it.

"That is my rule and my hand and my ink," said Vey. "You may say it and you may not say the rest of it, and I am going to say the rest of it once, so that you cannot go away and tell anybody nobody ever told you. Four households lost a fortnight on the sixth, and there is no office in this city that can give them the fortnight back, and I have been right, and I would like it noticed that being right and the lane being empty are the same sentence and not two. There is no version of this week in which anybody is a hero and I have stopped pretending there is one about me."

He put the brush down in the pail.

"And I know about the ninth month. It comes from my own office, it uses the word *advise*, and it names a district I have never been to, and when it lands my own rate will be standing on that account with the rest of it, and there will be four hundred and six people on it."

---

The restatement of a district's flood-years rate means the account can be got into a column small enough to be worked on, and an account that can be worked on is an account that gets cut, and the cutting is done by a written order in a frame nailed to the wall of the levy office, and there is no copy of it, and it is the only instruction in this city that a person cannot be given a copy of.

Grale turned the frame back on the wall on the morning of the twentieth at about the second bell. He had turned it to the wall in the rains and it had stayed there, and there was a pale rectangle on the plaster where it had been, and the frame went back over the rectangle and nobody mentioned the rectangle.

He is fifty-eight. He has been in the levy office nineteen years and he has cut four orders in that time, and the intervals between them are the part of it he does not say out loud, and every one of them has got cheaper as it goes down, and he worked that out when he was thirty-five and has said it out loud exactly twice, both times in a yard, both times to a person who did not repeat it.

He is also the officer who enters it, and the procedure is his own office's and has been since before him, and it is short. Any cut order is read, in the words of the office, to every party it names, before it is entered. It is not entered at the close of a month. It carries the day and not the month. And it carries the name of the officer who enters it.

Grale wrote the day on it and not the month, and then he read it, to himself, standing, which is the first of the four things and is not optional.

---

Six parties. The low ground's night lamp. The low ground clinic's coal. The river gauge at the second junction, which is one man and a candle and a float. The market's weighbridge lamps, which are what the second bell is called on and there are four of them. The pumps at the sluice. And, at the foot, the Elmsworth seed house's lamp.

He read the first four to the water office, the clinic, the gauge house and the market, and the fifth to the sluice house, and in each of the five a person wrote his own hand beside the line saying that it had been read to them, and in each of the five the person asked him the same question, which was what the order did, and in each of the five he gave them the same answer in the words of the office, which was that it held the district account to its presented figure, that the order would be entered on the day, and that the lines would be cut in the order they stood.

The pumps man said, "Then the sluice is above the gauge."

"The sluice is above the gauge."

"That is my whole life," said the pumps man, and put his hand beside the line, and went back to his pumps.

---

The sixth was the Elmsworth seed house, and Grale walked it on purpose, and he went to it last because the other five are offices and each of them has a person who can write beside its own line, and the seed house is a business and has nobody, and the rule for a business is that the officer goes.

It was a little after the fourth bell. The lamp was lit. Fisk was at the far end of the bench with her hands in the sand of a tray and the mark on the end of it a cut of a member's own tin pressed in wet eight years ago.

"I am required to read you the last line of a cut order before it is entered," said Grale, "and the last line is this office, and the office has no one to write beside it, so I have come, and I am going to read it to you and I am not going to ask you anything and you are not to feel that you have to answer me."

He read it. He read the whole of the order, because the rule is that it is read in the words of the office to every party it names, and the order is the office's words, and reading the last line alone would be a reading of a sentence without the thing that makes it a sentence.

Fisk listened with her hands in the sand. At the end of it she said, "Say the last line again."

"The Elmsworth seed house's lamp," said Grale, "which is the lamp the seed house works its trays under, at the bottom of the order, and which is cut after every line above it, and which the office supplies in the third month and not before."

"That is the lamp I turn sixty-one trays under twice a day."

"Yes."

"And if the account is short, the lamp goes out."

"Eventually, and after every line above it, and not on a day I am able to tell you."

Fisk took her hands out of the sand and wiped them on her apron, and stood for a moment looking at the lamp, which was lit, and which had been lit every day since the sixth of the month before.

"I have no answer to that," she said, "and I want it written down that I have no answer to it and that I was not asked one. And I want to say one thing about it that is not an answer either, and you may put it in your book or not."

"Go on."

"You have signed two of these in your life and this is the second one, and it is the first one that has been read to a party before it was entered, and that happened because of a rule and not because of you." Fisk did not raise her voice and the shed was very quiet. "That is not a thing about my lamp. That is a thing about your hand, and I have worked it out in about nine seconds standing here, and I have decided I am not going to say the rest of it out loud, and I would like you to notice that I have decided that, and not that I have not noticed it."

Grale wrote his own hand beside the sixth line, and then he went back up the hill and entered the order, and the order went on the wall in the frame, and it went up at the sixth bell on the twentieth day of the seed month, and it carried the day and not the month, and at the foot of it, under the last line, in the office's standing hand, it carried the name of the officer who entered it.

His hand went bad on the last letter.

It was a small thing and it took about two seconds and it happened on the last stroke of a name he has written at the foot of one other of these in nineteen years, and the last letter came out at a different angle from the other five and about a third heavier, and he noticed it, and nobody else was in the room.

He did not say anything about it. He went on down the column and signed the two other papers that wanted him that evening, and both of those were fine, and on the way out he stopped at the frame for perhaps four seconds and looked at the name under the last line, and then went home up the hill at the seventh bell.

And at the second bell that morning, before any of it, an envelope had come up the hill from Crown March in a Crown March hand with a Crown March seal on it, about the size of a form, containing a form, with a question on it.

*The fifth box is referred to this office for identification of the hands in the entries earlier than the eighth year. Answer is requested.*

Grale had read it standing up at his own desk at the second bell, and it had taken him a page and a quarter, and it had gone into the tray under the district restatement and the restatement had gone up, and it was still in the tray at the sixth bell, and it would be in the tray tomorrow, and on the twenty-second, and on the twenty-ninth, and the eleventh day after it came was the eleventh day after it came.

He did not open the form. He did not open the book. There was a line in that account standing since before the flood years and he had known about it for longer than the practice of putting anything in the fifth box has existed, and the answer to that referral would take a man about four hours of work at that desk.

He had wanted to stop since he was thirty-five. He had a condition, and he gave it to a yard in the rains and nobody has written it down anywhere except a charcoal line on a board in a wet yard and about forty people's memory. The day he proves the line in the account desk he has to stop.

He looked at the tray for a while.

A referral asks a question. A man who goes looking for his own condition is a man who has already decided the answer, and there is a difference between an officer answering a question and an officer arranging to be asked one, and the difference took him nineteen years to learn and it is the only thing in the building he would teach anybody.

He left the referral in the tray, and he entered the cut order, and his hand went bad on the last letter of his own name, and nobody saw it.

---

Fisk came up the causeway at the seventh bell that evening, which is the bell after the one the copy room shuts at, and she came on foot, and she was the fourth person to arrive at that desk in nine days and the first one who had walked.

"I am told the lamp is the last line," she said.

"It is."

"Then I am going to tell you the thing I came to tell you, and then I am going to go home, and I would rather it was said tonight while you have not had a drink and while the frame is still up with your name on it." She put her hands in her coat. "The room is not yours. Not now, not from the first, and not later. I told you the eighteenth that there were four days of it left and I told you I had decided and I had."

"May I ask why."

"You may ask it and I will answer it now, because I told you at the bench that I would and I do not intend to be a man who says things at a bench. I worked out which tray it was and I worked it out in about nine minutes and I did not say it, and I have been turning it over on the causeway for two days. I did not say it because you had not told her and I was not going to be the man who told a girl of sixteen that her lot was sand on an afternoon when she was filling cones, and because you had already decided to tell her in three days in a yard, and because a man with no office who tells a woman of thirty-four what is in her own shed is a man who has decided that being useful is a thing he is allowed to do."

"That is the right answer," said Mirren Fisk, "and I would like you to notice that I would have said the same one, and that neither of us saying it does not make it the right one, and that in a year somebody is going to write down that the keeper and the clerk held the same opinion and be quite right."

"You may ask and I am going to answer in about four sentences and I am not going to soften them." Fisk did not sit down. "The last line of the first cut order of this year is my lamp, and the second is the night lamp, and the third is my coal, and I have had three bad seasons and I am a woman of thirty-four who turns sixty-one trays twice a day and I have just had eleven of them go to sand, and I have a room going free over a shop and four a month is the difference between paying the woman who let it and not paying her, and I offered it to you because you stood by my door on the third of the month for four hours and asked me whether anybody here had ever read the whole of our instrument, and I said no, and you said I was the first this year, and you did not tell me what was in it, and I have kept this house eleven years and nobody has asked me that in eleven years."

"That is still true."

"It is still true and it was not a good enough reason for a room, and I thought about it for nine days and I could not find a better one, and it is not the reason any more," said Fisk. "In the ninth month a letter is going to come up this hill that uses the word *advise*, and every keeper in this basin has known that since the tenth, because a Trust officer said it out loud in a lane outside a shuttered window with forty people standing in it, and a person with no office who lives over a seed shop on the low-ground road is a person the office will send for. Not because you did anything. Because of where your bed is. You would not be a witness, Mr Vale. You would be a man in a room with a door who slept over the thing, and there are four hundred and six people in this ward who would be told to be glad of that, and in the ninth month somebody will ask me whether the second clerk's eye on our instrument had access to our premises, and I would have to say yes, and I would be saying it about a room I offered him in a good week."

"Give it to somebody else."

"There is nobody else," said Mirren Fisk. "There is a boy of twenty-one who counts twice and a woman of thirty who has a seal and a girl of sixteen who fills cones, and none of them can read a counterweight out of a paper, and I have watched all three of them this month and I have chosen wrong twice and I am not wrong a third time." She stopped at the door. "I have not decided that you are a bad man. I have decided that in the ninth month I need the roof over my seed house to belong to somebody I cannot be asked about, and that is a colder thing and I would rather tell you it than let you find it out in a corridor from Ivo Sarn."

Marek sat at a desk in a hall that was closed and did not say one word for about half a minute.

"Say it to me anyway," he said. "Because in the ninth month somebody is going to ask you who had access to the premises, and I am not going to be able to help you, and you have just told me the whole of it in a flat voice in an hour when I could not have asked it in a day."

"It is on a bench book," said Mirren Fisk. "Eleven marks, two sheds, and a year. I will put the room on it too, in the back, with the date it changed, because a thing that changed and does not say when it changed is a thing somebody can use two ways." She went out into the wet. "You will move the board back to Sill Row tonight. And I am sorry about the room, and I am not going to say so twice, and I would rather you did not thank me for the months between now and then either, because the months are yours and they were always going to be yours and I am only the one who is saying so out loud while there is still a bench to say it at."
