# Chapter 73 — A Body's Calendar

The Share worked its eleven days on the sixth of the growth month, in one field, in a line, with the frost still in the shadow of the ditch, and it was not a thing anybody had voted for and it was not a thing anybody had to be asked about, because eleven days of labour is what the instrument says and the instrument was signed in the bad autumn by sixty-one people on one seal.

Nineteen people came. Eleven of them were the households whose trays had gone to sand and the other eight were members who had a share of the loss because the return in kind divides it across the members, which is what a cooperative's own rule does when a cooperative's own lot fails: it takes it out of the people it happened to and calls that fairness.

Sera Holt came out at the seventh bell because Mirren Fisk had sent for her at the fifth and has never in her life not come when she is sent for, and she came with her own bag and did not have to look at anybody to know which of the nineteen she was there for, because it is a physician's whole trade to know that from a field, and she had known it from the ditch end.

The woman was forty-one, a second-generation member of a household at the far end of the low-ground road, and her name was Ilma Tresch, and she had drawn one of the eleven trays in the fourth week of the seed month and had filled cones and had carried two of the sacks, and she had done all eleven of the days.

She was taken to the low ground clinic at the second bell of the seventh and put in the back room, which is a room with a table in it and a curtain on a wire and no window that opens, and it is where the clinic sees people it is not going to admit.

---

"You will want to know what she has paid," said Sera Holt, standing at the end of the table with her hands behind her back, "and so will she, and she can add it up better than I can, so let her."

Ilma Tresch added it up.

"Eleven days," she said. "Not eleven days like the other eight, who had eight. Eleven days, because the rule divides it by the number of trays and there were eleven trays and eleven households and my own tray is one of them, so it is my own tray, so it is me, and that is the rule and I knew the rule when I drew the seed and I would draw it again because without the seed there is no lot at all.

"And a third of a sack out of a member's kept-back. Ansgar Drel's, and he brought it himself and he is seventy-eight and he carried it down from the top on his own back and nobody asked him to and I would have carried it if he had not. And the rest of it — the balance, the part of it that is eleven days of labour and does not come back in kind — the rule says the balance goes on my ground as a rate. And it is a rate, and I know what a rate is, my father's line was on the low-ground roll, and I have read my own line at the counter like everybody else has, and I am not a fool and I am not going to sit here and be told that a rate is a surprise."

"How long have you had the cough," said Sera Holt.

"Since the floods, and this winter it has not let go of me, and this week it has got into the small of my back, and I have had it since the ninth and I did not say so because the first round of doors closed on the twentieth and the second is finished and I am one of the three hundred and seventy-seven that nobody has ever asked anything, and I have been lifting sacks for eleven days with it."

She said it without any self-pity at all, which is the way people say things they have rehearsed on a walk from a field to a house.

Sera Holt took her hand and turned it over and put two fingers on the inside of the wrist and then on the side of the neck, and asked her to breathe, and did not ask her anything else for about four minutes.

"Who has seen this," she said at last.

"I have. He has." She did not look at Marek, and the *he* was the man in the room with no coat on. "And there is a woman at the top of the road whose mother has it and who has had it two winters and who cannot get anybody to write it down anywhere, and I know that because she told me on the bench, and I have not put her name in a book, because I am a member of this Share and so is her mother and it is not my book."

---

"You have paid for a season that did not come," said Sera Holt.

"Yes."

"Eleven days of lifting and screening and carrying, on ground that had already failed, for a lot that was sand at the bottom, and the sand was sand before you touched it, and the ground was frost in the shadow of the ditch when you were in it, because the frost came on the twenty-fourth and the eleven days began on the sixth of the growth month and the days are in the growth month because the days are always in the growth month, and in the growth month the frost is finished and there is nothing to lift."

"There was plenty to lift," said Ilma Tresch. "There was the eleven households' ground to clear and the Share's to re-sift and there was the new lot from the uplands to bring down and I did all of that, and I did it in eleven days, and I am not saying the days were not work."

"I know they were work. I am telling you what the work was for."

Sera Holt put the bag on the table and opened it and did not take anything out of it.

"Now I am going to say the thing that nobody in this room wants said, and I am going to say it slowly, and Mr Tresch, I am going to say it to you and not about you, and I would like the man in the room to write nothing down while I say it because there is no form for it and a piece of paper with a sentence on it is a thing that gets put in a column in four months.

"Your field's calendar and your body's calendar are two different calendars and they do not line up, and they have never lined up, and the distance between them is a season on one side and a month on the other, and that is the whole of the trouble and there is no office in this city that has both of them in it.

"A field's calendar is: the ground opens, you sow, it comes up, it is cut, and the frost decides the end of it. It is a year. It is the same year for everybody in the district, and the district can be named in it, and when the district is named in it, the year is a fine thing to charge against. A body's calendar is: what a person can do in a week, and when, and how many weeks there are in the year that are any use to a body. That is not a year. It is a month and it is a different month every season, and it is not a district and it cannot be one, because it is not the same for anybody, and you cannot assess a person at the top rate because they are the top rate at everything.

"A rate is a field instrument. It is written for ground. It is written for a parcel, and a parcel is a thing that can be described once and stayed described, and a woman who cannot lift in the growth month is not a parcel, and there is no box on a rate on which a person is asked whether she is well. Not one. I have been a physician for twenty-six years and I have been inside this building longer than most of the women you can see from the door have been alive, and there is no form in it, and there is not going to be one, and I would like it minuted somewhere that the absence is not an oversight. The absence is the form working exactly as it was cut."

"And if she cannot do the days," said Marek, "the rule says the balance goes on her ground."

"Yes."

"And if she does the days, the balance is a rate, and if she cannot do the days the balance is also a rate, and either way it is a rate, and she has one of them whether she is well or not, and nobody ever has to find out which."

"Yes," said Sera Holt, "and you have just described the whole of the instrument in a sentence and I would like you to notice that you said it in about nine seconds standing in a clinic, and that nobody has said it in this basin before today, and that I have been trying to say it since the ninth of the seed month in a shed with a stick in a tray, and that I could not get it into that shape, and that the shape you have got it in does not have a box in it either."

---

"Nobody is going to be charged for a cough," said Sera Holt then, "and I am going to say what I am going to do, and I would like Mr Tresch to hold me to it and I would like it written in whatever Mr Vale has not written down. I am going to certify her out of the remaining days. Not out of the rule. Out of the days, for this lot, once. And in nine days' time a member will come in here with the same cough and I will certify her out of the days, and there will be about eleven of them, because there are eleven trays and the women who drew them are the women who carry them, and I have done the arithmetic since the field.

"And here is the part I do not like, and I am a physician and I am going to say it anyway. A certificate goes to the board of the clinic and the board of the clinic writes it into the roll as a cost, and the clinic's costs go on the same account as everything else, and the district's answer to a cost is a rate. So I have found a way of making the cost smaller and the cost is still there, and I have moved it one desk to the left, and there is a form on the desk I moved it to, and I would like somebody in this room to ask me the obvious question and I would like to hear what the obvious question sounds like in an outsider's mouth, because I have not been able to say it myself in eleven days."

Nobody asked her anything. The curtain on the wire moved in the draught.

"That is what I wanted," said Sera Holt.

---

The woman from the top of the road came in at the fourth bell and had her sleeve mended first, because the seam had gone at the shoulder and there is a seamstress who comes on the first and the fourth and is paid out of the alms box and is not on the clinic's books at all, and the two women talked about the sleeve for about a minute and a half and it was the only ordinary thing that happened in that room all day.

Marek sat on the bench by the wall with his hands on his knees and did not ask Tamsin Vale anything, and he noticed himself not asking her anything, and did not write that down either, and understood with some clarity that there were three things in his life he was carrying that he had decided not to put in a room, and that the reasons were two different reasons, and that one of the reasons was a rule about forms and the other one was about her and he was not going to look at which was which.

At the sixth bell he went out into the low-ground road and the light was going and the field where the nineteen had been was four hundred yards off with the ditch down the side of it, and the water in the ditch had a skin of ice on it in the shadow and none at all in the sun, and he stood and looked at that for a while, because a field's calendar and a woman's back are the same length of year and one of them has a rate at the end of it and the other one has a certificate, and he had understood since the seventh of the month that a certificate moves a cost one desk to the left.

Fisk came out of the shed with her sleeves rolled and did not come over.

"Eleven households," she said, from thirty feet. "Those are the eleven. Every one of them drew seed from us, which is the point of a Share, and every one of them is one of the eleven, and that is not a conspiracy and it is not the clause and it is not anybody's doing, it is what happens when a cooperative takes the cost of its own failure out of the people it happens to. And on the twenty-first of last month I told one of those eleven, in a yard, in the words of a bench book, that her tray was sand. And in the ninth month every one of those eleven is going to be handed a number on a piece of paper, and not one of them will be able to tell you which clause the number came from, because the number will have been made out of a rule that is correct, by people who are not dishonest, out of a tray that failed for a reason nobody has proved."

"And the boy of sixteen," said Marek.

"The boy of sixteen is on that list and her mother's ground is on that list and there is no box on it for her either," said Mirren Fisk, "and when the number comes I am going to have to stand there and be the keeper. That is the job. Somebody has to be."
