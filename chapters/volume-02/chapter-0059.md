# Chapter 59 — Fewer of Them Than in the Rains

The Trust's Rainmarket credit window is a counter with a brass plate over it on the ground floor of a building with three windows and a ledger on a shelf inside, and on the morning of the tenth there was a bar across it and a painted board hung on the bar, and the board was dated the sixth.

*Seed advances suspended, 6th. District in arrears of two quarters. No advance may be made against a district whose rate is uncollected. — R. Vey, Settlement.*

The painting was good. Somebody had taken the trouble, which meant somebody had been told in advance what the board would say, which meant the board was not a surprise to the office and had not been made on the sixth by a man in a hurry. It had probably been made on the third.

---

Vey came out at the first bell because there were forty people in the lane and he is a man of fifty with nineteen years in the post who has learned that a shutter with a queue behind it is his to stand next to.

He was not a hero and he was not a villain and he had, in the third year of this post, painted four words on the wall of his own counting room because he was twenty-nine and believed that a rule that was going to hurt somebody should be visible to the person doing it.

*AN ARREARS CANNOT BE SECURITY.*

"There is no argument available to you and I am not going to listen to one," he said, before Marek had said anything at all. "Two quarters. The flood years' assessments were never written off and the district's account was never restated, so the low ground has been in arrears since before the water came into the kitchens, and the flood years added four more quarters to it, and the account as it stands cannot be got into a column small enough to lend against. I cannot advance seed against it. I could not advance seed against it if the arrears were a day old. If you brought me a low ground with a clean account I would lend to it at the ordinary figure this afternoon and I would be glad to, and I have wanted to, because they are good people and their ground is the best silt in the basin and I have said so in three reports."

"Then the window was shut on the sixth because of the flood years."

"The window was shut on the sixth because of the flood years, and it would have been shut on the fourth, and it will be shut on the fourteenth, and it is not shut because of the fourth clause and it is not shut because of anybody in a good house over a chandler's." He looked down the lane. "It is shut because an account could not be balanced. I would like you to take that away with you and use it, because I have watched men use it the other way for nineteen years and I have always known it would come to a day when I would be standing in a lane with a board."

"Somebody in that lane is lending to the low ground this morning."

"Somebody in that lane is lending to about forty of them, and I have read their paper, and it is not a loan, and it is not usury, and it is legal, and it is on the sixth of a wet morning in a lane with a queue behind it." Vey put his hands behind his back. "I have written the fourth sentence of this speech nine times this week. If I stop being able to say it, I resign, and I have told my clerk that, in writing, this morning, and the clerk is a man of twenty-four and thinks I am being dramatic."

---

The Compact's table was eleven feet down the lane and it was the best thing in the lane by a distance that was not a matter of opinion.

Two trestles and a board over them, and a third board with a slate on it and three figures chalked large, and a covered pail of something hot, and a bench, and a woman in a dry coat behind the table with a ledger and a pen and two clerks at the ends of it, and about forty people standing in the rain in front of it in an orderly queue, and a boy of about fourteen holding the queue in place by saying *that's the end of the list, that's the end of the list, come back tomorrow.*

Nobody was shouting. That was the thing Marek kept coming back to.

The three figures on the slate were the price of a share of seed at the February market, the price of the same share under the paper, and the date the office's board went up.

"That's the whole of it," said Ilse Ferrow, to the lane, to nobody in particular, in the voice of a woman who had said it nine times. "That is what it is at the market, that is what it is on the paper, and that is the day the window shut, and I have put all three up because I am not asking anybody to take a thing on trust from a woman in a good coat."

A man near the front of the queue said, "It's a rate. It isn't a sum."

"It's a rate and it isn't a sum," agreed Ferrow, "and I have said so to about thirty people this week, and the ones it puts off are the ones I want off, because a rate needs a floor and a person who has never missed a floor should not be in my queue at all."

The boy holding the queue was fourteen and had been doing it since the seventh, and he was being paid a shilling a morning out of a tin behind the trestle, and his name was Tam Rell and he had four sisters and a father who was not there. He had worked out the whole of the day's method by himself: the list goes down, you do not get a second place, you come back tomorrow, and the last man in the line is not the least man because he is last, and he had told three separate people this in the first hour and had been shouted at by one of them for wasting time.

Vey had been watching the boy from the shuttered window for about ten minutes when Marek came down the lane.

"That is the fourth day," said Vey, without turning round. "I have stood at that window for ten minutes twice now and I have not gone out there, and I know exactly why I have not, and the reason is that if I stand at that counter and read that board out loud then a man at that trestle will look at me, and the next time the boy holds that queue he will be holding it for me as well as for them. I would be doing it to be seen, and it would work, and it would be the cheapest thing I have ever done."

"Are you going to?"

"No," said Rustam Vey. "I am going to go back inside and write to the district accountant about the flood years' assessments, which will take me until the sixth bell and will put a rate on a piece of paper that somebody has to answer for. If I have done that by the end of the week then this window reopens and this lane empties. And in the ninth month of this year this office is going to send me a letter about a line of forty thousand nine hundred that I have never been able to make head or tail of, and the letter will use the word *advise*, and the district it will want to advise is Tallow, and Tallow does not know that it has been named, and by the time anybody tells it there will be my rate standing on the same account as the rest of it, and that account is four hundred and six people."

Marek had got as far as the fourth figure on the slate when a woman at the front said something to the clerk that made the clerk look up and look past her, down the lane, at a girl of sixteen standing eleven feet away in the rain with her hood up.

---

Wenna Drel had come on the fourth and had been turned away and had come again on the sixth and had been turned away and had come a third time on the tenth, and she had told nobody about the first two, and she told Marek about all three in about nine seconds because she is sixteen and the entire machinery of adult tact was four days old.

"My mother's name is on the roll," she said. "So mine is on the roll too, because mine is on hers. I drew seed. I filled forty-one cones. I have not voted once in my life, I have been in the room for two meetings and I did not speak both times." She was looking at the table. "So I want to ask the woman one thing and then I will go and help Fisk turn the trays."

Marek said, "Ask it."

She asked it. She went the eleven feet and said it to Ilse Ferrow and Marek heard the whole of it because the rain was not making any noise on that lane.

"Is my name on the paper?"

"Under your mother's, on the same line, in the same hand as hers. Yes."

"What does my name being on it do?"

"Nothing at all," said Ferrow. "It is a membership. It means that when a thing is signed for the Share your hand is in it as hers is, and it does not mean you read it, and it does not mean anybody asked you, and it is not a separate thing. You are not being asked anything today. You are not being asked anything this week. Your mother is not here and the instrument says she does not have to be."

"She is ill."

"I know she is ill. I have written her a note in the autumn." Ferrow put the pen down. "I am not going to tell you that you have done a brave thing, because you have not, and you are sixteen, and in this lane everybody is going to tell you that you have. What I am going to tell you is that you came here three times and you were sent away twice, and that is not a thing your mother would have done, and that I have written your name in a second hand on a second line so that there is a piece of paper in this house that says a girl of sixteen stood in a lane and asked, and that paper is worth exactly nothing and it is mine and I keep it."

Wenna Drel looked at the pen.

"You have to sign where she signs."

"That is the line. Yes."

"Will you write that she is ill?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because in nine months somebody is going to come with a form that says a person of sixteen signed for a cooperative in a lane in the rain," said Ferrow, "and there will be nothing on the form about her mother, and I would be the woman who wrote it, and I do not put anything in a form for a person who is not standing in front of me." She dipped the pen and held it out. "Sign where she signs. And take the seed up to Fisk afterwards, and if she asks you why you were down here, tell her, because she will not ask you and she will find out in a week and it is better that you tell her."

Wenna Drel signed on the line where her mother signs.

---

Neris Kade was standing eleven feet away with her notebook inside her coat and she did not do anything at all.

Marek looked at her twice. The first time she was looking at the girl and the second time she was looking at the slate, and between the two times she did not move, and she did not raise her voice, and she did not go in.

"I want to be honest about it at the time and I am going to be honest now," she said, in a voice with nothing in it. "I am an independent examiner for the Rainmarket season works and that is a form and a seal and a district, and there is no box on the form for a girl of sixteen, and I have no standing to speak to a party to an instrument I did not certify, and if I had spoken in that lane there would have been nothing in it — no form, no minute, no name, and a lawyer for the house in four hours would have had the only page of it in the city, written by a woman in a wet coat with no office.

"So I stood eleven feet away, and she is sixteen, and I watched a child put her name where her mother's name is, because the correct thing for me to do was to stand eleven feet away." She turned round and looked at the rain coming down the low ground. "I have been right about that lane for six years and I would be right about it again in a court and I would be wrong at this table. Do not tell me they are the same thing. I have had one of those this week already and I could not carry it."

Marek did not tell her anything. He stood in the lane and let it be the two of them, which was the correct thing, and which was also a coward's thing, and he has known which of those two it was for some time.

Then Ferrow came round the end of the trestle with the ledger closed under her arm and said the thing that was going to be in his head for eleven days.

"There are fewer of them than in the rains, Mr Vale. There were three hundred and something at the sluice in the third week of the water and there are forty-one at my table this morning, and I would like you to notice the two numbers together, and work out which of them I am doing on purpose."

"You are doing both on purpose."

"I am doing the second one on purpose and the first one is arithmetic," said Ferrow. "The price went up and the queue went down and I have had a very good week for a man who came down here to close me."
