# Chapter 52 — The Man Who Is Useful

The Elmsworth seed house is a stone shed with a lime floor and forty-one shallow trays of river sand under oiled paper on a bench along the north wall, and the bench has to be turned twice a day by a person who knows the trays by the marks on the ends of them, and the whole building smells of wet sand, lamp oil, and the sour turn of a thing that is being kept at a temperature and not left alone.

Marek came in off the low ground at the sixth bell with the list in his coat, and Mirren Fisk looked at him from the middle of the floor with her hands in the wet sand of the fourth tray and did not put them down.

"You are from Bell Stair," she said.

"Yes."

"Then you are the recertification, and I have nothing to say to you, and you may stand by the door until I have finished this tray, and I am telling you that so that neither of us is pretending."

"Neris Kade — Examiner — I am told you and she—"

"Examiner was here before the light." Fisk turned the tray with a movement that came from the back and not the wrists. "If you want to know what the Share does you will ask the Share, and the Share is not a building, and I am the building."

---

Neris Kade was on the third tray with a sheet in her hand and she had not used it.

She was thirty years old and she had been Rainmarket's independent examiner for the season since the reckoning turned, which meant that the whole low ground and every yard in it had been hers since January, and that she had been in the seed house four days running and had slept two nights in the loft above the chandler's shop behind the yard.

She did not get up.

"Vale."

"Examiner."

"You have a list in your coat and you have had it since yesterday and you have not come in, and I want it understood that I noticed and that I did not say anything to anybody." She put the sheet face down on the bench. "I am not glad to see you and I am not sorry you are here, and the reason is in that second sentence, and a person who tells you which of the two they mean is a person you can work with."

"Then say the other one."

"I said it."

---

The Share met at the fourth bell, which is not a meeting time and was chosen on purpose.

Sixty-one members and about forty of them in a shed built for twenty, standing, sitting on the seed sacks, a child on somebody's hip in the doorway. The business took an hour and a half. It was the rotation — which member's ground goes in first and which goes in second, decided by a man who had been doing it since the flood years and who had four rules and no opinion. It was the labour days, eleven each, which were the same eleven days that the Share's own loan was pledged against, and a woman at the back asked whether the days were the same days, and the man said the same days, and she said that then they were doing them twice, and there was a noise in the room for about nine seconds.

Then the last business, which is the business that makes a cooperative a cooperative, and which took under four minutes.

Mirren Fisk read out the membership roll. Sixty-one names. It is read out because a cooperative that does not read its roll is a cooperative that has a roll, and this one had been read out at every meeting since the year it was founded. And at the end of the reading, in the ordinary way, at the ordinary hour, with the ordinary pen going in the ordinary book, the chair asked for the rotation to be carried and the hands went up, and forty of the forty-one hands that were in the room went up, and a woman said *carried* and wrote it in the book.

That is how a cooperative of sixty-one people makes a decision. It is a count. It is in the minute book, and the minute book is on the bench by the door, and anybody can read it, and it is the whole of the practice.

When the room emptied Marek said, to Kade, without any preamble: "They carried it on hands."

"They did."

"Sixteen people were not in the room, or he had counted the door and got it wrong."

"Twenty, and you counted the door, and I counted the roll twice. He is in the twenty. He is in the loft asleep, because he is seventy-eight and it is after the fourth bell." Kade folded the sheet and put it in her bag. "And that is how it is done in every cooperative in the Red Uplands and on this low ground, and it is not a scandal, and it is not stupidity. It is a good way for forty people to agree on a rotation without paying a clerk to write down that forty people agreed on a rotation. I have watched four cooperatives do it and I have never once seen it fail."

"It means the meeting has to be the only way a decision gets made."

"It means that, yes."

"And it means that if somebody goes round the sixty-one one at a time, over eight months, and asks each of them the same question separately, then there is no meeting anywhere in that instrument. There is a roll, and there are fifty-two people who each said yes in a lane, and no room." Marek heard it arrive in his own voice and stopped.

Kade looked at him for a while.

"You have spent a month being a man with no office," she said. "So there is no form on which you are asked anything, and no column with your letter beside it, and no office that has to be told when you walk into a room. Which means that you have spent a month being let into rooms, and you have not noticed that you are being let in, because being let in is a courtesy and a courtesy does not leave a mark." She had her bag over her shoulder. "Useful is not standing. You are going to be useful to somebody in Rainmarket inside a week and it is going to be the reason you are in a room you have no business in, and when that happens you are going to find out what you are, in a room, in front of people who can write things down. Somebody is going to take the usefulness off you at the worst possible moment and it will not be malicious. It will be a Tuesday."

"I have not been useful to anybody."

"You are being useful to me," said Kade. "I have had four days in a shed and no clerk, and you came and asked the question nobody asks, which is whether anybody has read the fourth clause. That is a service and I am not going to pretend it is not one. It is also the first thing anybody in this building has done for the Share since the autumn, and you did it by accident, in a coat, with a list."

---

Wenna Drel was on the bench with her sleeves pushed up, filling forty-one little paper cones with seed, which is the job, and which takes a whole morning, and which is not the same as a vote.

She is sixteen and she is on the roll because her mother is, and her mother is the fourth name from the end, and has been ill since the flood year and did not come. Wenna Drel had drawn seed — a double handful of the Ansgar stock, the oldest line in the Share, kept back every year since the founding and never sold — and had filled eleven cones before Marek sat down, and she did not look up at him once.

"You are the clerk," she said.

"No."

"You are the one with the list." She went on filling cones. "Fisk says you have to be here because of the second eye. Fisk says the second eye is a person and not a procedure, and she said it twice, and the first time she said it she was looking at you and the second time she was not."

"What does the second eye do?"

"It looks at it and says yes or no."

"That is what your mother would say."

"My mother would say it wrong," said Wenna Drel. "My mother would say the second eye makes it true. It does not make it true. It makes it so that if somebody comes later with a different figure, there is one other person to ask. That is all. Fisk has told me that twice as well, so I know it twice."

Marek sat on the sack at the end of the bench and looked at the cones.

"What is on the fourth line of the thing I have got?"

"The threshing share," said Fisk from the middle of the floor, without turning round. "Section four is the threshing share. The share is a sixth of whatever comes off that ground, taken at the threshing and not at the weighing, and it is how the house pays for itself, and it is the only part of that instrument the people in this room argue about, and we argue about it every year."

"Right," said Marek.

He had the file in his coat and the fourth clause was in it, and the fourth clause was not the threshing share. The fourth clause was a counterweight: *the district named in the schedule bears the failure of the season favoured.* And the schedule named the Rainmarket low ground, and the Share's seed was going into the Rainmarket low ground, and the clause was correct, and it was the only part of the instrument he had not understood before he walked into this room, and he had not asked, because nobody had ever asked him.

He looked at the woman with her hands in the sand for about ten seconds.

He could not tell her. He had no office, and there was no form on which a man with no office informs a cooperative that its own instrument contains a clause, and even if there were one, he had read it in a file he had no business carrying out of the building, and the second eye on that instrument had a form and a date and a name on it, and the second eye was him, and telling her now would be a man frightening a seed house before sowing with a sentence he could not support and could not repeat under oath.

So he said, "Is there anybody here who has read the whole of it?"

"No," said Mirren Fisk, turning the fifth tray. "You are the first this year. Sit by the door if you like."

He sat by the door until dark, and the lamps came on, and the trays were turned twice, and at the seventh bell in the evening Neris Kade came in out of the dark and sat down on the bench at the end where Wenna Drel was still filling cones by lamplight, and looked at the roll on the wall with the sixty-one names on it, and said:

"Tell me what you found in the fourth clause and I will tell you why the seed house has not read it since the year it was drafted, and I will tell you that before I tell you what is in it."

Marek told her.

She listened all the way to the end without once looking at the paper, and then she said, "Say the last part again."

"*The district named in the schedule bears the failure of the season favoured.*"

"Say the words *named in the schedule* and then say the words *is a person*, and see whether you can get them into the same sentence."

"I cannot."

"No," said Neris Kade. "You cannot, and I have been trying for four days, and I have not said it out loud to anybody either, and there is a tray to turn in an hour, and I would rather it was you who said it in a room than me, because you are the clerk and I am not."
