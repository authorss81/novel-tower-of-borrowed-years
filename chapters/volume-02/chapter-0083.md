# Chapter 83 — One Price

Oren Pike's counting floor opened at the second bell and by the fourth minute of it there were ninety people standing in a room built for forty, and none of them were shouting, and the reason none of them were shouting was that the whole of the room's attention went into four seconds of silence twice an hour.

There is no door on a counting floor. There is a chalked line on the boards and a slate on a post with eleven names on it and a man with a stick. Somebody calls four numbers. It takes about nine seconds for the numbers to go round the room. Then there are four seconds in which nobody moves, because everybody is adding, and then the man with the stick puts the mark on the board, and about ninety people who have all arrived at the same figure say nothing at all.

Marek Vale stood at the end of the boards with his hands behind him and had been in the basin since the second of the seed month and had watched a price being set four times that morning, and had understood all four, and could not have done any of them.

The man with the stick was not Pike. Pike was on the floor and came down the line at about the third call and stood behind his own clerks and said nothing, which is what a man does who has been on a floor for thirty years and trusts it.

---

He found Marek at the boards at the interval and looked at him for a while without saying anything, which two of the eleven on the slate were watching and were not going to enjoy.

"You have had a bad week," he said.

"I have had six refusals in six days."

"Then you have come to the right floor," said Oren Pike, "because this is the only floor in this basin where somebody is paid to be told the truth, and I am about to tell you the truth and it is going to cost you about four pounds."

He said it lightly, and then he stopped being light, and said the rest of it in a voice that four of the eleven on the slate could hear.

"A market in which nobody can read the paper is a market with one price. That is not an opinion, that is arithmetic, and I have counted it, and I have counted it for nineteen years and I will count it for you now.

"On this floor, a house that cannot read a claim will pay me to tell them what the claim says. I have got a price for that and it is on my slate and it is two, and it has been two for eleven years, and I set it at two because I counted who comes.

"Eleven years I have sold two a reading. I know how many a week. It is between sixty and ninety, and the split is the whole of my trade and it has not moved in four years. Two thirds of them are houses: factors, carriers, a man who wants to know whether a flood quarter is a flood quarter. They can afford two. They would pay eight.

"And a third of them are not houses. They are people standing at that end of the floor, and they come for a reading of a rate, because a rate can be read at a water office counter for nothing and they have found out that in the last two months, and they come to me because the counter is four miles off and they cannot read the paper they are being sold.

"They do not pay. They never pay. Some of them put a coin down and I do not know what to do with the coin and it has been in a drawer for eleven years and it is a drawer I would not show anybody. And they take up the same nine seconds as a house, and the same four seconds of silence, and they go away knowing exactly what they know."

He picked the slate up off the post and held it out flat, the way a man holds a thing he wants read.

"Two for a reading. Nine seconds. Sixty to ninety a week. That is my price for reading and it is a market price and I have counted it, and I have never once pretended to anybody that it ought to be different, and I would like it understood in whatever room you are going to build that the two is not a cruelty of mine. It is what the paper is worth to a person who cannot read it, and the paper has been worth that for eleven years, and it will be worth that in a year, and a register that says nothing is charged is a register that has found a new way of charging."

---

"Somebody has to read it out," said Marek.

"Somebody has to read it out and somebody has to pay him, and those are the same sentence, and I have priced both of them for you, and you are not going to like the second one."

He put the slate down on a bale and did the arithmetic in the ordinary way, out loud, in front of the eleven, which is a thing a factor does when he wants it minuted.

"A room four hours a day, four bells. A reader who can read out a rate and a date and a name and can stop when somebody says *that is not what I said*, and somebody who cannot read has to be able to trust that he stopped. That is a trade. That is not a beadle and it is not a clerk and it is not a carrier and it is not one of the eleven on this slate, because every man on this slate is paid to be somewhere else at the end of the week.

"Now, what does that trade cost. I have priced it three ways this morning and I have got three figures and I am going to give you all of them, because the first one is what a market would pay and the second one is what a levy can carry and the third one is the one that decides it.

"A market pays two a reading. So a market pays a man four bells a day at two a reading, four hundred and eighty a season, and there is not one man in this basin who will do it for that, and I have looked, because I look at the young men on this floor every year and I do not want a man off a floor.

"A levy carries something. Call it four a week, the same as the girl in Merrow's shed, and that is the going rate for sitting in a shed four hours a day answering a stranger, and I would not pay my own daughter less. The room wants a person for the whole of the season, and I am not going to stand in this shed and tell my floor how long a season is, because it is not the same season in Rainmarket as it is in the Red Uplands and anybody who says otherwise is selling something.

"So I have priced it twice and it comes to between one hundred and forty and one hundred and sixty, and the figure I will stand behind in a room is one hundred and forty, and it is a hundred and forty a season.

"And a hundred and forty on sixty-one households is a little over two pounds each, and I will tell you what that is at my gate and then I will tell you what it is at yours. At my gate it is a day of a carter. In Tallow Ward, from a woman who sells rope, it is the rope she did not sell on a Tuesday, twice over.

"And every one of those sixty-one households is a household that has already had a rate put on its ground out of a piece of sack with nothing at the head of it in the last month, and I am not the man who says that to the Share in a room. You will have to say it to them in their own yard and I would like to be there when you do, and I do not expect to be asked."

He stopped there and let the floor hear it.

"Or," he said, "somebody in this basin who is neither a market nor a levy."

---

"You have not asked me what I came for," said Marek.

"You have been in this basin since the second of the seed month and you have not once asked a factor for a price and I have been waiting for it since the third," said Oren Pike. "Here it is, and I will say the reason before the figure, because I am not going to be a man in a good coat about this one.

"My house will be the first one in it. I am going to put our paper in, all of it, the buys and the holds and every line we have against the low ground this season, in the order we hold it, with the dates, and nobody at Merrow's is going to be asked for a line of it and neither am I, and I will not be thanked, and I am not doing it out of a sense of anything.

"Here is the reason and I have counted it as well. A claim that cannot be read is a claim that cannot be told apart from any other claim. In this basin right now the low ground's flood quarters, a district's dead rate, and a woman's eleven trays of sand are the same object to a buyer: paper. And there is one price for paper, and the price is set by the people who cannot tell the difference, and the people who cannot tell the difference are the ones who have the least money in the room.

"Put my paper in and it can be told apart. From that day on, a claim that is a district is a different object from a claim that is a carrier, and they get different prices, and the difference between those two prices is worth more to a man on this floor than anything anybody has offered me this season. I am going to make money out of your register. I am telling you that in front of my own floor so that there is one record of it, and I would like the record kept in words I would have written myself."

"How much of your margin," said Marek.

"Less than a house thinks and more than I would like," said Pike, "and I have not got the figure and I will not invent one in front of eleven men, and I will have it by the end of the month and you may have it then and it will not be a good number."

---

The one that landed was not Pike's, and it came from the man at the end of the boards with his hands in a rope-string, and Neris Kade had not been in the room for any of it and came in at the interval and heard the last of it.

"You will want to know," she said, "whether I think a factor should put his own paper in a public register, and I am an examiner and not a member of this floor and I cannot vote on anything here, and nobody is going to thank me for the next four minutes.

"I think it should be necessary. A register that only houses can read is a schedule, and a schedule is a thing the office sends round, and you said so yourself in a corridor on Bell Stair six days ago, Mr Vale, and I heard you say it and I have not forgotten it and I am not going to let it be the only sentence anybody in this room has heard this month.

"And I think it should be paid for out of a levy, and I think the levy should be a figure a person can carry, and I have just listened to you arrive at one, and I would like to know what you would do with it, and I know what you would do with it because I know what I would do with it, and neither of us is going to be in the room when it is collected."

Pike looked at her for a moment.

"Examiner," he said.

"That is what I am."

"Then I will say the thing to you that I will not say to him, and it is that I have not got a number and you are right that I have not, and I have had a clerk on this floor trying to put one on that slate since the sixth of the seed month and he cannot, and neither can I, because a levy is not a price. A price is what a thing is worth to somebody who wants it. A levy is what a body has decided to take off itself in order to keep something running, and there is nobody in the basin who has ever had to work out the second of those and there is no form for it, and I have been in this basin since before you could read a rate."

He put the slate back on the post.

"You are right that it is not a good number and I have not got one. Do not put that in your book as the Fund's woman being right. I have not got a number."

---

Marek walked out at the fifth bell and got as far as the boards and was overtaken at the gate by a man he had met once, on a terrace four days' road off, who had bought a claim on the low ground's flood quarters for four hundred and ten pounds in the bad spring and had been married in the flood year and whose daughter's wedding money it had been.

Josip Malnar had come down off the road for a barrow load of boards and had not come for the register and had not known any of this was going to happen.

"You are the man who has been in my yard twice," he said, in a good coat that was not a good coat. "Do not tell me anything about my paper. I have not read it and I am not going to and I told you that in my own yard before you asked me twice, and I am telling you now because there is a man in there asking people to put their figures in a public book and I would like you to know what my four hundred and ten pounds is, since it is about to be in it.

"Four hundred and ten pounds, out of a daughter's wedding money, for a piece of a thing, at a discount, from a man with a table and a stool in a yard, who was honest with me. She is married. It was a good wedding. That is the whole of what my money is."

"I am not going to say anything about your paper."

"Then say this instead, because you are a man who reads things in rooms and there is going to be a room," said Malnar. "When they say the number of people who will bear the failure, and they put a district in it, I want somebody to say the district is not me. I know it is not me. I have known it since the second day of the month and I have said so twice and neither of you has written it down. It is not mine and I cannot pay it and I never bought it, and I am on a piece of paper in a row of fifty-two and I am the only name on it that has ever been anybody's daughter."

Marek Vale stood in a gate on the low-ground road and understood that he had a sentence in his mouth that he could not put anywhere, and that the person who had earned it had said it to him in a gate instead of to the room, and that this was now the fourth time this summer somebody had handed him the true thing on their way out of a building.

He said, "I will say it in a room when there is a room, and I will say it in the order you have said it, and it will be your words and not mine, and I will not be thanked for it and neither will you."

"That is the first sensible thing anybody has said to me since the second of the month," said Josip Malnar, and got his barrow out of the gate.
