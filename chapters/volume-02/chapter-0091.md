# Chapter 91 — Advise

The ninth month came in on a wet morning and the levy office on Bell Stair did not notice it, because a levy office does not notice months. The only thing in the building that had noticed was a man of fifty-eight who had said a number out loud in the second month and had been right, and who had then put the letter in a tray and gone on doing the day's work, and who had not said the number again since.

The frame was on the wall where it had been since the sixth of the rains, turned back over the pale rectangle in the plaster, and it was on a wall that men walked past about nine times a day, and nobody in the room looked at it, because the frame is the furniture of that office in the way a door is furniture.

---

The man who came up the hill with the ninth month's paper came on foot, and he was about twenty-six, and he was carrying it in a leather tube, and he apologised for the tube.

"I am sorry to be without a proper hand for it," he said. "There is a proper hand for it. He is at the sluice and the sluice is where he is."

"That is a worse answer than most people manage at twenty-six," said Grale. "Give it here."

It was one sheet and it was not long, and it carried the water office's seal, and Grale read it standing, which is what he does, and then he read it again, which is also what he does, and both times the copy room went on around him and neither reading took more than half a minute.

*Reconciliation of the district account to the year-end carry. The sum standing unapplied is required to be applied in the ninth month. The district named is to be charged and the charge entered. The office will wait until the close of the month.*

"There," said Grale. "Sit down, do not sit down, it is a corridor. Tell me the word in the second one."

The young man said, carefully, the way a young man says a word he has been sent to say.

"The word is *charged*."

"And in the first one, which came into this office in the second month and is in a drawer in this building with my own hand on the routing slip—"

"*Advise*," said the young man. "It says *kindly exhibit the line and advise the district*."

"That is right," said Grale. "You have read it. You have never been to Tallow Ward."

"No, sir."

"You are not going to," said Grale. "Nobody from that office is going to, and that is not your fault and it is not the office's, and I will tell you why in one sentence and then you may go down the hill. There is a district's name on a piece of paper and the district is four hundred and six people and a ratepayer in this basin may read a rate and may take a number off a counter and may not ask a single question, because there is no form on which a person asks one, and a question asked without a form is an opinion, and an opinion about four hundred and six people that gets into a record in a building they have never been in is a thing they cannot answer in a year. Go down the hill."

The young man went down the hill with the tube, and did not understand any of it, and did not need to, and has never been in a room in Tallow Ward and is not a party to anything that happened in this volume.

---

Grale put the sheet face down on the corner of the desk, which is a thing he does with a document he is going to think about for a long time, and then he went and did the day's work, because it was the fourth bell and the day's work does not care.

Here is what a district account is, and it is worth saying once, in a room, because nobody in Morrowfen has ever said it and every office in this city works on it.

A district account is a page. It is kept in this office, in a book, on a shelf with the other books. It shows what is owed to a district: what a ratepayer owes, what a standpipe owes, what a yard owes, what a clinic's coal owes. It is not a record of money. It is a record of claims, and money is only ever a thing that happens when somebody in an office decides a claim has been dealt with, and decides it by writing a line in a book, and the line has a date on it, and then the claim is off the page, because a claim that has been paid is not owed and an account shows what is owed.

The standing carry is a line on that page that has been entered once a year since before the flood years and has never been applied to anything. Forty thousand nine hundred. A district's name went beside it on the seventeenth of the rains, when a board of five named a district for the first time in three hundred years, and a district's name beside a number turns a number into a debt, and a debt has somebody standing behind it, and the somebody is a district.

It is not the households' money. Grale has said that out loud in a yard and in a room and it is the sentence he is most careful with. What a yard pays at the ordinary wage is drawn from the households and never comes through that account. The recited reserve is a line in a book in this office and it has been there for three hundred years and it has never touched anybody's pail.

---

The problem with the ninth month's paper is that it is asking for a thing that cannot be done in this room by anybody.

A sum standing unapplied cannot be applied. It can only be applied against something, and the only things a sum can be applied against are the accounts it is offset against, and the offset is made by holding the account of the district it is named against to its presented figure, so that the carried sum comes out of the account and the account is smaller by that much and the ward is a ward that has paid.

Holding a district account to its presented figure is a cut.

There is one cut order permitted in this basin. It is a written order, and it is held in a frame, and the frame is nailed to the wall of this office because this is where the account is, and there is no copy of it anywhere in the kingdom, and the water office is permitted to issue it and not permitted to let anybody have one. It is read, in the words of the office, to every party it names, before it is entered. It is not entered at the close of a month. It carries the day and not the month. And it carries the name of the officer who enters it.

The officer who enters it is the officer of this office, and the officer of this office is Grale, and he has cut four orders in that time and signed two of them, and there is one on the wall at this moment with a name under the last line in his own hand.

"The paper wants a cut," he said, to the room, at about the fifth bell, to nobody in particular, in the voice he uses. "And I am not going to do a cut because a sheet came up the hill in a tube. A cut is not a filing. A cut is a thing I go and do, at six parties, on foot, and one of them is a business with nobody to write beside its own line, so I walk up there and read it to a woman with her hands in sand and I do not ask her anything, and I go home at the seventh bell and I do the day's work the next morning with it on me."

He turned the sheet over and looked at the seal.

"And if I do a cut in the ninth month of this year," he said, "then it is the second one of the year, and there is one permitted, and the next one anybody wants will be a request from a man in a good coat and there will be no answer to it, and I will be the man who made the first two in one year and there will be no third. That is the arithmetic of it and I did it in about nine seconds standing here and I have been doing it in one form or another since I was thirty-five."

Nobody in the copy room said anything, because there are eleven people in it at the fifth bell and two of them are owed money by the hall.

---

Marek Vale had come up the hill at the second bell to look at a thing that was not his business, which is what he does now.

The recertification list of twenty-two files came out of the Chamber's post in the second month and it came due back on the twelfth of that month and it is still in his coat, and there is a pencil line on the back of it that is a sentence and not a document, and there is no box anywhere in this city for a sentence, and he has stopped looking for one. He had come up the hill because the levy office keeps the district rolls and a roll is a thing he can read, and because in the ninth month somebody in the hall had asked him, in the ordinary course and without interest, to check one column of the Tallow account against the Gate tank's own count, and that is a piece of work that a man with no office can be handed because it produces nothing.

He had it in front of him on a spare corner of a table and he had been at it for two hours, and he had found that the Gate tank's own count and the district account do not agree, and that the difference is nine names, and that all nine of the nine are on a line headed with a word he did not recognise and dated some years back, and that the word is a kind and not a place and not a person.

He wrote it on the back of the recertification list, under the pencil sentence, in pencil, in his own hand, in two words: *a kind?*

And then he did not write anything else, because a man with no office who has written *a kind?* on the back of a list in a room in a hall has put a question into a record that nobody can produce and that he cannot answer, and the ninth month was four days old and the frame was on the wall and the sheet was face down on Grale's desk.

He did not ask Grale about it. He had come up the hill to ask Grale about it at the second bell and he had got as far as a table and a column and nine names, and at the fourth bell he understood that he was not going to ask, and that the reason was not the nine names, and that the reason was that Grale had said a sentence out loud in a yard in the rains and had not repeated it since and there was a charcoal line on a board in a wet yard and about forty people's memory, and that a man who has a condition like that and has kept it for twenty-three years is not a man you walk up to in a copy room and ask a question.

So he put the sheet away and went down the hill, and Grale watched him go, and neither of them said one word to the other, and that is the whole of what passed between them in the ninth month up to the twelfth.

---

At the seventh bell Grale turned the sheet over again and read the last line of it, which was the office's own closing courtesy.

*The office will wait until the close of the month.*

"Four hundred and six people," he said. "Two charges in one week and I have only been sent one of them, and I am the man both of them have to go through, and I have been in this chair nineteen years and I have wanted to be out of it since I was thirty-five and I have a condition and the condition is not a wish."

He put the sheet in the tray. He did not write a minute about it, because a minute is a record and a record is a thing somebody can be shown, and the day he proves the line in the account desk he has to stop, and a man who has decided to stop does not begin keeping records in his last month.

The tray went up to a desk upstairs and the day went on, and in Tallow Ward the shutter went over the Gate tank at the seventh bell as it does every night, and the register that had been shut since the twenty-ninth of the harvest month was still shut, and the second collecting morning of the levy was on the trestle in the yard and had been since the fourth bell and had been a box and a slate with no total on it and two people of the Share and about forty people putting coin in it and seed in the bottom of sacks, and the yard's reason was given out loud about nine times in two hours and was the same reason it had been given at the other trestle: a total read in a room in six months is a number of people who agreed with something, and none of them had agreed to anything, and all of them had been asked.

And a man who has nothing pays nothing and is not entered, and a member who pays in kind pays in seed, and the cap that made those two sentences correct was written in a Fund secretary's own words on the twenty-fourth of the harvest month in a Crown March hand, on a nail, in the open, about nine people in Tallow Ward had read it, and it was going to produce a person who does not exist by about the third week of the month, and nobody was going to be able to do anything about it, because there is no form in this kingdom on which anybody asks a girl of sixteen whether she minds.
