# Chapter 57 — The Price of a Market

The grain floor calls its prices at the second bell in the morning and is quiet for the four seconds of the call and deaf for the eleven minutes after it.

Oren Pike's house owns the east end of it and has owned it for thirty years, and at the fifty-first year of his life he came down to the floor at half past the one and stood at the third pillar with his hands behind him and watched a clerk chalk four numbers onto a board, and then he said, without turning round:

"Your hall's second eye has had a bundle on his table since yesterday and he has not come down here, and I want to know whether that is because he is slow or because he has found something."

"How would I know what you want?"

"Because you would have told me by the fourth," said Oren Pike. "I have been in this room for thirty years, Mr Vale, and a thing that is going to cost me money comes down the stairs before the middle of the day, and you have not come down, which means either you found it or you found nothing and are ashamed. I would rather have the first. Sit down. The floor is no use talking on it for an hour."

---

The floor of a grain exchange is the least thoughtful room in Morrowfen and it is where the price of a loaf is made, and Marek stood at the third pillar for an hour and learned two things about Oren Pike that nobody would have told him.

The first was that he had been kind to the eleven men on his slate. A factor on the west side called Ilar Betz had a bad week in the third month and owed the house nine hundred and he came in on the fourth morning and told Pike he would put his name to anything, and Pike made him stand on the board and read the amount out loud in the ordinary voice, and then gave him a job at a figure that did not pay Pike anything, and Betz said nothing about it then and told two people about it afterwards, and both of them were on the floor within the week because that is how a floor works.

The second was the call itself, at the second bell.

There is no bell for it. A man at the west end lifts a hand, the floor goes quiet, and four numbers are called out by one voice in about nine seconds, and the chalk goes on the board, and then there is a silence of about four seconds in which about ninety people who have come to shout do not shout, and after that it is as loud as before. In that four seconds the whole day's price is set, and it is set by men who cannot read a single instrument among the papers on their own desks, and every one of them has an opinion about whether the Compact is a good thing and not one of them has ever read the fourth clause of a seed instrument.

"You see it," said Pike, when the board was up and the floor had gone loud again. "That is what I am buying. It is not the paper. I could write the paper out in a morning. I am buying the four seconds."

He is fifty-one and he is not a large man and he does not perform. He had a room behind the floor with a bench of chalk, a slate, and eleven names on a slate, and the eleven names are the factors who work for him, and he read the slate out to Marek without being asked because he is a man who believes a thing should be said once in the right room.

"I do not want to stop the Compact and I am going to save you the trouble of trying to make me sound noble about it. I want to price it."

"You cannot price a bundle of fifty-two rate claims."

"I can price anything. That is the entire trade." He wiped the slate. "Here is what the paper does to this floor, and I will give you the number and not the theory. A share of the low ground's seed is worth four hundred and eighty at a good hearing and six hundred and twenty at the one you are going to get, because the paper pays for seed at a cost of capital that the Trust will not lend at, and the difference is not a margin, it is a market. Now. A household that has signed that paper has sold me the right to collect its water rate at a discount and I have sold it on. That rate is in the district account. The district account is in every price on this floor, because the district account is where the seed contracts get settled and the contracts settle against the low ground. So a bundle of fifty-two assigned claims in the low ground is not a cost to the market. It is a supply of collectable water. And when there is a supply of collectable water, the price of the seed does not go up by a margin. It goes to four hundred and eighty, because I can now put a floor under the collection and you cannot."

"Which means the Share gets a worse hearing and everybody else gets a better price."

"Which means the Share gets a worse hearing and everybody else gets a better price," said Oren Pike, "and I have been in this room for thirty years and there are eleven names on my slate and every one of them has a woman and a kitchen behind him, and not one of them has ever once asked me in whose hearing the seed was priced. They ask me about the weather."

He put the slate down on the bench.

"And here is the second half, and it is the half I do not say at the rate call because it makes me sound like a man who has read a book. When I have priced it I cannot unprice it. There is no way of taking an assigned claim out of a district's collection and putting it back. It is gone. Not delayed, not suspended, gone into a bundle that has been sold to four houses and a factor's widow. So the day after I buy the paper the low ground's collection is worth less to the district than it was, and the district will feel that in about four months, in the form of the cut order, and there is a man on your hill with a frame on a wall who will have to decide where it lands." He looked up. "I do not care who is owed. I care that I cannot read the thing I am trading."

---

"You have had your own clerks read it," said Marek.

"Two of them, at my own cost, in my own time, and I will say now that I did not get them to do it and I did not have to, because I have a buyer on the west side who has been trying to read a Compact paper since the floods and who came to me about it and could not sleep. They read it in a day and a half and they came back and they said the thing that I have been saying to you for ten minutes, which is that it is not a lie." He sat down on the bench, which was not an invitation to sit. "What did your clerk do?"

"He counted it twice and it balances to the penny."

"To the penny," said Oren Pike, with something that was not quite satisfaction. "Then that is the end of that line of inquiry and I am sorry for your week."

---

"Ansgar Drel," said Marek, later, when the floor had gone quiet and the chaff had settled and the two of them were sitting on the bench with the tar smell coming up off the boards.

Pike did not pretend.

"Seventy-eight. Sixty years old when he joined that Share and it is the oldest stock in the basin and it is in that bench under oiled paper being turned twice a day by a woman of thirty-four." He said it evenly. "I offered him a hundred and forty for a quarter of it in the bad spring, before you people were ever involved, and he took it, and it was a good price, and I have never had a moment's doubt about the price."

"And?"

"And I have known every year since that it was the price at which you stop." He looked at the floor. "I am not going to stand here and be told off by a clerk for that, because you would have taken the same price if you had had a house and eleven names, and the reason I do not sleep is not Ansgar Drel. The reason I do not sleep is that I could have paid him two hundred and forty and I did not, and I can put the two hundred and forty in a box, and there is no form in this city for a man to put a number in a box and say *that is what I decided about Ansgar Drel and nobody was to know*."

---

"Your brother," he said, at the door.

Marek stopped.

"Your brother is the cheapest competent bridgewright in this basin and he is working for a ward at the ordinary wage three seasons of eleven days at a time, and he is on a schedule that a Crown March representative cannot tell you is lawful because the countersignature is on a road between here and the coast." Oren Pike said it the way he said prices. "I am going to tell you why I know every figure of that, because you are going to assume I am being cruel, and I am, and I want to be exact.

"Your hall cost your brother twenty-six months of his own recovery in a month in which he did not get a day of anybody's kindness, and it bought him a piece of paper with a schedule on it, and the piece of paper is at a Crown March office on a road that does not come into the city until the autumn, and the wage is the ward's ordinary wage, and the ward's wage is coming out of a line that was dead three hundred years and that a board named a district against in a yard in the rain. So your brother is on the public at a rate that exists because of a decision made in your own city, by people who were in a room, in the open, with his brother's wife in it." He put the slate back on its hook. "I do not say it to take it off you. I say it because when the cut comes, and it will come, the north-road span is the first thing a man with my sheet looks at, and I would rather you had heard it from me at fifty-one than from me at fifty-five."

Marek walked up the causeway and up the hill and did not have an answer, because there was no answer to that and there had not been one since the eighteenth of the rains, and the man who had said it had no interest in him whatever and had given him a competitor's figures for nothing at the fourth bell of the morning because he could not sleep.

At the desk there were seventeen files left on the list and six days, and the fourth line of the list was a bundle that balanced to the penny, and he had not gone near it since the fourth, because there was nothing to go near.

He did the fourth bell and the fifth and the sixth, and the column of second-eye counts came down the left side of the tally in his own hand, and it was his own name seventeen times over by the time the copy room shut, written seventeen times, and a man who has written a name down seventeen times has made seventeen small claims on a thing that is about to be examined by somebody else.

The card was in his coat. It had a figure on it and an address and it had been there three days, and he put his hand on it once and left it there.
