# Chapter 51 — Nine Months

The letter was on Grale's desk at the first bell with the wet still on it, and it had come inside the city rather than out of it, which was the part that made him stand there for a while before he picked it up. Nine days ago the reckoning had turned. The rain had not done anything for a month. The Bell Weir was still at its limit and would be at its limit for another three weeks, and the four Tallow yards were drawing their water off a board at the Gate tank, and the wage on that board was being paid out of a line of the district account that had been entered once a year since the flood years and had never been applied to anything by anybody in the history of the building.

Forty thousand nine hundred. A board had put a district's name beside it, and a district's name beside a number turns a number into a debt, and Grale had said that in a yard on the seventeenth of the rains with about four hundred people standing in the rain listening, and it had been true then.

Now the water office was writing to ask about it.

He read it twice. It was not a demand. That was the thing he kept coming back to while the copy room filled up around him and the lamps came on along the north wall by degrees, the way they do; it was a query, in the plainest form that office has, and it asked the levy office to show the line and to state what the line is charged against.

*We are reconciling the district account against the year-end carry and find a district named against an unapplied balance. Kindly exhibit the line and advise the district.*

Grale put it down flat on the desk with the wet print still coming through onto the blotter, and he said, out loud, to nobody, which he had been doing for eleven years in a building where nobody was listening:

"Nine months."

Then he said it again, because it was worth hearing twice, and then he sat down and did the day's work with a question in his head that was not about four hundred pounds' worth of money.

"It is not about the money," he said, to Ostrowsky, who had come in at the quarter and was hanging his coat on the same nail he had hung it on for nineteen years. "Four thousand— forty thousand nine hundred. It is a small sum for that office. They spend that in a week on a sluice. It is about the word *district*."

Ostrowsky, who was fifty-eight and had signed nine hundred settlement countersignatures and had never in his life asked where a fourth clause went, said, "They want to know what it is against."

"They know what it is against. They are the ones who named it. Three days after a board named it." Grale turned the letter round so the desk was looking at the seal. "That is a question with a word in it, and the word is *named*, and what I want to know is what happens in nine months to a line with a word in it, and there is nobody in this city I can ask, and I have not got nine months, and that is exactly how long it will be."

"Where are you going to look?"

"I am going to look at it every day for nine months," said Grale, "and one of these days it is going to be somebody else's turn to look at it, and that is the whole of my plan and it is not a good one."

He put the letter in the tray, and the tray went up to a desk upstairs, and the day went on.

---

On Bell Stair the assignment column is a strip of board about nine feet long with a hook on the end of it, and a clerk's file hangs on the hook for as long as the file is his, and the file comes down when the file is finished, and the letter beside the name is the grade. First grade, second grade, third grade, and above the third grade, for the officers, a small painted board that has no letter on it at all and never has.

Marek Vale stood in front of it at the eighth bell and there was no letter beside his name.

The slip had come down off the column on the morning of the eighteenth, footed *grounded in a paper produced to the board*, and he had read it standing at the bottom of the hill on his way up and had not gone into the hall that day because he was not clerk of the day for anything. He had been in the building every day since. He had been to the copy room, the Threshold, and the chandler's shop under the window where the posting board is kept dry. He had read a hundred pages of other people's recertifications for nothing, because a man who is employed and not assigned has no business doing that either and has to do it anyway to be let in.

Sedge came past with a tray and stopped, which he does not do.

"You have been on that board four days."

"Yes."

"You have not asked me about it."

"You have not been asked," said Sedge, "and I have been four days finding somebody to ask." He put the tray down on the sill, which he does not do either. "There is no form. I want to be exact with you, because the last month of your life was exactness. There is no form that says a clerk of the Chamber is employed and unassigned, and there is no box for him on that board, and the reason there is no box is that the board was cut for a building where a clerk was always somebody's file and a file was always somebody's business, and in four weeks you have become the only man on this hill whose file belongs to no room, and the strip of board has no opinion about that and neither have I."

"Is the Chamber going to say something in writing?"

"The Chamber has said something in writing. It came down on the eighteenth and it said eight words and one of the eight was *paper*, and the eight words are the reason you are standing in front of a board with nothing beside your name, and I would ask you to consider that they were said in your favour and were also the reason." Sedge picked the tray up again. "Nobody is going to be unkind to you about it. That is the arrangement. It is also the situation, and you may have either."

---

He went down the hill at the interval, which is a thing he had not done in five weeks and which took eleven minutes longer than it used to, because a man who is not going anywhere has no reason to walk fast and a man who has a letter in his coat does.

The board at the Tallow Gate tank is four columns and about two hundred names and it is written up on a Monday and corrected when somebody's household changes, and the tank is the ward's own count and the four yards' counters read the rest, and the Ropewalk standpipe is short again and has been boiled on the ration for a fortnight, which is a sentence in a yard and not a thing anybody writes down. Three women were at the pump. A man from the Tanner's yard was filling a can for a dog, and the dog was not a dog that needed filling, and nobody said anything about it.

Lio was sitting on the low wall with a slate and a stick of chalk and nine names on the slate, because the school is shut and has been shut since the sixth of the month before, and a shut school in this ward does not send a child home, it sends her out to a place where people are and sends her home at dusk with an answer to questions nobody asked her.

"You have been in a room all morning," she said, without looking up.

"I have."

"You have not told me one thing that happened in it."

"No," said Marek, "and if I did you would write it on the slate and take it to the low ground, and the low ground would have it by the fourth bell tomorrow, and it would be true, and it would be the only true thing anybody had, and that is how a thing stops being anybody's."

She considered that and put the chalk down, which was a concession and more than she usually made.

"Is the yard going to go back out?"

"I do not know."

"You are in a room every day," said Lio, "and you do not know that. That is the whole of what I came to say and I have said it and now I am going to write the Ropewalk woman's name on the slate because hers is the only one I do not have."

He let her write it. Then he said, "If you ever find out, tell me, and I will not tell anybody where I got it."

"You have told me three times that you will not tell anybody where you got it," said his brother's daughter, "and the fourth time you will say it in a way that means you have, and I shall want to be somewhere else."

He asked after her grandfather's span and she said the north road had got a new bad place on the second and that her father was on it and that he had sent a message by a carter on Thursday that said only *we are holding*, and that she had copied it out on the slate because it was the only thing in the world he had written for her that month.

Then she told him, at some length and in his brother's exact voice, including the pause before the last word, that the second pair of boots by the door at the rooms was his if he was going to be walking on ground that had been under water, and that Tomas had said *there is no point in a good pair over a bad road*, and that Lio had been given this message and that Tomas had not given her any other message at all, and that she considered this interesting.

They were a size too big. He got them and wore them down the causeway and up the hill and they were a size too big all the way, and he put them on the end of his own desk at the interval beside a coat he had not worn since the floods, and a man coming into a hall in a pair of boots that are not his is a man who has said something about himself without opening his mouth.

---

The recertification list came out at the third bell, and it came out because twenty-two seasonal instruments were going to be sealed in the same fortnight and the second clerk's eye on a seasonal instrument is a person and not a procedure, and there was one person available.

Marek read the covering note standing up.

*A seed instrument cannot be sealed on one clerk's eye. Where the season's instruments are numerous the second eye is taken from the list of unassigned clerks. The list is closed at the sixth bell on the third of the month. The return is due before the seed goes in on the twelfth.*

Underneath the note, twenty-two lines, and every line a district or a house or a yard, and every line a loan of seed or a threshing share or a roof, because the low ground had had a bad autumn and then a flood and then a bad spring, and the whole basin does its borrowing in the same fortnight, which is nobody's design and everybody's habit.

He read to the fourth line.

*The Elmsworth Seed Share. Cooperative. Sixty-one members. Recertify. Loan of 2,400, sealed the ninth day of the bad autumn, MR 411.*

Sedge was at the desk behind him and did not say anything at all.

"All twenty-two," said Marek.

"All twenty-two."

"Two clerk's eyes, and I am the second clerk's eye, and you are the first."

"You are the second clerk's eye on the list of unassigned clerks and I am the first clerk's eye because I am supervising and the supervisor's eye is the chamber's, and neither of those is a compliment, and both of them are the job." Sedge turned back to his own work and then, in a manner that suggested it had been sitting in him since the second bell: "You have four days. If the second eye is wrong on the twelfth, the instrument is not sealed, and the seed does not go into the ground, and the seed does not wait. It is not a filing. I have seen it happen twice in nine years and both times it was a seed year and both times the people came back to the Threshold in the autumn and asked whether anybody had been told."

"Then it is not a filing," said Marek.

"No," said Sedge. "Everything in this building that anybody tells you is a filing is not a filing, and you have been here five weeks and I would expect you to have learned that. The filing is the part where we write down that we could not do the other thing."

He took the list and read it again standing up, and the twenty-second line at the bottom was somebody's roof, and above it the fourth line was sixty-one names on one seal, and it was four o'clock in the afternoon of the second day of the month in which the whole basin was going to owe the same week.

The third line was the Rill Yard threshing share and the fourth was the Share, and Marek noticed — because it was the sort of thing he could not stop noticing, and it was going to cost him all month — that nobody in Rainmarket had drawn a threshing share this year, because nobody in Rainmarket had any threshed grain worth sharing, and the low ground had spent its whole seed stock in the autumn and had bought in February at a price that had made a seed merchant's widow leave the Witness Room of the Elmsworth Share in tears in front of two neighbours.

He wrote the twenty-two numbers on the inside of his own cover, because that is what he did, and he was aware while he was writing them that he had done it in a form.

Then he went down the hill to walk it before dark, and it was the second of the month, and the reckoning was nine days old, and a line of forty thousand nine hundred in a district account had been asked about by the water office in one sentence, and nobody in Morrowfen had to answer it until the ninth month.
