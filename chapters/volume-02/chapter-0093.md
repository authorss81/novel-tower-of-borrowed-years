# Chapter 93 — Twice

The lamp went out on the sixth day of the ninth month and the office sent a man to tell the seed house about it, and the man was Grale, because the office is permitted one such order and the officer who enters it is the officer who reads it, and a business has nobody to write beside its own line, and the rule has been the same since before anybody in this basin was born.

He walked up the low-ground road on foot at about the fourth bell with the frame's wording copied onto a sheet in his own hand, which is not a copy of the order, because there is no copy of the order and he has never made one and would not know how.

Mirren Fisk had the lamp lit when he came, because it was still lit, because nothing had yet come off the ground. It was a good lamp and it was the lamp the seed house works its trays under and it had been lit every day since the sixth of the month before, and it went out at about eleven minutes past the fourth bell, and Grale watched it go out, and did not say anything about the eleven minutes, because the eleven minutes are not a rule and there is no form in this kingdom for eleven minutes.

She listened with her hands in the sand of a tray, the way she listened to him in the seed month, and at the end of it she said one thing.

"Go on, then," said Mirren Fisk. "Say the part you have not said yet, because you have read it to me and you have stopped and there is another half of it."

"The office supplies a lamp," said Grale. "In the third month. And not before. That is not a threat and it is not even an office's decision. It is how a shed is run, because a shed is a shed and a lamp is a lamp, and the order is a line in a list and the list is cut in the order the lines stand, and your line is the last line of the first order of this year and I signed that order on the twentieth of the seed month and my hand is under it."

"Then we are working in the cold," said Mirren Fisk.

"Yes."

"Not the field," she said. "Say the part where it is not the field, because I have had a day of thinking about this since the twentieth of the seed month and I have got it down to one thing and I would rather you confirmed it than that I said it."

"It is not the field," said Grale. "The second sowing is in the ground and the ground does not want a lamp. It is the bench."

"Eleven of sixty-one," said Fisk. "Nine on the ninth and two on the eighteenth, and the shed they came out of was shut up wet in the flood year, and I have said all of that out loud in this yard twice this season and I am not going to say it again for a man from an office."

Then she said the other thing, and she said it in the flat voice of a keeper of a seed house who has run one for eleven years and has never once in eleven years been asked whether the bench mattered.

"I would like you to hear this once and then never repeat it, and you will not repeat it, and that is not a favour either, it is that there is nothing at the end of it to repeat. Our own rule turns a lot that does not come up into eleven days of labour, and then into a third of another member's kept-back, and then into a rate on a household's ground, and there is no form in this kingdom in which a rate on a member's ground goes. It goes nowhere. It is a piece of sack with nothing at the head of it. I have written eleven of them on a piece of sack and I keep it in my house and I have never shown it to anybody and I am not going to now.

"So a lamp goes out in the ninth month and comes back in the third, and the bench stands cold for six months, and whatever comes up or does not come up in that time goes on a sack with nothing at the head of it, and in about two years a woman on this road is going to be standing where you are standing with a number she cannot pay in money and in seed she does not have, and she is going to be told it is a rule, and she is going to say yes, because there is nothing else to say, and that is how it was decided and by whom and on what day will be nobody's business to say.

"I am not asking you for anything," said Mirren Fisk. "There is nothing in this room for a man from an office to do and you have come up a road for it and I would not have come down."

Grale stood in the seed house yard with the sheet in his hand for a moment and then he put the sheet inside his coat, which he had not intended to do, and said:

"I am required to read it to you and I have read it. I am not required to say anything else and I have said the rest and I would not have."

"You would not have," said Fisk. "That is the first time in eleven years anybody in this house has said that to me about a lamp."

---

He came back down the low-ground road at the fifth bell and went up the hill and the ninth month's sheet was still face up on the corner of his desk, and the day went on, and in Tallow Ward the shutter came off the Gate tank at about two o'clock and the trestle was out for the second week of the collection, and about forty people came up the lane in the wet and put something into a box, mostly coin, and seed in the bottom of sacks, and there was no total on the slate and the reason there was no total was given out loud about nine times in two hours.

Bartel Reisz came up the lane at about the sixth bell and he had been up the hill, and he had been to the water office counter, and a ratepayer in this basin may read a rate, and he had read the rate charged on the plate on the door of the third shed of the carriers' row between the Tallow Gate and the north road.

He is fifty-eight. He has thirty-one years in that shed. He has a bad knee in wet weather and he is not a man people make a fuss of, and he came down the lane at the pace of a man walking with a thing in his head, and he came to the yard, because a yard is where you take a thing in this ward, and he was not wrong to.

"Nine names," he said, to nobody in particular, and about six people heard him. "I have carried that plate since my father's time and I have been in arrears two seasons in thirty-one years and never three, and there is a sum on it now that was not on it in the seed month, and it is forty-three and some months, and there is a house at the end of it."

"Somebody help him," said Marta Brel, from the weighbridge end.

"No," said Reisz. "Let him say it, he has come down a hill to say it and I am not going to make him say it twice." He turned round. "Forty-three and some months, at a discount, and the discount is the old sort, the one a man gives you in a yard and calls a kindness, and at the end of it there is a house. I have never heard of the house. I have bought nothing. I have sold nothing. I have owed that plate two seasons and I have paid it both times out of my own pocket and out of my own pocket for the year before, and I could not tell you today what I owe you for the year before last, because nobody has ever come and asked me."

"You wanted to pay it yourself," said Aldis Kern, from the step of the tank. "You put it in writing. Eight days ago, in a lane, to a clerk who was not the one who wrote it down."

"Then a clerk wrote it down."

"A clerk wrote it down in a book," said Kern, "with no letter beside your name in it, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a man may be given a piece of paper saying *I will pay this*, and that is not the clerk's fault and it is not yours, and I have been a captain of this yard's water for a long time and I can tell you exactly why: a thing you can be shown is a thing somebody can produce, and a thing somebody can produce in a room is a thing somebody can produce against you. So your offer is in a book that nobody is permitted to give you, and you are still in arrears, and I am sorry."

"I have never wanted anything out of it," said Reisz. "That is what I have not been able to get round for two days. I did not want it written down so that I would look like a man who pays. I wanted it written down so that if somebody ever came to this plate with a paper I could say *that is mine, I offered, you had it.*"

Nobody had anything to say to that, and the yard did what this yard does, which is to let a thing stand until somebody cannot stand it.

Then Reisz said the other half of it, and he said it slower, and this is the half that made the ninth month.

"I cannot pay it," he said. "That is what I have got to. Not because of the money. Look at it. Forty-three and some months, and at the end of it a house, and a discount. And on this plate what is owed is to this yard. If I go in there on the morning of the market with forty-three and some months and I pay it, the money goes into the yard's account against this plate, and the plate stops owing it — and the house at the end of it is not here, and the house is not paid, and the plate is still the plate, and next season it will be on there again with a different house at the end of it or the same one, and I will have paid it twice and been in arrears both times.

"Because you cannot pay your own debt," said Bartel Reisz. "You can only pay somebody else's. And this is my plate, and it is on the ward's roll because a shed is not a household, and if it were a household it would be on a household's roll and a household can be asked, and a shed cannot, and that is not an injustice anybody did to me, that is just what a shed is."

---

They counted it in the yard and they got it wrong twice, and then they stopped, because a yard does not do totals and that is on the yard's own rules from the twentieth of the rains month and it is the only one of the four that is about counting.

What the yard had, by the seventh bell on the sixth day of the ninth month, was this.

There was a lamp out in Rainmarket that had been lit every day since the sixth of the month before, and a bench that would stand cold for six months, and a rule in a seed house's own book that turned what did not come up into a charge on a household's ground, and no form anywhere for that, and a keeper who had said all of it out loud once and would not say it again.

And there was forty-three and some months on a plate on a shed door, at a discount, with a house at the end of it that nobody in Tallow Ward had ever heard of, and a man's written offer to pay it in a book that nobody may be shown.

Two things against this yard in one week, in a season when a yard had refused nine hundred and forty pounds in the seed month on the ground that it would not take on somebody else's rate, and had a refusal on a nail with a bit of string through it, and had said out loud in that yard that a yard's refusal binds the yard and has never in the history of this city bound a household.

Kern said the sentence from the step, because he is the one who says sentences from the step, and because there were about ninety people in that yard and it is his yard.

"So we have been charged twice in a week," he said. "I am going to say it out loud because it is the ninth month and everybody in this lane is standing in it. We have been charged twice and the two things are not the same size and one of them is not ours, and I am not going to be the man who stands on this step and works out which of the two we can afford, because that is how you get a yard to say yes with its mouth while its hands are doing something else.

"Four hundred and six people," said Aldis Kern. "And somebody in a building on a hill has got a third one on a piece of paper with our name on it, and that one has not come down the road yet, and it is bigger than the two of these, and I do not know what it is, and I have asked nobody, and I would like it minuted that I have asked nobody."

Nobody asked him anything, and the shutter went over the tank, and in a shed on the low-ground road a lamp stayed out, and on a plate on a shed door between the Gate and the north road forty-three and some months sat at a discount with a house at the end of it, and the man who had offered to pay it went home at the seventh bell and did not sleep much.
