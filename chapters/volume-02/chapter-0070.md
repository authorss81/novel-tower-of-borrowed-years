# Chapter 70 — A Date

Bel Harrow had come up the hill for the third time in five weeks and the room was still the second-floor hearing room, built for thirty, and there were nine in it, and four of the nine had been in that room on the sixteenth of the rains month, and one of the five who was not there that day had been brought to another room since and was in a cell forty miles up the Crown March road.

The Trust's seat was empty.

That was the first thing. Renata Vole had been reassigned to the coastal returns in the rains and the Trust had sent nobody, and a board of five with a hole in it is not four members and a Chair, it is a board that cannot do the one thing a board is for, which is be a party, and the Trust was a party, and every week the seat was empty was a week in which the office that had certified the whole of what the ninth month was going to be about was not in the room.

Harrow had written for it twice. He had been told twice, in the first person, that the seat would be filled when the office was ready, and the office's answer on the second occasion had contained the words *in the ordinary course*, and Harrow had put those two words in his own minute in quotation marks, because he serves the minutes himself and has no clerk and cannot get one, since no office will serve the record of a body it is a party to.

Sedge was there for the Chamber. Kern was there for the ward, in his own name, because a ward is not a party to a proceeding and cannot sit at a table at all, and the standing provision that lets a ward's captain sit is read out loud at every sitting, and Kern has been in this room five weeks. Vendl was there for the Benevolent Fund of the Counter-Clerks, in the good coat she wears to a Fund meeting and nowhere else.

At the second seat, which is not a membership, Neris Kade had a sheet, and the Chair had given it to her at the door, and the one function of that sheet is to hold four figures in the order in which they are called out.

Marek Vale was at the back, on his feet, with no coat, because he had come up the hill with the rest of them and a man with no office and no work has nowhere in Bell Stair to be that is not the back of a room.

---

"What I have been asked for," said Bel Harrow, "is a date."

He put a single sheet on the table. It was a draft, and it was initialled in the margin in a district hand, and it had a seal on it that was not the Crown March seal and not the Trust's and not the board's, and Marek could see from the back that it was a district's seal and that a district's name was at the top in the third line.

"I have never seen a draft in my life before this year," said Bel Harrow. "A representative's office is sent anything that concerns a district's account, and what this office has been sent since I arrived is a great deal of paper addressed to the wrong place. This one came because somebody in the water office's second floor was careful, and that person did not sign it and has not been asked who they are and I have not asked. I am telling the table that the letter exists, that it has not been served, and that I have had it in my hand for six days and have shown it to nobody until this morning, because a representative who shows a district's letter out of a room before it is served will not be asked for it a second time." He put his hand flat on it. "It will be served in the ninth month. It is a query. It uses the word *advise*. It names a district, and the district it names is this one, and the district is in the letter and not in this room, and I have been on this hill since the day before I saw the letter, which is six days, working out how to say that to four hundred people without making a promise I have no office to promise with, and I have decided that I will not say it, and I will be asked why, and my answer is going to be the sentence I am about to say."

He looked at the sheet and then at the four of them.

"I can call a meeting," said Bel Harrow. "That is my office. A Crown March representative can call a meeting, and can put a day on it, and can serve the minutes in his own hand, and can say a date out loud in a room, and there is not one other thing I am able to do. I cannot pay anybody out of it. I have tried to arrange a fund for this board twice in nineteen years of being a clerk of one kind or another and I have failed twice, and the Fund has proved out of its own books that it cannot pay a sitting, and a ward cannot be assessed a rate at all, and the Trust's share of the recorder's pay rose to cover it and the Captain objected and was outvoted three to one. So there is a table in this city where a district can be named, and there is no table in this city where a district can be paid, and those are not the same table, and the distance between them is the whole of what this board is."

Nobody said anything, because it was the truth, and the truth in that room had a habit of arriving without a vote.

"The question before the table is this. Is there a date on which the demand that is coming in the ninth month can be met, or refused, by somebody with an office? And I am asking it because I am the only man here who can ask it and be answered, and I want an answer in my hand, in a minute, with a day on it, that I can read out on a step in Tallow Ward on a morning when four hundred people have come to find out."

"There is not," said Aldis Kern.

"That is an answer."

"That is an answer," said Kern, "and it is not the only one I have come with, and I am going to give you the rest of it in the yard and not in here, because this room is where I am a member and the yard is where I am a man, and I have been in this room five weeks and I have watched it do three things, and the three things are all it." He put his hands flat on the table. "It recorded all four of our figures and chose none of them. It put a word *sworn* on a sheet that is not sworn, in my hand, and let me have it. And it sat, and its duty to write down what a member says in his own words has been used twice against the man who put it there, and I would not take it away, and I have thought about it every week since."

---

The business took an hour and a half and none of it was the thing anybody came for.

Harrow gave the board the next sitting, at the third bell on the fourth day of the growth month, which is a date, and which is the only kind of thing he is able to hand anybody, and he read it out twice.

The Trust's seat was minuted as empty, with the two words in quotation marks, and the Chair minuted that he had asked twice and been refused twice, and minuted that a board cannot proceed to a money matter with a party not in it, and minuted that he was not going to pretend that this was a legal opinion, because he had no office that would give him one.

A member dissented, and term four of the board's four terms is that a member who dissents may have the grounds written in the grounds' own words, in the finding, and the duty to write them is the board's and not the member's, and Vendl did it, and hers is four lines, and the four lines are that she has been asked twice to take money for a sitting and has declined twice and is in the room, and that the reason she is in the room is not a better offer and she would like that in the minute.

And then Neris Kade said the thing she had come for, from a seat that cannot vote, and the Chair minuted that she was not a member and had spoken as an examiner for the season works.

"I have been right about this market since the second of the month," she said, "in a lane, in a shed, at a bench and at a trestle, every time, and I want the minute to say the rest of it. Being right about a market is worth nothing, and it has been worth exactly nothing for twenty days. About two hundred people have been shown a number they could not read and there is still no register in this city they can be shown it in, and there is a shed in a lane on Rainmarket that is the best anybody has done about it, and it belongs to a man with a licence, and he did it in eight days and for nothing. I have not thanked him and I am not going to, because he is not a friend and I do not think he wants to be, and because a man who puts a free register in a lane in eight days has understood something about this city that I have not, and I have been doing this for six years."

"Examiner," said Harrow, "that is a dissent and you cannot vote, and I am going to write it as what it is."

"Then write it as what it is," said Neris Kade. "That is what it is."

---

And at the end Harrow asked the only question left in the room, which he had put there himself, at the beginning, in order that somebody would have to answer it late.

"Mr Vale."

Marek came the four steps up out of the back of the room, which was not a thing anybody in Bell Stair had ever seen a man with no office do, and stopped, and did not sit.

"You are in this room as a person with no office and no standing, which I have said out loud twice today and which I will now say a third time for the minute," said Harrow. "You are not on this table. You cannot enter anything. I want to ask you one question and I want the answer to be a sentence, and I am going to read your sentence out on a step in Tallow Ward in the ninth month, without your name, if you will let me, and I will tell you now that I am going to do that whether you will or not, because a Crown March representative is allowed one thing and this is it."

"Ask."

"Forty thousand nine hundred has stood unapplied against a district for three hundred years. A board named a district against it on the seventeenth of the rains, and a board cannot name a thing that is not a party to it, and I have found that out the hard way and in public. In the ninth month an office will ask that district to be advised, and the district will be advised, and the advice will be a rate." Harrow put his pen down. "Is there a date on which anybody in this city can be made to say what the charge is, before the charge is made?"

Marek thought about it for longer than a room of nine people expected, and he thought about it honestly, which is what he had been doing for five weeks and what had got him nothing.

"There is not," he said. "And I want to say two more things in the same breath and I would like the Chair to write both, because you have been very fair about writing things down for people who cannot get themselves read."

"Go on."

"There is a date in this city in eight days that nobody in this room controls," said Marek Vale, "and it is not a date in a room, and it is not going to be a minute, and it does not care what any of us write down. There is a frost coming in the last week of this month, and it is coming whether the second crop is sown or not, and after it there is no second crop this year for eleven households on the low ground who drew off a shed that was shut up wet in the flood year, and I have been told that by a woman who turns sixty-one trays twice a day and who is the only person in this basin who has ever told me a date she cannot be argued with.\"

"And the minute will have that in it," said Bel Harrow, "and I can put a day on the next sitting, and neither of those is a frost."

"No," said Marek. "And I want it minuted that a man with no office has told a Crown March representative that there is a date in this city he cannot reach, and has declined to say where he got it, and would rather it were said out loud on a step in Tallow Ward than written on a page in this room, and has not been able to arrange that and has not tried, because he has nothing in his hands that anybody in this building would believe."

Vendl put her pen down. "Mr Vale," she said, "that sentence you have just not said is the fourth one anybody has said at that desk in five weeks and none of the four were heard, and I would like it minuted that it was said and not heard, because in a year the only thing anybody will have is the minute."

"Nobody in this room will ask you," said Bel Harrow, and he wrote it.

Then he wrote the clock, because he was the only man at that table who had a clerk's habit and because it was the ninth month and it had to be in a record somewhere in his own hand, in five lines, and he read them out to the room before he signed them, which is the ordinary practice of an office that keeps its own record.

*The reserve carry of forty thousand nine hundred, named against a district in the rains of this year, is asked for in the ninth month of this year.*

*The water office's letter of that month uses the word advise and names a district which is not in this room and cannot be in this room.*

*In the same month a sum is due to this district from a private party, and the office that holds it is the same account, and no office in this city can show how it came to be due, and I have asked and been told that the question cannot be answered without naming a party who cannot be named.*

*The school is the last line of the standing order of that account and that order has no copy and it is entered by an officer whose name is on it.*

*This board has no fund, its Trust seat is empty, and no date was given.*

He signed it and served it himself, in his own hand, and it went on the nail on a public table at the door of a room built for thirty, and about nine people read it, and nobody said afterwards that they had read it, which is what people in this city have said about notices on doors since the fourth of last month.

Then the room emptied, and the four hundred people who had come up the hill to find out what was going to happen in the ninth month went back down the causeway in the rain without having been told anything, because there was nothing to tell them, and because the five lines on the nail at the door were the only sentence anybody in this city had put on paper about the ninth month before, and it had been written by a man with no office to write it with.

Kade went past him at the bottom of the stair and stopped, and did not say anything warm, and did not say anything about the yard or the trestle or the shed.

"You were right in the rains," she said, "and you were right in Rainmarket, and you have been right every day since, and it has not once been worth anything, and I am not going to pretend I have learned anything from you, and I am not going to pretend either that I would have got to the carrier's row as fast without you, and those two sentences are going to have to do each other for about eight months."

"I know," said Marek.

"I know you know." She went down the stair. "There is a man on a shed row at the Tallow Gate who asked to be written down in a book and there is nowhere in this city to put it, and you are the only person in Morrowfen who could have put it anywhere, and you have not been back to that row since the thirteenth, and I did not ask you why and I am telling you that I noticed."

Then she was gone, and Marek Vale stood in the bottom of a stair in Bell Stair at the fourth bell on the twenty-third day of the seed month with nothing in his hands at all, and a card in his coat with a figure on it, and a pencil line on the back of a list, and no office, no form, no room over a seed shop, and a ninth month that both of the two charges in this city were coming into, and not one single person in Morrowfen who could be made to say what either of them was, before either of them landed.
