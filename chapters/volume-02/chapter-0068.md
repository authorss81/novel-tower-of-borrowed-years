# Chapter 68 — The Second Line

Fisk told her at the second bell on the twenty-first, in the seed house yard, three days after she had said she would, with the bench book open on the pail, and there were five people in the yard and three of them had known since the ninth.

She read what the bench book says. Eleven marks on eleven ends, two sheds, four feet of water in the flood year, dried in the growth month, the year in her own hand. She read it in the words it is written in and she did not improve it, and she told the girl before she read it that she was going to be told something in the words of a book and that the book is not kind and that she was not going to be asked to say she was all right.

Wenna Drel did not cry and did not say she was all right.

She looked at the book for a while and then she said, "Whose name is on the tray."

"Yours is on the tray. Your mother's is on the tray, and yours is on it under hers, which is what your being on the roll means."

"Then whose ground is the rate on."

Fisk stopped with her hand flat on the book.

"That," she said, "is the best question anybody has asked in this yard in eleven years, and I am going to answer it exactly and then I am going to tell you that I do not like the answer."

---

The rate is on the ground, and the ground is in her mother's name, because a member of a cooperative holds ground in the Share's name as a household and a household is the member on the roll, and a woman who has been ill since the flood year and did not come to a meeting in a month is still a member, and there is no box on a rate on which a person is asked whether she is well.

The eleven trays go to sand. The Share's rule is a return in kind: eleven days of labour and a third of a kept-back stock, and where a member cannot bring in a third of anybody's the shortfall is entered as a rate against that member's ground and gathered in the ordinary way at the quarter.

So the answer is that the low ground will not be harmed by the girl, and the family will, and the family is a woman who is ill and a girl of sixteen and a lot that is sand, and the rate will be gathered at the quarter out of a household that has nothing to draw on and a member who cannot work, and the office will write the name at the top of it and it will be the same name that is on the girl's own line.

"That is the whole of it," said Mirren Fisk. "And there is no form in this city on which a person who is ill has a rate written about them in a different hand, and I am not going to invent one in a yard, and if anybody tells you on any other day that there is one, ask them for the number of it and watch their face."

---

The second line was in a house over a chandler's shop and it belonged to a woman of thirty-five, and the girl went there the next morning by herself and asked for it, and was given the same answer she had been given on the tenth of the month, in the same voice, at the same trestle.

"You keep it because I asked," said Wenna Drel.

"I keep it because I wrote it," said Ilse Ferrow. "You asked and I wrote it, and the reason I wrote it is that I had sent you away twice and the third time you came back and stood in the rain and asked a question out loud, and if nobody had written that down then the only record of it would have been in four people and one of them was a boy of fourteen who is paid a shilling out of a tin. That paper is worth exactly nothing. It is not evidence of anything. There is no box on any form in this city for *a girl of sixteen asked a question in a lane*, and I would have had to invent a box to make it count, and a woman who invents a box is a woman who can be asked later why she invented it, and I would rather have a piece of paper worth nothing than a form I have to defend."

"Then put my mother's illness on something."

"No."

"Ferrow—"

"The woman who keeps that seed house has told you about a bench book," said Ilse Ferrow, "and he is standing at the other end of this table and he is not going to help me and he is not going to help you, because the help you want is a line about your mother in a book, and any office that took it would be taking it from a woman in front of her, and your mother is not in this lane and would not know that it had been taken, and I will not do that to her and neither will you and neither will he."

---

Marek had come down the causeway at the first bell and had been at the far end of the trestle for about a quarter of an hour, and it was Neris Kade who came up the low-ground road at about the second, with her slate under her arm, and who found the two of them there, and who said the thing she came to say without any preamble at all.

"I want that paper entered somewhere."

"By whom," said Marek.

"That is not the question I asked and you know it is not the question I asked." Kade put the slate down on the trestle. "I have been on this road for six years. I have watched about eleven things get said out loud once, in a lane or a yard or a kitchen, and get no further, because a fact that lives in one woman's coat in one house over a chandler's shop is a fact that dies with the house, or with the woman, or with the week she stops working. That paper is not evidence. It is also the only object in this basin that says a girl of sixteen was in a lane three times and was sent away twice. If the house closes, it is gone. And then in a year somebody is going to stand up in a room and say a girl of sixteen was asked about her mother's ground, and everybody in that room is going to agree, and the one piece of paper that says she was in the lane and came back three times is going to be in a drawer in a house that a man with a licence has bought."

"It cannot be entered anywhere," said Marek. "That is the point of it. If it goes into a book with a form on it, it becomes a form about a girl of sixteen that a person of sixteen did not give, in a month when a cooperative is putting a rate on her mother's ground, and in six weeks there will be a lawyer in a good coat saying that the girl was asked and answered and understood a paper that was not hers. Every one of those words would be true and the whole of them would be a trap, and I have watched it happen to a village and I have read what was left of it."

"It has happened to a village because nobody wrote anything down," said Neris Kade. "And it has happened to a second village because somebody wrote down a thing that was not quite what they had seen. You are proposing the third thing, which is nothing, and calling it a principle."

"I am proposing that she be asked, in her own words, and that the answer go on a bench book in a shed, where anybody can lift it off a shelf, and that it be her hand and not yours and not mine."

"That is a bench book."

"Yes."

"That is a bench book and I have never once in six years seen a bench book survive a bad autumn, and this is a bad autumn, and the woman who keeps it is the only person in this basin who has put a cost in a box on purpose and written the date it changed in the back of her own book." Kade picked her slate up again. "And I am not going to win this with you and you are not going to win it with me, and I would like it recorded that we both said the true thing and one of us was braver and it was not the one who was right."

They did not settle it. Wenna Drel was standing at the end of the trestle with her hood up and had been listening to both of them for about a quarter of an hour, and she had the look of a person who has decided that two adults are arguing about a box on a form she has never been shown.

---

She asked Marek about the roll later the same day, at the seed house door on the way out of the low ground, and she asked him in the way she asked everything, which was directly, at the front, before anybody had offered her anything.

"You read rates," she said. "You said so to a woman at a counter and she said you could not have a copy. So can you read me the line my mother's on."

"It is on a roll in Rainmarket and I can read it standing at a counter and I cannot take a copy away and I cannot enter it anywhere and I cannot write it out for you in my own hand, because a man with no office who writes a figure down on a piece of paper has a piece of paper."

"Read it to me."

He read it. He had it by heart, which he did not tell her, and it took under a minute, and it was a figure for a quarter and a component in the same line that was not water and had no box of its own, and when he had finished she said:

"Which box am I in."

"You are not in one. There is no box on a rate for a person. There is a ground, and a member's name against the ground, and a figure at the quarter."

"And whose name is against the ground."

"Hers. Yours is on a tray in this shed and hers is on a book at Rainmarket, and the rate will be asked of the book and not the tray, and the tray is how we know it and the book is how it is taken." He put his thumb on the edge of the bench. "And there is nothing anywhere for the fourth thing, which is a person in that house who is ill, and there is no column for it, and if a clerk were to try to make one he would be inventing a form about a household, and a woman who invents a form is a woman who can be asked later why she invented it."

"So it is the illness they cannot write down and the rate they can."

"Yes. And the rate is real and the illness is real and only one of them has a place to be written in."

Wenna Drel stood in the wet road on the low ground and worked the rest of it out.

"So my name is not on the paper the woman in the good coat read out in the lane," she said at last, "and my name is on the tray, and my mother's name is on the roll, and the rate will be asked of my mother's name, and my mother is ill, and there is nowhere on any of it to say that she is, and the only form in this city that has my name anywhere on it is a bench book in a shed, and nobody has to look at it."

"That is exactly right," said Marek. "And it is not the worst of it and it is not the best of it, and the thing you cannot do about it is write a letter, because a letter to whom. There is no office to complain to about a counterweight. There is a ward in this city that has been told it is a district and cannot say so, because it is not a person, and a man in Crown March has spent a fortnight on a form that has a fifth box on the bottom of it, and the fifth box is the oldest thing on the form and it is the box a district's returnable sum goes in, and it is not a box anybody can be asked about either, and that is why it has had a hand in it for longer than the eight years anybody has been using it."

"You have got something written on the back of that list," said Wenna Drel, "and you keep touching the place where it is, about once a minute, and you did it at the tank and you did it at the door."

He had. He had no idea he had been doing it.

"Since the ninth," he said. "It is not a document and there is no box in this city for a piece of paper with one sentence on it and a man's hand at the bottom, and I have been carrying it in a coat for thirteen days and I cannot give it to anybody and I cannot enter it and it will not be in any room I ever go into." He put his hand flat on the inside pocket where the little book of promises to his mother was, and where a card with a figure on it was, and where the folded list was.

"Then somebody should be given one," said Wenna Drel.

"You are sixteen and you have filled forty-one cones and you have not voted once in your life, and I have known you since the third of this month," said Marek, "and I am going to tell you that the last sentence you have just said is the whole of what I have been unable to do for five weeks, and I am not going to pretend to you that a girl of sixteen in a wet road is going to be able to do it, and I am not going to pretend to you that anybody in this city will do it this year. Somebody is going to have to be given one. I have written it down in a place where nobody can lift it, and I do not know what that place is, and I have stopped pretending I do."

She counted nine cones and a half of the second sowing out of a twist of paper in her coat and set it on the edge of the bench in front of him, and it was the last of a lot that was sand, and she did it because there were nine cones and a half and he was in the yard.

Then she went off up the low-ground road with a slate under her arm, because there were sixty-one trays to be turned that evening and a woman of thirty-four was doing them and had been doing them since before light.
