# Chapter 65 — Eleven of Sixty-One

The bench is the length of the north wall of the Elmsworth seed house and it has to be turned twice a day by a person who knows the sixty-one trays by the marks on the ends of them, and the marks are not numbers, they are the ends of things cut off a member's own tin and pressed in wet and left to go hard, so that a tray can be recognised in eight years by a woman who is not the same woman.

Mirren Fisk had got to the far end of it at the first bell on the eighteenth, and she had been there since before light, and there were two marks on the marks.

"Eleven," she said, before Marek had got the door shut behind him.

---

"Two more since the ninth."

"Twenty and thirty-nine," said Fisk. "Same stock. Same shed. Same four feet of water in the flood year, and the same decision to dry it in the growth month instead of the seed month, and I have written the year on the board and I have not written a reason on it because I do not have one that is better than the weather's."

"Do you have a reason at all."

"I have a shed," said Fisk. "A seed does not fail because of a schedule. It fails because of a thing about where it was kept, and the thing about where it was kept in this case is a shed that was under water and then was shut, and every one of the nine on the ninth is out of that shed and there are two more out of it and there may be four more out of it in a fortnight, and I would like it noticed that I have said the same sentence three times in four days and that the weather has not moved at all."

She put her hands down the sides of the tray and turned it the way a person turns a tray, from the back and not from the wrists.

"And I am going to say the other thing now, and then I am not going to say it again all season, and I want it in whatever you are going to write down, which I am told you cannot write down." She did not look at him. "Nine trays on the ninth, and two this week, and one of the nine was a shower that went over the ridge and missed us, and I do not know what caused the eleven and neither does anybody else, and a tray of seed does not fail because a shower went over a ridge four miles off. Whoever puts those two things together in a room in the ninth month will be the first person to put them together, and a person who puts them together once will have put them together twice by the harvest."

"Nobody is going to hear it from me."

"No," said Fisk. "You are going to hear it from me and then you are going to carry it, and carrying it is not the same as being able to do anything with it, and I have watched about nine people carry things in this basin and none of them has ever yet put one down."

---

Then she said the thing about the room.

"There is a room over the shop on the far side of this road," she said, "and the woman who had it has gone to her sister at the coast and gone for good, and the room is four a month and there is a table in it and a lock on the door and a window that looks into the seed house yard, and the Share will let it, and I have been thinking about it since the ninth and I have decided, and I am telling you at the top of the day because if I tell you at the bottom of it you will have had a good eight hours of thinking about it."

"Why me."

"Because on the third of this month you stood by my door for four hours and asked me one question, and the question was whether anybody here had ever read the whole of the instrument, and I said no, and you said *you are the first this year*, and then you sat down and you did not tell me what was in it, and I have kept this house for eleven years and nobody has asked me that question in eleven years." She turned the next tray. "That is the whole of the reason, and I want it said that it is not a good enough reason for a room, and I have thought about it for nine days and I could not find a better one. It is not because you are useful. A person who is useful in a yard is a different person from a person who is useful to a house, and this house has been watching the difference since the fifth of the rains month, and I would not let you have the roof on the strength of a lane."

"Thank you," said Marek.

"Do not thank me yet," said Fisk. "It is from the first of next month and there are four days of it left and I have told you no in the yard and I do not know yet which of the two of us was talking about this room."

---

The season was the other half of the day and the season has a date in it that nobody in the shed could move.

The Share's own rule for a member whose lot fails is a return in kind. Eleven days of labour, the same eleven the instrument is pledged against, and a third of the kept-back seed of another member, brought in before the sowing of the second crop; and if a member cannot bring in a third of anybody's kept-back, the standing is that the shortfall is entered as a rate against the member's ground and gathered in the ordinary way at the quarter.

It is in the minute book. It has been in the minute book since the year the Share was founded and it was carried on a count of hands and nobody has ever voted on it, and there are four boxes on the settlement desk's form for an entry of that kind and the fourth one is the right one, and that form was cut before the bad spring.

"Eleven households," said Marek. "Eleven days each. And a third of somebody's kept-back for the ones who have none of their own."

"Yes."

"And the ones with none of their own get a rate on their ground, in the ordinary way, at the quarter."

"Yes," said Mirren Fisk. "Which is the same rate as the water and the levy on the water, and it goes in one sum, and in the ninth month of this year that sum is the fourth line on a page with three other lines on it that the Share did not put there."

"Who is carrying a third of theirs this week."

Fisk stopped turning the tray.

"Ansgar Drel," she said. "And he has not signed a piece of paper in this business since the bad spring, and he did not take the paper, and he is the only household in this Share of sixty-one whose ground came up, and he has the oldest stock in the basin in a sack against the north wall, and he is seventy-eight years old, and he has just been asked by a woman of thirty-four whether he will bring in a third of it for eleven households, and he has said that he will bring in a third of it, and he asked me one question when he said it."

"What question."

"He asked whether the rule is the same if it is his lot that fails," said Fisk. "And I said yes, because it is, and he said *then it is all right*, and he meant it, and he went out to the cart. I would like somebody to be here next time it happens and I do not know who to ask."

---

Nobody in the shed had said the arithmetic out loud, and Marek did it, standing at the end of the bench with his hands behind his back, because it is a thing he could do and because there was nobody else in the room who could.

"Eleven households, and none of them has a harvest this year, and all eleven are pledged under one seal to the same instrument, and the settlement on that instrument is out of the harvest. A settlement out of a harvest from ground that did not come up is a shortfall, and a shortfall is not a punishment, it is a rule, and the rule looks for the borrower first, then a surety, and there is no surety for a member's own lot, because a member cannot be their own surety on a cooperative's own instrument. So it goes past the Share and lands on the office that certified it."

"We did not fail the trays," said Fisk.

"You did not fail the trays," said Marek. "The office sealed that instrument in the bad autumn, and its second clerk's eye has been on the paper this month and off it again, and what I am telling you is that a rule which collects the cost of eleven bad trays out of eleven households and then out of a district is the same rule that has been walking a sum from a shed on a carrier's row into this ward's account, and I am not saying they are the same rule. I am saying I can draw them with one pencil and I would rather somebody in this shed heard me draw it while I was here than had it drawn for them by a man in a good coat in the ninth month."

Mirren Fisk took her hands out of the sand and wiped them on her apron, and stood looking down the bench at fifty trays that had come up and eleven that had not.

"There is a frost coming in the last week of this month," she said, "and it is coming whether the second crop is sown or not, and after that there is no second crop this year for anybody, and the eleven are not a household and they are not a fraction and they are not a rate, they are eleven tins of sand with a mark on the end of them, and I am going to write eleven in the bench book and I am not going to write eleven names anywhere that anybody can lift off a shelf, because a bench book is in this shed and a list of eleven names is a list of eleven names in a city where somebody is buying lists this month."

"Write eleven," said Marek. "And the two marks, and the shed, and the year. And nothing else."

"That is what a bench book is."

He stayed until the second turning and helped carry the pail, which Fisk permitted, and which was the last piece of work the Share was ever going to let him do in that building, and neither of them knew that either.

---

The girl was at the far end of the bench with her sleeves pushed up, filling paper cones, which is a whole morning's work and which is not a vote and has never been treated as one by anybody except by her.

Wenna Drel had drawn her own seed in the spring off the Ansgar stock, the oldest line in the Share, and she had filled forty-one cones in one morning in front of a clerk from Bell Stair, and she was filling cones again now, and she had asked Marek on the tenth of the month whether her name was on the paper, and had been told the truth about it in a lane, and had signed on the line where her mother signs.

One of the eleven was hers, by the mark on the end of it, and one of the other ten is a household of four at the far stile that nobody has said out loud. She did not know that. Neither of them knew it, and Marek worked it out standing at the end of the bench with his hands behind his back about nine minutes after he had worked out the shortfall, and the working-out of it took him rather longer than the arithmetic did, and he understood perfectly well why, and it was the first time in five weeks that he had wanted a thing in this world to be a different shape than it was and had to do nothing at all about it.

"She is not to be told at the bench," said Mirren Fisk, about half a minute before he had decided not to say anything, in a voice that did not rise. "Not today. Today she is filling cones and there are twenty-six of us in this shed and four of us know which tray it is. Go on."

"She is filling cones for a Share that has just put a rate on her ground for seed that did not come up."

"She is filling cones for a Share that is going to owe her a third of Ansgar Drel's sack by the end of the month, which is better than a rate, and she does not know either of those things, and she is sixteen." Fisk turned the tray and did not look at the far end of the bench. "I will tell her in three days in the yard with her mother not there, which is a cruel way to do it and is the only way available, and I will do it in the words the bench book uses, and I will not improve them. And when you have finished being the man who was in the room, you and I are going to have a short conversation about the fact that you worked it out and said nothing at all, and about the fact that I noticed you working it out, and I am not going to let it go the way the yard lets things go, because a yard is not allowed to be the only thing in this that is careful."
