# Chapter 85 — A Mark in a Calendar

Halvard Sunn kept two rooms over a sailmaker's shop on the Bell Weir road with a lease that was charged in Crown March money and a counterseal press that had belonged to a man of his father's who had never in his life been to Morrowfen, and he was forty-four and had been four years in this city and had a standing at home that consisted entirely of the fact that he was the only notary of the coast who could be shown to have spent four years somewhere nobody was watching him.

He was at his table on the seventeenth day of the harvest month at the second bell when Marek Vale came up the outside stair, and he did not get up, and he said:

"You have brought me something to refuse and I have known that since the sixth of the month, so sit down and let us not waste the morning on it."

"You have known that since the sixth?"

"I have known it since the fourth," said Sunn. "That is the day a man from Bell Stair with no office sent a boy to ask me a question about a mark. I did not answer it and the boy took it back and I have been waiting nine days to be asked it properly by somebody who has come up a stair instead of sending one."

---

"There is a register," said Marek, "and it is not a year-spire instrument and it is not anybody's instrument, and it is going to be read in a room by a man with no office for nothing, and one of the lines in it is going to be a grain factor's whole book. And in about a month that factor is going to be asked by a house on the coast whether his paper can be read there. And I have come to find out whether it can, and I have come to you because you are the only person in this basin anybody has ever said that about."

"The answer is no," said Halvard Sunn, "and the answer costs me about four pounds a month, and I am going to tell you the cost because you will not be able to see it from a stair.

"Understand what the counterseal is, because nobody in this city understands it and everybody in this city is frightened of it in the wrong place. A mark in the Tide Reckoning names four things. A month, in a calendar that is not yours. A tide phase, which is a thing you can only know if you have been on that water. A harbor seal, which says which harbor's idea of the year it is. And a translator, which is a man who has put his name to the four and will answer for them to a person he has never met.

"That is all it is. It is not a warranty about a figure. It does not say that the sum is right. It says the paper comes from a calendar somebody will recognise, and a house that will not buy Morrowfen paper will buy that, because otherwise there is no way to know in four hundred miles when the thing happens.

"Now put one on a register that has no month in it, and you have said that a piece of paper in a shed in Rainmarket is in a calendar, and it is not. A register is not a thing that happens on a day. It is a list that goes on adding to itself for as long as anybody in a basin keeps it up, and a tide phase in the middle of that is a sentence that cannot be true. And I will not stand behind the sums in it either, because they are not mine and I have read them from the end and they are somebody else's trade, and a notarial office and four people in a shed are two different trades and have been since before either of us had a father."

"So the register cannot be read outside this basin," said Marek.

"It cannot be read in the next street either," said Sunn, "and that is what I would like you to go and tell them, and I have watched you work it out in the time it has taken me to say it, so I am not going to insult you by saying it slower.

"You are building a thing that cannot leave the room it is in. And that is correct, and I am not going to pretend otherwise, and I want it recorded that the man who could have made it travelable has told you in his own rooms that he will not, in the fourth and last year of a four-year post, in a city where there is exactly one of him."

---

He said the rest of the cost about four minutes later, with a cup in his hand, in a way that was not made for anybody.

"There is a house at home that thinks I am useful," he said. "I have not been there in seven months and I write to it every month and in every letter I describe the office I do not have, because the point of a notary in a port is that he stands between two calendars and I have four years of that and it is the only thing anybody out there has ever paid me for.

"And in about a month a man from that house will write and ask me whether Morrowfen paper is being read anywhere outside Morrowfen, because a Morrowfen factor has put a book in a room and a Morrowfen factor sells outside Morrowfen. And I will have to write back and say that I do not know. And a notary who writes *I do not know* to a house that has been paying him for four years is a notary they will not need in a year, and I have two rooms on a lease and a press that is not mine, and there is no second office in this city and I am the one that is here."

He put the cup down.

"So I have refused your register and I have told you the reason for the refusal in four sentences, and the reason is a trade and not a principle, and I would like it noted that I did not enjoy it, and I am not going to be grateful to you for understanding it in nine seconds, because being understood is not a consolation and I have watched one person be understood in this city this summer and it did him no good whatsoever."

"I am not going to put that in a book," said Marek.

"I know," said Halvard Sunn. "That is the second time this month that has been the right answer in this room and I would like a third one eventually, and there is a chair by the wall and you may sit in it, and the morning is not over because I have said no."

---

The stairs went up outside the wall and the stair was the outside one because the door was shut, and Marek heard a man on it before he saw him, because a drayman on stairs makes a noise like a chair being moved.

Petar Duss came in with a rolled sheet under his arm and a rope round his wrist, and he had been in this room once before, in the growth month, in the rain, and had had his bed and his gear taken off him at the sixth bell under the fifth box of a factor's pledge form, and had been given the gear back at the same bell, four hours later than a man on that road would have expected, and had never asked about the four bells and had not been asked about them.

He had come about a different thing entirely.

"Sunn. I have got a haulier's paper off the terraces and the man on the coast will not look at it because there is no mark on it, and I have got no money for a mark, and somebody told me you would do it for a small thing."

Sunn took the sheet and unrolled it on the table, and it was a Red Uplands grain contract of a kind that a hundred men in this basin carry every season, and there was nothing on it that had ever come near the Elmsworth seed house, the low ground, the flood quarters, the fourth clause, or the ninth month.

He read the whole of it, and then he took the press across, and the wax, and he did the thing in the bottom corner that a notary does, and Marek Vale was standing four feet away with his hands behind his back and watched the hand.

The hand was the same hand. Not the same way of signing — the shape of the letters, the way the downstroke of the last letter of the month went a little long and turned up, the way the translator's name was set with a space before it and none after. He had seen it twice on a board over a door in a shed on the low-ground road, and once on a bundle of nine sheets and a schedule of fifty-two lines that had been the reason a man had been beaten over an instrument with two hands.

Three instruments. Two of them were the paper, and one of them was a haulier's contract for barley out of the second terrace, and there is nothing whatever in this basin that connects a grain factor's margin to a cooperative's fourth clause except that one of the two was a district.

Marek Vale did not write it down.

He had not written it down the first time either, on the twenty-second of the seed month, standing in the rain on the wrong side of a lane with a good reason and a true one, and he had not told anybody, and it had cost him the whole of that evening and it had been the correct thing to do, because there are two people in this city who would have carried it into a room inside the hour and neither of them would have been able to say which of the three it was about.

Sunn put the sheet down and blew on the wax and gave it back.

"That is four shillings and it is not worth it and you should not have come up here for it," he said. "And it is correct, and it will be honoured at the harbour, and I have put my name to four things and three of them are a calendar and the fourth is me, and I have not checked the sum because I do not check sums and you should not have brought me one to check."

"Will it be read there," said Duss.

"It will be read there as a mark in a calendar by a person who has never been to Morrowfen and has never heard of a place called Rainmarket," said Halvard Sunn, "and that person will know the month and the tide and the harbour, and the sum will be nothing at all to him, and he will not care, and that is the best a mark has ever done for anybody and I have never been able to explain it to a man from a terrace."

Duss went down the outside stairs, and the room was quiet for about a minute and a half.

Then Sunn said, without any preamble and in a completely different voice:

"You have been looking at my hand."

Marek Vale did not answer for slightly too long.

"I have."

"Three times, and you have said nothing, and you have written nothing, and I noticed you not writing it, and I want to say one thing about that and then I am going to ask you a question and I would rather you answered it than that you said anything about the hand.

"A notary is paid to be the man whose mark can be looked up. There is a list at a harbour, and it is a long list, and my name is on it, and on that list there is nothing whatever about what my hand did on the seventeenth of the harvest month. If anybody ever came up these stairs and said to you *the notary's hand is on a bundle and on a board and on a haulier's paper and there is one hand in all three*, you would have to hand them that list, and the list would say *yes*, and that is the whole of what it would say, and I would be standing in a room in a port with a mark in a calendar on three instruments that have nothing in the world to do with each other and no way at all to explain it.

"And I do not know what the marks are," said Halvard Sunn. "I want that said in this room, today, by me, before either of us asks the other one. I have not known for nine years. There are two answers anybody in this city will give you and one of them is *I do not know* and the other one is *you are not the sort of person that page is for*, and I have had nine years of both and I have stopped asking, and the reason I have stopped asking is that I make a very good living out of a mark nobody in this basin can check, and if I start asking about marks I have to stop making it."

"Then I will not ask," said Marek Vale.

"I know that too," said Sunn, "and I have not thanked you for it and I am not going to, and I would like you to understand that a man who is not thanked is not a man who is owed anything. Put your coat on. It is a cold stair and I am forty-four and I have been on a lease for four years."
