# Chapter 69 — The Best Thing in the Lane

The shed was taken on the fifteenth, on a week's notice, from a man who sells pitch and has been losing money on it for four years, and it is eleven feet by nine and it is forty feet down the lane from the trestle, and on the twenty-second, from the fourth bell, it had a painted board over the door with a counterseal on it and a woman sitting in it.

The board said, in the ordinary painted caps of an office that means it:

*A REGISTER OF ASSIGNED CLAIMS. OPEN FOUR HOURS A DAY. NO CHARGE. ANY PERSON MAY ASK.*

Signe Alve was twenty-nine and had been out of the seed business since the bad spring and had been hired on the fifteenth for four a week, and she had a licence of no kind, and she was the best thing in the lane by a distance that was not a matter of opinion.

The second part is the part that made about forty people stop in the rain, and it is this. On a bench along the wall there were fifty-two lines, and each line had a date and a rate period and a sum and a discount and the name of the party it had been handed to, and beside those, in a second hand, was what the household had said.

It had taken eight days and it had cost the house a clerk's time and a copy of every assignment in the file, and the fifty-two households had been asked, one at a time, in words, what they thought they had signed, and the answer had been written down as it was given and improved on by nobody. Thirty-eight had answered. Fourteen had not, and the fourteen are entered as *asked, and no answer given, and asked once*, which is the ward's own rule and was not the house's.

"Thirty-eight," said Ilse Ferrow, standing at the end of the bench with her arms folded. "They will not like the fourteen. Everybody wants to be on the good side of a list."

"They will like the fourteen better than they like being on the list at all," said Ovid Merrow.

---

He came down at the fifth bell, in a good coat, in the rain, and stood outside the door of his own shed with his hands behind his back for about four minutes before he went in, and Marek Vale was on the other side of the lane and watched him do it.

What Merrow had built was not a register of risk. It was a register of what people had actually said when they were asked what they had signed, which is a different and older and much better thing, and it was free, and it was open four hours a day, and it answered in an afternoon a question the district water office had declined to answer on the nineteenth by letter and would have required a season for.

And the fourth clause was in it.

Not in the office's words. In the words of the low ground. *The district named in the schedule bears the failure of the season favoured*, and then, against a line for each of the fifty-two, what that household had said when a woman from the house had said it to them in words at their own door, and eleven of the fifty-two had used the word *district* and understood it, and four of them had said *the year that isn't ours* and used it as though it were the same thing.

"They cannot all have been told that by us in eight days," said Marek.

"They were not," said Merrow. "I have read fifty-two households' worth of what they think they know about water, and about the flood quarters, and about the district, and about what a rate is, and it is my considered opinion that this city has been explaining things to itself in kitchens for two hundred years and has never once had to write any of it down." He looked at the bench. "I did not teach them the words. I asked a question and wrote down what came out. That is the whole of my trade and I have been doing it for nine years for money and I have been doing it since the fifteenth for nothing, and you may put both of those in whichever book you are keeping."

---

The column with nothing at the head of it was on the end of the bench, and it was a hand's width wide, and there was nothing at the top of it, and on the twenty-second, in front of about a hundred and forty people, Ovid Merrow explained why, and nobody in the lane had heard a yard say it and nobody in the lane said where he had got it.

"I had a board painter and a signwriter here on the twentieth," he said. "They were in this room for four hours between them and they put a head on six of these seven and they were going to put a head on the seventh and I sent them home. There is a reason and it is not modesty. A heading is the one thing on a sheet that cannot be put in afterwards by anybody who was not there on the day, and a column that has been given a heading cannot be given a better one, and a column with a bad heading in it is worse than a column with no heading in it, because a bad heading is a thing people can be told they agreed to." He put his thumb on the bare wood. "I do not know what that column is for. I have had it for eight days. I intend to have it for longer than eight days, and I intend to find out what it is for from a person who is not me, and there is not one in this lane this evening, and I would rather have an empty column for a month than a wrong word on it for a year."

A rope-maker of thirty-nine with a string round her wrist stood at the back of that queue for about a minute and a half, and did not say a word to anybody, and went home up the causeway, and told four people on the way that a man in a good coat over a chandler's shop had said the exact sentence the yard says at the tank when it has to write one down, and that the yard had never heard where he got it, and that she was not going to be the one to tell him.

---

Halvard Sunn came at the sixth bell and stood in the doorway for a while, because he is forty-four and a Saltglass harbor notary resident in Morrowfen and he does not hurry into a room where a document is displayed.

He put a counterseal on the register's board, and he would not put one on the book, and he said why in one sentence, and the sentence was that a mark in the Tide Reckoning is a mark in a calendar and not a warranty about a figure, and he was not going to stand behind a set of numbers written by four clerks in a shed in Rainmarket because his office is an office of notaries and the two things are not the same trade and never have been.

"That is a hundred and forty people outside hearing you say you will not stand behind it," said Merrow, pleasantly.

"It is forty and they are wet," said Halvard Sunn.

And Marek Vale, on the edge of the lane with his hands in his coat, was looking at the board over the door and had gone very still, because there were two marks on it in the same hand, and one of them had been on a bundle of nine sheets and a schedule of fifty-two lines for a fortnight, and Sunn was talking to Merrow about tides and had not looked at the board once.

Nobody in Morrowfen can check a Tide Reckoning mark. There is no officer on this hill who may read one or say whether one is what it claims to be, and a man with no office and no ledger and no way of making a comparison has not got a claim, he has got an impression, and an impression is the one thing in this basin that he has been keeping out of rooms for five weeks.

He wrote nothing down and he told nobody and he went back up the causeway at the seventh bell, and it is on the record that he did not tell anybody, because there are two people who would have carried it to a room within the hour and he is not one of the people in this city who can be got to do that on a wet evening.

---

And on the twenty-second, at the seventh bell, about two hundred people had been through the shed, and about sixty of them had been through it twice, and the Tallow Gate entry was on the third bench on the right-hand wall and had been read by eleven people who had not been through the door twice, and it said a licensed carrier and a sum of forty-three and some months and a date and a party, and it said that the carrier had been asked, and had not answered.

It said nothing about the low ground and nothing about the Share and nothing about a fourth clause and nothing about where any of it had come from, and two hundred people in a lane in the rain had stood in front of a piece of painted board and had not been able to find a single other link on it, and that was not an accident and it was not a favour and it was the whole of what a lender is for.

Then Ovid Merrow said the last thing of the day, to about a hundred people, from the step of his own shed, and he said it as though he were talking about the price of seed.

"The best thing that has happened in this lane this month did not happen in this shed," he said. "Four hundred people at a tank in Tallow Ward were offered nine hundred and forty pounds' worth of dead district's rate to be taken on by a ward that cannot be asked whether it minds, and they said no, in a yard, in their own words, on a sheet with two of their own hands on it, and they put it on a nail where anybody can lift it off. That sheet is worth more to me than the nine hundred, and I did not get the nine hundred, and I went away, and I have been in a good mood for five days, and I would like somebody to tell me why, because I have been trying all day and I have not managed it."

He got no answer from the lane, and the lane went home, and about nine people were still standing at the board at the last of the light reading a column with nothing at the head of it.

"I will tell you why," said Ovid Merrow, to the nine, and to the shed. "Because that sheet has no holder. Every claim I have ever bought has had a person standing behind it and I could not buy the page in this city's case because there is nobody behind the line. That sheet has got a yard behind it and a yard is not a person, and there is no form on which a yard can be a party, and so for the first time in nine years there is a document in this basin that nobody in it can be shown to be the seller of, and I have been trying for nine years to get my hands on a thing like that, and I have failed every time, and it is hanging on a nail in Tallow Ward with the rain coming through the roof of that shed."

Then he went inside and shut the door, because the register opens again tomorrow at the fourth bell and there is a queue on the lane already, and he had worked out in eight days, sitting in a shed with a piece of chalk, what an empty column head is worth, and it was worth more than the shed, and he had said so to nobody at all.
