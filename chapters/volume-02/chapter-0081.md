# Chapter 81 — Every Office in the City

On the sixth day of the harvest month Marek Vale stood at the Gate tank in Tallow Ward at the first bell with a flat board under his arm and about a hundred and forty people in the yard, which was fewer than had come for the rounds and more than had come for anything else, and he had not called it and could not have, because a man with no office cannot call a meeting in a ward.

Behind him on the wall were three boards in forty hands and a nail with nineteen working sheets on it and their strings, and on the top of that nail, since the fourth bell on the twentieth day of the seed month, the yard's own refusal in the yard's own words, read out twice by a boy of nineteen who had not been asked a question by anybody until the fifth month of the year.

He put the board down on the flat top of the tank, because his arms had gone, and because a thing under a man's arm all morning is a thing he is not going to put on a table, and because there was nowhere else in Tallow Ward for a man with no office to put a thing down.

"There is a thing I have been carrying since the fourth of this month," he said, "and I would like to put it in a room. I am going to say it in a yard first, because I would like it to be refused in a yard before it is refused anywhere else."

Nobody said anything, which in that yard at that hour was a thing people did.

---

"A register of risk. A room, with hours on it, and anybody may come and ask whether their own line is in it, and nothing is charged, and nobody is asked to sign anything to come in.

"And one rule. A bundle may not be entered as one line."

He let it sit, and then said the rest of it, because a rule with nothing under it is the exact thing this yard had spent a month refusing to publish.

"It goes in as its parts. Every part on its own line, with the sum, the date, the discount and the party it went to. And the counterweight named to the people who will bear it, and the number of people who will bear it, in the words those people used. And if a counterweight is named as a district then the district is not a person and there is nobody to name it to, and the line does not go in at all."

Sanne Droll, who was thirty-nine and made rope and had held a sheet in the first round and kept it, said from the middle of the yard:

"And so the paper is not in it."

"No."

"That is the whole of why you want it."

"It is one of the reasons. It is also the whole of why it will not work, and I would like to say that before anybody else does."

Nobody thanked him for that either.

---

"You have understood what it is," he said. "A room in which a person comes and finds out what they are standing in. Not a room that says a person ought not to be standing in it. A room that says, here is your line, and here are the words you used, and here is who holds the other end. The one thing it will not do is tell anybody they were wrong, because there is nothing in this basin that can do that, and a thing that says so in a room gets believed."

"Then what is it for," said a man at the back.

Marek Vale found that he had prepared for four offices and a yard and not for this.

"For the next person," he said. "There is a chain nine links long in this basin, and two people know it, and neither of them can say it in a room. A chain is a thing that is invisible from both ends and obvious in the middle, and the middle is a room, and the room is the whole of the instrument."

There was a long stretch of that particular quiet that a hundred and forty people can make. Then Aldis Kern said, from the step of the tank, in nine seconds, no.

---

"We gave that yard a way of saying no," he said. "We wrote it on our own sheet in our own words and put it on a nail where anybody can lift it off, and it is the only piece of paper in this basin that nobody in it can be shown to be the seller of, and a man in a good coat has told us in public that he has been trying nine years to get hold of a thing like it.

"And now you want to take it off the nail and put it in a building."

"I want to copy it. It stays on your nail."

"You want to copy it and hand the copy to a room in a building with a counter and hours on it, and in the ninth month there will be a man in a good coat up this road with a piece of paper, and he will say *I would like the yard's own method applied to my instrument, please*, and there will be nothing to refuse him with, because the yard's own method will be in town with a person to ask, and we will have handed it over in the month we needed it."

He put his hand flat on the tank, about a foot from the board.

"That is not a reason about principle. I have got no principle about it. It is a reason about the shape of the thing, and I would like it written down in somebody's book, and I am not writing it in mine, because then it is a rule, and a rule is the third box."

The yard laughed, and it was not a good laugh, and it went off before Kern had finished, which told everybody present exactly how much of that sentence the yard had already thought.

---

The water office at Rainmarket keeps its counter on the ground floor with the door open and a bench outside for people waiting to be told something they did not want, and on the seventh day, at the second bell, a woman of forty-four named Berta Lund read Marek's request twice and gave him the same answer in the same voice both times.

"You may read your own rate at this counter," she said. "Any day in the year, nobody charges you, it is your right. A copy of a rate may not be produced, because a copy of a rate is a return, and a return is this office's own document, and I have issued about nine thousand of them and not one has gone to a man who was not a party.

"You are asking me for a copy of a rate in a shed with a queue in front of it. Then I could not issue it out of this counter if there were a form for it. If I wrote it on this office's paper it would be a return, examinable by a season examiner, and it would go onto a roll in this building and be findable by people you would not care for. That is what the paper is. I am not refusing to be unkind about it."

She put her hand on the book in front of her and did not open it, and gave him the reason for the eleven offers in one sentence: total, two dates, receiving party. It is not a name. A person in front of a total learns nothing about which household they are in. A person in front of a name learns everything and can do nothing with it.

Marek thanked her and meant it, and she said *do not*, and he went down the lane to the credit window, which is the only other office in Rainmarket that will see anybody.

---

The Trust's credit window was shut for the day by the time he got to it, and Rustam Vey came out onto the step in his coat and stood with the door behind him open, which is a thing a man does when he intends to be quoted.

"Water office first."

"You could not have known that."

"I did not know it. I would have bet the wrong way. She is the best counter in this city and she will refuse you in a sentence you can put in a book." He came down one step. "And now I am going to get to the thing first, because otherwise we will spend an hour, and because I have thought about a clause for nineteen years and have never once been asked about it.

"Fourth box. You want a rule that says a counterweight is named to the people who will bear it. I have certified that clause about four hundred times. Do you know what a certification of it is?"

"That all the parts are present and the parties went before a witness and the thing may stand."

"That is what a certification is. Nobody in nineteen years has asked me whether the district named in the fourth box is the right district, or to whom that district answers, because that is not what I certify, and the office has been printing that clause in type since before I came and has never asked the man who uses it which district to put in it.

"And now you want to stand a room up beside it. In nine months the same clause will be in two places. The office administers it twice. The first time the room and the form disagree about a figure, one of the two is wrong, and it will not be you, Mr Vale, because you have no office and nothing to lose, and I have nineteen years."

He put his hands in his coat.

"That is my answer, and it is correct, and I am not going to improve on it. I am also not going to be a hero about it. I closed the window on the sixth of the seed month and four households lost a fortnight of their seed, and I have said so in a lane, and I will say it again."

---

The Benevolent Fund of the Counter-Clerks of the Chamber has one room, over a chandler's shop, eleven feet by nine, two walls of it shelf, and Zorka Vendl was in it on the ninth day sorting forty-one years of marks into cases with a hand going slower than it had been going in the rains.

She let the room be a room for about four seconds, which is what a secretary of a mutual does before she finds out what a visit is, and then said, "Sit if you like. It is nineteen years and I have never asked anybody to sit in here and I am not going to start."

She listened to the whole of it with her hands flat on a case, and interrupted once.

"The Fund's form has a box for *injury sustained in the course of duty*. It has four boxes and a fifth at the foot which is the oldest box on it, and in nineteen years I have never seen anybody fill the fifth one and I have never asked why.

"What you have described is a list of things that have not happened yet to anybody. If it were a wound I would want it, because a wound is a person I could name, and I have forty-one in a book, and I have paid for the clinic and the cot and the medicine and the hand-book, and I have never once paid for the years. And I have been asked twice in the last two months to pay for a sitting, by a Crown March representative, in person, in this room, and I declined twice, and I am still on that table."

"Then I have my answer and I am sorry for the time."

"You have my answer, which is no." She turned a case round on the shelf. "And I am going to give you the other thing for nothing, because you will not be able to get it in a room and I have no reason to keep it.

"You are going to need a person to read it out. Not a clerk, because a clerk has a wage, and a wage is a rate on a hall, and a man from a hall is a man with a form. A reader. Somebody who stands behind a thing and says it in words, four hours a day, to people who cannot read, and is not paid out of an office that can be closed.

"And there is no box for him anywhere. Not in the Fund, not in the Trust, not in that hall. He has not been injured. He is not a party. He is a man who reads, and the only two kinds of man in this basin who can be paid for reading a thing to other people are a clerk with a wage and a factor with a margin. You know the second one. He is not on your side, and he is the only one of the two nobody has to ask."

---

Bell Stair on the tenth, a corridor, and Sedge, a third-grade clerk who had been on that hill long enough that twice in it, and who did not stop walking when Marek came round the corner, and then did.

"Give it here," he said, and read it standing up, twice, and gave it back, and did not say no.

"I want to say the no first, because it is the shortest thing anybody has said to you in six weeks and it is the true one. The Chamber enters what it is handed. There is no form on which a bundle is entered in parts, and there cannot be one, because a bundle is not an instrument. It is a piece of paper with a rate on it and fifty-two other pieces of paper inside it, and the fifty-two other pieces *are* instruments. Bring them to my desk and I will enter fifty-two instruments. That is not a register. That is a file with a different cover on it, and it will be behind two doors in the copy room."

"That is a no," said Marek.

"That is a no and it is correct and I have expected it since the sixth. Now I am going to tell you the thing that will cost me more than the no costs you, and I have thought about it for two days, and I am not sure of it, and I am saying it anyway because you will not be given another chance to hear it.

"The third box will take them."

Sedge stopped in the corridor.

"Not the bundle. The parts. Fifty-two lines, one per part, in the oldest working box the Chamber has, headed *schedule*. And a schedule does not have to be anything anybody agreed to. It only has to be administered."

Marek Vale heard the yard's own sentence come back at him down the hill, in the voice of a clerk who had never been to a yard.

"No," he said.

"It is yours. Take it to the tank."

"That is the sentence your yard said on the sixteenth of the seed month, and a boy of nineteen said it before you, and the yard spent four hours refusing to be a schedule, because a schedule is a thing the office sends round and a thing the office can send round again without asking anybody. A register of parts *is* a schedule. I have spent six weeks telling anybody who would listen that the whole failure of this year is a cost in a box with no person in it, and the thing you have just offered me is the same box with a different word on the lid."

"You are turning down a form that would work."

"I am turning down a form that would work, in a corridor, because the yard said the sentence first, and if I use it in a building then in four years the only argument anybody has against a schedule is a thing a man in a hall said in a corridor. The yard did not write it. The yard is not in it. I would be spending the yard's best sentence like money."

Sedge looked at him with an expression that was not unkind and was not anything else either.

"Then I have not got a form for it," he said. "And I have never been asked for one by anybody. Where does it go, Mr Vale?"

"That is the fourth," said Marek Vale, "and it is the first one in six weeks that has cost anybody anything, and I do not know what to do with it."

---

There was a sixth, and it came on paper, in a Crown March hand, because Bel Harrow served everything in his own hand and had no clerk and could not get one.

*A board may not maintain a register of risk. This board has no fund, no date, and one seat standing empty, and I have asked twice for a person and been refused twice, the second refusal using the words in the ordinary course, which I have minuted in quotation marks. — B. H.*

He read it twice and put it in his coat, because a thing with a date on it and somebody's name at the foot of it is worth carrying even when it says no.

Then he went down the causeway and up to Tallow Ward, and it was the fourth of the afternoon and the yard was empty and the tank was under a shutter somebody had put over it against the weather, and the nail was inside the Gate shed where anybody could still get at it.

He stood in front of it a while in the cold with the flat board under his arm that he had put down on a tank in the morning like a man putting down a drink.

Five offices and a yard in six days, six refusals, and every one of them correct, and not one of them about whether it was a good idea. The hall entered what it was handed. The Trust had been administering the same clause in type for nineteen years and would now be administering it beside a room. The water office could not issue a copy of a rate to a man with no office even if there were a form for it. The Fund had no box for a thing that had not happened to anybody yet. A board could call a meeting and could not pay for one. And a yard had said no in nine seconds for a reason nobody had thought of, which was that the yard's own way of saying no was the only piece of paper in the basin that could not be traced, and handing it to a room would spend it.

He worked it out standing in the yard, and it took him about as long as it takes a man to decide he is cold.

It could not be a room in a building. There was no building in this basin that would hold it, and the two bodies that could agree to pay for it were a cooperative that had counted hands in a shed and a ward that could not be assessed a rate at all. So it could not be a room anybody paid for. It could only be a room that people came to, and that is not free, and the person who stood behind it and read it out would have to be paid out of a levy, and a levy is a thing two bodies agree to, and these two had not agreed to anything this year.

Marek put the shutter off the tank, which took him longer than it should have, and started to walk. The ninth month was short of a month away, and the whole of what he had learned in six days was the shape of the thing he had to build: that it had to be paid for by two bodies who could not be made to agree, out of a sum neither of them had, by a person neither of them could reach. And the only person in this basin who had already done it correctly was a yard that had just refused him in nine seconds, and would know he had been refused, and would not have mentioned it in three days, and would never once mention it.

He got as far as the causeway before he understood that he was going to have to go back up there in the morning and ask anyway.
